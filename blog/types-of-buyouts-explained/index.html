Types of Buyouts: LBO, MBO, ESOP and the IBO

By Michael Chasen · Updated October 9, 2026

A buyout is the purchase of a controlling interest in a company, usually funded with a mix of debt raised against the business and equity put up by the buyer. Every type below does that same thing. What separates them is three variables: who the buyer is, where the cash at close comes from, and who runs the company the morning after. Owners get quoted structures by name - LBO, MBO, recap, ESOP - as though the name were the deal. The name tells you almost nothing about your proceeds; the three variables tell you everything. Here are the seven a $3M+ EBITDA owner actually gets offered.

Structure Who buys Where the cash at close comes from Who controls the company after
Leveraged buyout (LBO) A financial buyer, usually a private equity fund Bank debt on your company plus the fund's equity The fund, through the board
Private equity buyout (majority) A PE fund buying 70-100% Same as an LBO; often with a required rollover The fund
Management buyout (MBO) Your existing leadership team Bank debt, a seller note, and usually an outside equity partner Management - unless the equity partner holds the votes
Management buy-in (MBI) An outside executive or team Bank debt plus the incoming team's backer The incoming team and its backer
Secondary buyout One PE fund buying from another New debt plus the new fund's equity The new fund
ESOP buyout A trust holding shares for employees Bank debt plus a seller note to the trust The trustee, voting on behalf of the plan
Independent Buyout (IBO) A trust structure backing the existing team Third-party debt plus seller paper - no sponsor equity The existing leadership, with no fund on the board

Leveraged buyout: the template the others are variations on

In an LBO the buyer borrows against the cash flow of the company being bought, not against its own balance sheet. That one mechanic is what makes a $40 million purchase possible for a buyer writing a $20 million check, and it is why the debt lands on your company rather than the acquirer's. The full mechanics are in how private equity actually finances a buyout.

The leverage is not unlimited, and the market numbers are public. In the lower middle market, deals in the $25M-$50M enterprise value band priced at about 6.8x EBITDA through the first nine months of 2025 and carried roughly 4.0x total debt and 3.4x senior debt; the $10M-$25M band priced at 6.4x (Capital Pad, citing GF Data). Those two numbers - the multiple and the leverage - set the boundaries of every structure on this page. If a buyer tells you a structure pays more than the company's debt capacity and its multiple allow, the extra is coming from you, in paper.

Private equity buyout: what people mean by "a buyout offer"

When an owner says they got a buyout offer, this is almost always the structure: a fund buys 70-100% of the equity, puts debt on the business, and takes board control. It is an LBO with a specific kind of buyer, and it is the dominant form of buyout by dollar value. Global buyout deal value reached about $904 billion in 2025, up 44%, with thirteen individual deals above $10 billion - the largest being the roughly $57 billion take-private of Electronic Arts (Bain & Company, Global Private Equity Report 2026).

Three features matter more to a seller than the headline multiple. The rollover: funds routinely require 10-30% of your proceeds to stay in the new entity, money you are credited with but cannot spend - see rollover equity and the second bite. Governance: even a minority position comes with a consent list longer than most owners expect, and a majority position comes with the whole board. And the clock - the fund owes its own investors a return, which sets what happens to your company in years one through seven. The honest case for and against is in selling to private equity: pros and cons.

Management buyout: your team buys the company

An MBO transfers ownership to the people already running the business - the structure owners reach for when they want continuity and no family successor exists. The constraint is always the same: capable operators rarely have the equity. A team with a few hundred thousand dollars of savings cannot fund an eight-figure purchase, so the gap gets filled with bank debt, a seller note, and - in most deals - an outside equity partner who ends up with the votes. Management buyout financing walks the capital stack; management buyout vs private equity sets the two side by side on price and control.

The question to ask of any MBO proposal is not whether management is buying it. It is whose money is buying it, and what that money requires in return.

Management buy-in and the buy-in management buyout

An MBI is the mirror image: an outside executive or team buys the company and installs itself as management. A BIMBO combines the two. For a seller the practical difference from an MBO is risk transfer - an MBI buyer has no operating history with your customers, lenders or staff, which matters when a large part of your consideration is a seller note the business has to earn.

Secondary buyout: one fund sells to the next

A secondary buyout, or sponsor-to-sponsor deal, is one private equity fund buying a company from another. Most owners meet it not as sellers but as the likely fate of their own company after a PE sale, and the backlog makes it hard to avoid: roughly 32,000 unsold private-equity-held companies worth about $3.8 trillion sat in portfolios at the end of 2025 (Bain & Company). When your buyer's fund reaches the end of its life, someone has to take the company off its hands - increasingly another fund, with its own debt package and its own hold period.

ESOP buyout: the employees buy it through a trust

In an ESOP, a trust buys shares on behalf of employees, typically funded by bank debt plus a note from the selling owner. It is a real market, not a curiosity: the most recent count identifies 6,609 ESOPs in the United States with about 15.1 million participants and more than $2 trillion in plan assets (NCEO, Employee Ownership by the Numbers, 2023 data).

The feature that drives most ESOP conversations is tax. Under 26 U.S. Code § 1042, a seller of qualified securities to an ESOP can defer gain by buying qualified replacement property, provided the plan owns at least 30% of the company immediately after the sale, the seller held the shares for at least three years, and the replacement property is purchased within a window that opens three months before the sale and closes twelve months after it. The offsetting constraints are equally real: the price is an independent appraiser's fair market value rather than a negotiated strategic price, a trustee votes the shares, and the company owes departing employees a repurchase for as long as the plan exists. Confirm all of it with your own tax advisor.

Independent Buyout: leverage without the sponsor

An Independent Buyout uses a trust-based financing structure - similar in mechanics to a leveraged buyout - to buy some or all of an owner's shares, without an outside financial sponsor ending up on the board. The team that already runs the company keeps running it. Because there is no fund equity in the stack, there is no fund timetable, no consent list, and no second sale forced by a fund's life. Depending on the seller's specific facts it may also be structured for tax-advantaged treatment under the same § 1042 framework described above - again, a question for a tax advisor, not an assumption. The numbers comparison against a sponsor deal is the useful read if you want the after-tax arithmetic rather than the description.

What the structures actually pay: a worked example

The company below is invented, and so are its figures - this is arithmetic, not a quote and not a description of any transaction. Assume $6 million of normalized EBITDA and $2 million of existing funded debt. At the 6.8x the $25M-$50M band carried in 2025, enterprise value is about $40.8 million, so roughly $38.8 million of equity value. Senior capacity at 3.4x is about $20.4 million for the whole company, of which $2 million is already drawn.

Structure Cash to the owner at close What the rest becomes
PE majority buyout, 20% rollover ~$31M before fees, escrow and tax $7.8M of rollover equity in the new entity, realised only on the fund's next sale
Management buyout, no outside equity ~$18M (new senior debt, net of the existing $2M and fees) ~$21M seller note, subordinated to the bank, amortising over 7-10 years
Minority recapitalization, 30% sold ~$10M, after a one-turn non-control discount You keep 70% and the company keeps running; a new shareholder joins the cap table
ESOP or Independent Buyout Bounded by the same ~$18M of debt capacity Seller paper for the balance, with a different tax profile and no sponsor on the board

Two patterns show up every time you run this. First, only the PE structure converts most of the equity to cash at close, and it does that by handing control to the fund and parking a slice of your money in rollover. Second, every internal structure - MBO, ESOP, IBO - is capped by the same debt capacity, because they are all borrowing against the same cash flow. The differences between them are tax treatment, governance and who holds the paper, not headline price. Run your own EBITDA through the valuation calculator and a defensible normalized EBITDA figure before you compare any two offers.

How to choose between them

  • If you want to be done, and maximum cash at close is the only thing that matters, a majority PE buyout or a strategic sale pays the most - and costs you control immediately.
  • If you want continuity for the team and the brand, the internal structures are the ones to price: MBO, ESOP, or an Independent Buyout. Expect more of the consideration in paper.
  • If you want cash now but are not finished building, a minority recapitalization or another route to partial liquidity while keeping control is the lane.
  • If the driver is a family transition, the buyout question sits inside a longer one - see family business succession planning.
  • If you have not yet decided that a buyout is the right move at all, start with the alternatives to selling to private equity.

Frequently asked questions

What is a private equity buyout? A private equity fund buys a controlling stake in a company, funds most of the purchase with debt secured against that company, and takes board control. The fund typically holds for three to seven years and then sells, often to another fund.

What is the difference between an LBO and an MBO? An LBO describes how the deal is financed - with debt against the target. An MBO describes who the buyer is - the existing management team. Most MBOs are financed as leveraged buyouts, so the two are not alternatives; they answer different questions.

What is a buyout company? Usually a private equity firm whose strategy is acquiring control of mature, cash-generative businesses, as distinct from a venture firm that takes minority stakes in early-stage companies. "Buyout fund" is the more precise term.

Which buyout type pays the seller the most? Measured as cash at close, a majority sale to a financial or strategic buyer. Measured after tax, after rollover, and after what the structure does to the company you may still partly own, the answer changes often enough that it is worth pricing at least two structures before choosing.

Can a buyout happen without private equity? Yes. An ESOP, a seller-financed management buyout, and an Independent Buyout all use third-party debt and seller paper rather than fund equity, which is why none of them puts a sponsor on the board.

The bottom line

There are not seven different prices for your company. There is one company, one cash flow, and roughly one debt capacity - and seven ways of dividing the result between cash, paper, equity you keep, and control you give up. Pick the structure by what you want the day after close, then negotiate the price inside it.
