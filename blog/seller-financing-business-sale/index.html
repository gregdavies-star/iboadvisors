Seller Financing in a Business Sale: Terms

By Michael Chasen · Updated October 17, 2026

The short answer

Seller financing in a business sale means you lend the buyer part of your own purchase price. You sign the deal, hand over the company, and take back a promissory note — the seller note — for a slice of what you are owed. It carries a stated interest rate and a maturity date, and in almost every leveraged deal it sits behind the bank.

In the lower middle market that slice is usually around a tenth of the price. A summary of the Alliance of Merger & Acquisition Advisors' lower-middle-market survey describes the average $25M–$50M enterprise-value deal as roughly 70% cash at close, 10% seller note, 15% rollover equity and 5% escrow, with an earnout in about a third of deals (Lippes Mathias).

So the headline number on your letter of intent is not the number. Seven-tenths of it is money. The rest is a set of promises with different odds attached, and the seller note is the one owners misprice most, because it looks like debt and behaves like equity.

Where a seller note actually sits

A buyout is paid for in layers, and every layer above you gets paid first. How private equity finances a buyout walks the full stack; for seller-financing purposes there are four rungs that matter:

  • Senior debt. A bank or credit fund, secured by a first lien on everything the company owns. Paid first, always.
  • Mezzanine or unitranche. Secured behind the senior lender, priced accordingly.
  • The seller note. Usually unsecured, always subordinated, and the last piece of debt in the line.
  • Equity. The sponsor's cheque and any rollover equity you keep.

Being above the equity sounds like protection. It is worth less than it reads: the equity holders run the company and decide what it spends, while the terms of your paper are written by the senior lender rather than by you.

Subordination is the clause that decides whether you get paid

You do not have to take anyone's word for how hard a subordination clause bites, because these agreements get filed. A subordinated promissory note filed with the SEC as an exhibit to an 8-K shows the standard shape: a $6,000,000 note dated 31 August 2005, issued as part of the purchase price under a stock purchase agreement, 6% per annum paid semi-annually, maturing in five years (SEC EDGAR). That is a seller note in everything but name, and its subordination section is the part to read.

Three mechanisms do the work, and they appear in some form in nearly every seller note behind a bank:

  • The blockage notice. The senior lender can send one when the company defaults on the senior debt. From that moment the holder of the note may not "ask, demand, accelerate, declare a default under, sue for, set off, accept or receive any payment" without the senior lender's written consent.
  • The standstill period. In that filing it runs 180 days from the blockage notice, ending earlier only if the senior debt is repaid in full or the default that triggered it is cured. Any insolvency proceeding is its own standstill, with no clock on it at all.
  • No cap on notices. That document sets no limit on how many blockage notices the senior lender may give. Some agreements cap the total blocked days in a rolling year; that one does not, and a seller who does not ask for the cap does not get it.

Read together, those terms mean the senior lender decides when your note gets paid, and a company that breaches a leverage covenant — not a company that has failed, merely one that has had a soft year — is enough to stop your payments for half a year at a time.

A worked example: what the last tenth is worth

The company below is invented. The figures are arithmetic on a hypothetical, not a quote, a projection, or a description of any transaction.

An owner has $6 million of normalized EBITDA. GF Data reported that average purchase-price multiples across 297 completed transactions in 2025 held steady at 7.2x trailing twelve-month adjusted EBITDA (ACG / GF Data), so call the enterprise value $43.2 million. Apply the survey's average structure:

Component Share Amount When you see it
Cash at close 70% $30.24M Closing day
Rollover equity 15% $6.48M On the sponsor's next sale, if there is one
Seller note 10% $4.32M Interest as permitted; principal at maturity
Escrow 5% $2.16M 12–18 months, less any claims
Headline price 100% $43.20M

Now price the note. Take the same terms as the filed note above: $4.32 million, 6% interest only, principal due in five years. That is $259,200 of interest a year and a $4.32 million balloon. The question is what that stream is worth on the day you sign, and the answer depends entirely on the rate a rational lender would charge for unsecured, deeply subordinated paper against a company carrying fresh acquisition debt.

Discount rate applied Present value of the note Cents on the dollar
6% (the coupon — i.e. the note is riskless) $4.32M 100
10% $3.66M 85
14% $3.13M 73
Blocked and never cured $0 0

At 14% — not a punitive rate for junior unsecured paper — the $4.32 million is worth about $3.13 million, so roughly $1.19 million of the headline price evaporates at signing. The 7.2x offer is a 7.0x offer. That is not an argument against ever taking a note; it is an argument for pricing one. Run your own EBITDA through the business valuation calculator, then discount every deferred component before you compare two offers, because the offer with the bigger headline is often the one with the thinner cash.

The same discipline applies to the other deferred pieces. An earnout in a business sale depends on performance the buyer controls; a seller note depends only on the company staying solvent and the senior lender staying quiet, which is a better bet than an earnout and a worse one than cash.

Why buyers want one, and what that tells you

A seller note is cheap capital: it prices below mezzanine, needs no new lender, and closes with the rest of the documents. But the reason buyers push hardest for one has nothing to do with price. Lenders count a subordinated seller note as quasi-equity when they size the senior facility, so every dollar you lend back is a dollar of sponsor equity that does not have to be written. It doubles as a retention device, keeping the person who knows the business financially interested in it long after they have stopped controlling it. Management buyout financing leans on this hardest, and the types of buyouts compares where the note sits in each structure.

The useful inference: if a buyer needs your note to close, the buyer is short of capital. Ask what else in the structure is being financed by you rather than by them before you concede anything else.

The tax the installment method adds

A sale paid partly after the year of closing is an installment sale, and it is reported on the installment method by default unless you elect out. Each payment splits into three parts: interest income taxed as ordinary income, a tax-free return of your basis, and gain (IRS Publication 537). Three consequences are worth pricing before you sign.

Deferral is usually the point. Gain is recognized as the principal arrives rather than all at closing, which is why most sellers do not elect out.

Understated interest gets imputed. The contract must provide "adequate stated interest" — broadly, a rate at least equal to the applicable test rate, which for seller-financed sales cannot exceed 9% compounded semiannually. Where it does not, the IRS recharacterizes part of the principal as interest and taxes it to you as ordinary income whatever the document calls it. A zero-coupon or token-rate seller note does not convert interest into capital gain; it just moves where the interest is labelled.

Large notes carry an interest charge. Under section 453A, if the total balance of nondealer installment obligations that arose during the year and are still outstanding at year end exceeds $5 million, you owe interest to the IRS on the deferred tax attributable to the excess. It applies to sales with a price over $150,000. A single $4.32 million note sits under the line; the same note alongside a second note, an earnout paid over time, or a second sale in the same year does not.

None of that is advice on your own return. It is the list of questions to put to your tax advisor while the structure is still negotiable — before the letter of intent is signed, not after.

What to negotiate, in order

  1. Less note, more cash. On the discount rates above, a dollar at closing beats a dollar of subordinated paper by fifteen to thirty cents. Trade the note down before you trade anything else, and make the buyer fund the difference.
  2. A cap on blockage. Limit the total number of days payments can be blocked in any rolling twelve months, and limit how many notices the senior lender may send. Without a cap, consecutive notices can block you indefinitely.
  3. Payments permitted while the senior loan performs. Scheduled interest and principal should keep flowing unless the senior debt is in actual default — not on a technical or reporting breach, and not at the lender's discretion.
  4. Security, if you can get it. A second lien behind the senior lender, or a pledge of the buyer's equity, converts your note from a promise into a claim. Many lenders refuse; ask anyway, and price the refusal.
  5. Acceleration on the events that matter. Payable in full on a sale of the business, a refinancing that repays the senior debt, or a change of control. Otherwise the buyer can sell the company and leave your note behind.
  6. Real interest, paid in cash. A rate that clears the test rate and is paid rather than accrued, so the note does not quietly become a zero-coupon instrument.
  7. Financial reporting. Quarterly statements and covenant compliance certificates. A creditor who cannot see the borrower's numbers finds out about trouble when the payment stops.

Frequently asked questions

What is seller financing in a business sale? It is the portion of the purchase price the seller lends back to the buyer, documented as a promissory note with a stated rate and maturity. The buyer pays it from the company's cash flow after closing, behind whatever the company owes its bank.

How much of the price is typically seller financed? Around 10% in the lower middle market. The AM&AA survey summary puts the average $25M–$50M enterprise-value deal at 70% cash, 10% seller note, 15% rollover and 5% escrow. Smaller deals carry proportionally more, because smaller buyers raise less.

What happens if the buyer defaults? Subordination comes first: you usually cannot accelerate or sue until the standstill period runs out. After that, the installment-sale repossession rules govern what happens if you do take the business back, and they turn on whether the repossession protects your security rights in the property (IRS Publication 537). Taking back a company that has just failed under someone else's management is a remedy in name more than in fact.

Is a seller note better than an earnout? Usually, yes. A note is a fixed obligation that depends on solvency; an earnout is a contingent one that depends on decisions the buyer makes. The pros and cons of selling to private equity sets both against the cash alternative.

The bottom line

Seller financing is not a worse price. It is the same price with part of it moved behind a bank, lent at a rate below the risk, and made conditional on a company you no longer run. Price it that way — discount it, cap the blockage, and ask what the cash-only number would have been — and it becomes a term you can trade rather than a line you accept.

The reason a seller note exists at all is that the buyer cannot fund your view of the value today. An Independent Buyout attacks that directly: your own management team becomes the buyer, financed against the company's own cash flow at a valuation set the way a sponsor would set it, so more of the consideration is cash at closing rather than paper you hold afterwards.
