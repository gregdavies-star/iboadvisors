Seller Financing in a Business Sale

By Michael Chasen · Updated October 16, 2026

The short answer

Seller financing in a business sale means you did not sell the whole company for cash. You sold part of it and lent the buyer the rest. The financed piece is a promissory note from a company you no longer control, almost always subordinated to a bank that gets paid first, and secured — where it is secured at all — by assets the bank already holds a first lien on.

That is not an argument against ever taking one. A seller note is the only deferred piece of a purchase price that carries a stated rate and a maturity date, which makes it a better instrument than an earnout, whose amount depends on decisions the buyer makes after closing, and different in kind from rollover equity, which converts only when the next owner sells. It is an argument for pricing the note as a credit instrument rather than counting it in the headline number, because a credit instrument is what it is.

Where the note sits in the capital stack

A buyer funds an acquisition in layers that get repaid in order: senior debt first, with a first lien on everything and the tightest covenants; any mezzanine piece beneath it; the seller note beneath that; sponsor equity, and whatever equity you rolled, at the bottom.

What that ordering buys the buyer is cheap capital. Your note is the layer a bank will not lend against and a mezzanine fund would price in double digits with fees and warrants attached, and you are typically asked to provide it at a single-digit rate with no fees, no warrants and no board seat. How a private equity buyout is financed walks the same stack from the buyer's side, and management buyout financing shows why the seller note is usually the largest single layer when the buyer is your own management team rather than a fund.

Two features of that position matter more than the interest rate, and both live in documents that get drafted late:

  • Payment blockage. The senior lender can stop the company paying you — usually on any default, sometimes on a covenant breach alone, for a standstill period that renews. Your note keeps accruing while the cash stays inside the business.
  • Standstill on remedies. Even in default, you agree not to accelerate, sue, foreclose or file against the company for a defined period. A note you cannot enforce during that period is a note the senior lender has that long to restructure around.

What the note is actually worth: a worked example

The company below is invented. These are arithmetic on a hypothetical, not a quote, a projection, or a description of any transaction.

A business does $4 million of normalized EBITDA. The buyer offers 6.5x, or $26 million, and funds it with $12 million of senior debt (3.0x), $9 million of its own equity, and a $5 million seller note — 19% of the price. The note runs five years, pays 8% interest only with the principal due at maturity, and is fully subordinated. Cash to you at closing is $21 million.

Now price the note three ways instead of one.

Outcome Principal repaid Interest received Total consideration Effective multiple
Pays in full, on schedule $5.0M $2.0M $28.0M 7.0x
Blocked in year three, settled at 50 cents $2.5M $0.8M $24.3M 6.1x
Senior default, note written off $0 $0.8M $21.8M 5.5x

Read the top row first, because it is the one nobody points out: a seller note that performs pays you more than the headline price. The coupon is real money, and 8% on $5 million for five years is $2 million the all-cash buyer at 6.5x was never going to hand you. This is the honest case for taking one.

Then read the bottom row. The gap between the top and the bottom is $6.2 million — more than the face value of the note, because the interest goes with it. On this hypothetical the whole range sits between 5.5x and 7.0x against a headline of 6.5x, and nothing you do after closing moves your position within it. Run your own EBITDA through the business valuation calculator before you agree to finance any part of a price, and compare the result with what the after-tax ledger on a private equity sale leaves you.

The subordination agreement decides everything else

Sellers negotiate the note and sign the subordination agreement. It is the wrong way round. The note states a rate and a maturity; the subordination agreement states whether either one means anything.

It also survives the event you are worried about. Under 11 U.S.C. § 510(a), "a subordination agreement is enforceable in a case under this title to the same extent that such agreement is enforceable under applicable nonbankruptcy law." The priority you agreed to at closing is the priority you have in a Chapter 11, and a bankruptcy court does not revisit it because the terms turned out badly for you.

So read it before the note. How long is the blockage period, can it be reimposed, and does blocked interest keep accruing at a default rate? Can the senior facility be increased ahead of you without your consent? Each answer is a line in a document that is negotiable before closing and unavailable afterwards.

The tax bill can arrive before the money does

A seller note usually makes the transaction an installment sale — in the IRS's words, "a sale of property where you receive at least one payment after the tax year of the sale" (IRS Publication 537). Gain is then reported as payments arrive, which is the attraction. Three rules take some of that back.

Depreciation recapture is due at closing. Publication 537 is explicit: "you must report any depreciation recapture income in the year of sale," and "only the gain greater than the recapture income is reported on the installment method." An asset-heavy company with years of accelerated depreciation behind it can therefore generate a cash tax liability in year one on money that will not arrive until year five.

Above $5 million of deferred paper, the IRS charges you interest. Under the section 453A rules described in Publication 537, you owe interest on the deferred tax where the property "had a sales price over $150,000" and the total balance of all non-dealer installment obligations arising during, and outstanding at the close of, the tax year exceeds $5 million. The charge is figured by multiplying the deferred tax liability by an applicable percentage — the face amount outstanding at year end less $5 million, divided by that face amount — and then by the IRS underpayment rate for the month the tax year ends. For a calendar-year individual closing 2026, that rate is 7%, compounded daily (IR-2026-98, Revenue Ruling 2026-15).

On the hypothetical above, a $5.0 million note does not cross the line. Take the same deal with an $8 million note and $6 million of unrecognized gain at year end: the applicable percentage is ($8M − $5M) ÷ $8M = 37.5%, the deferred tax liability at a 20% maximum long-term capital gains rate is $1.2 million, and the charge is $1.2M × 37.5% × 7% = $31,500 for that year, repeated every year the note is outstanding. It is not ruinous. It is simply never in the model the buyer shows you, and it is a cliff rather than a slope, which makes the size of the note a tax decision as well as a credit one.

Unstated interest gets imputed. If the note does not provide adequate stated interest, sections 483 and 1274 impute it at the applicable federal rate and recharacterize part of what you thought was principal as ordinary interest income. A "no-interest" seller note offered as a sweetener is therefore not tax-free; it is a taxable one whose rate the IRS picks. Price any of this with your own tax advisor against your own basis before you agree to the structure, not after.

What to negotiate, in order

  1. Less note, more cash. Every term below is a way of improving a bad position. Shrinking the note removes the position. Trade rate, term or almost anything else for a higher number at closing first.
  2. A shorter blockage and a hard cap on standstills. One standstill, defined in days, non-renewable, with blocked interest accruing at a default rate. This single clause moves more value than the coupon does.
  3. A debt-incurrence limit. Cap the senior debt that can sit ahead of you, as a multiple of EBITDA, so the buyer cannot dilute your position by borrowing more after closing.
  4. Acceleration on a sale or a change of control. Without it, your note is an asset the next owner inherits, on the terms you agreed with this one.
  5. Security, with the lien terms written down. A second lien is worth what is left after the first is satisfied, which in a distressed business is often nothing — but a second lien plus a cap on senior debt is worth considerably more than an unsecured note.
  6. No set-off. Bar the buyer from netting indemnity claims against note payments unless the claim has been adjudicated. Otherwise every post-closing dispute becomes a reason to stop paying you.

All six belong in the letter of intent. Choosing an M&A advisory firm covers what to ask a prospective advisor, including whether the fee is calculated on the headline price or on money you actually receive — which decides how hard anyone at the table argues about the difference between the two.

Frequently asked questions

What is seller financing in a business sale? A portion of the purchase price the buyer does not pay at closing, documented as a promissory note from the buyer to you with a stated interest rate, a repayment schedule and a maturity date. It is a loan you make to fund your own sale.

How much of the price is usually a seller note? It varies with the buyer and the size of the deal, and the published ranges come mostly from brokers rather than audited data, so treat any single figure with suspicion. The number that matters is the one in front of you: take the proposed note, assume it pays nothing, and ask whether the cash at closing alone is a price you would accept.

Is a seller note safer than an earnout? Generally yes, and for a structural reason. An earnout pays only if a business someone else runs hits targets, so the buyer's own decisions move the amount. A note is a fixed obligation with a coupon that accrues whether the company performs or not. The risk is the buyer's credit and your position behind the bank, not the buyer's discretion.

Do I pay tax on a seller note before I am paid? Partly, often. Depreciation recapture is reported in full in the year of sale regardless of when cash arrives, and above $5 million of outstanding installment obligations the section 453A interest charge applies annually to the deferred tax. The installment method defers the rest of the gain until payments are received.

Can I sell without financing any of the price? Sometimes, by changing the buyer rather than the clause. A buyer who can fund the full value against the business itself does not need you to lend part of it back. The alternatives to selling to private equity sets out those routes, and the seven types of buyouts shows which structures lean hardest on seller paper.

The bottom line

Seller financing converts a price into a credit exposure. Strike the note out, look at the cash at closing, and ask whether that number alone is a deal you would sign. Then treat the coupon as the premium you are paid for the risk, and negotiate the subordination agreement as though the business will have a bad year — because over five years, many do.

The deeper question is why the buyer needs you to fund part of the purchase at all. An Independent Buyout makes your own leadership team the buyer and finances the valuation against the business itself, at a price set the way a sponsor would set it, so the value is paid at closing rather than lent back.
