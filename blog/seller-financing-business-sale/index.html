Seller Financing in a Business Sale: What a Note Pays

By Michael Chasen · Updated October 15, 2026

The short answer

Seller financing is the part of your price you lend back to your own buyer. You sign the deal, you hand over the company, and instead of money you hold a promissory note — a seller note — that the business is supposed to pay you out of future cash flow.

It is not a higher price. It is a loan you did not ask to make, to a borrower you no longer control, ranked behind the bank that funded the rest of the purchase. Priced honestly, a dollar of seller note is worth somewhere between 65 and 90 cents of closing cash, and the gap is the real concession in the deal.

Where the note sits, and why that is the whole story

A buyout is paid for in layers. Senior debt goes in first and gets paid first. Sponsor equity goes in last and gets paid last. The seller note sits between them, and the documents that define that middle position matter far more than the interest rate printed on the face of the note.

  • Subordination. A subordination agreement ranks your claim beneath the senior lender's. In a liquidation, the bank is made whole before a dollar reaches you.
  • Payment blockage. Separate from ranking, and more consequential day to day. The senior lender typically reserves the right to stop the company paying you — principal, interest, or both — for a defined period once a covenant is tripped. Your note does not default when payments stop; it simply accrues, and you have agreed in advance that it may.
  • Standstill. Even when you are genuinely in default, you usually cannot sue, accelerate, or foreclose until a standstill period has run. A 180-day standstill on a company in trouble is a long time to watch.
  • Full standby. The strictest version: no payments of any kind to you until the senior facility is retired. Common where the senior lender is lending to the edge of what the cash flow supports.

None of this is unreasonable from the bank's side — it is lending real money against the same cash flow. But it means the coupon you negotiated is a coupon the senior lender can switch off. The four layers of a typical management buyout show how the stack is assembled, and how private equity actually finances a buyout covers what the senior lender will and will not advance against your EBITDA.

What a seller note is actually worth: a worked example

The company below is invented and the numbers are arithmetic on a hypothetical, not a quote or a description of any transaction.

A business does $5,000,000 of adjusted EBITDA. The buyer offers 7.0x, or $35,000,000. The funding looks like this:

  • Senior debt, 3.5x EBITDA: $17,500,000
  • Buyer equity: $12,250,000
  • Seller note, 15% of price: $5,250,000 — 7% coupon, five-year bullet maturity, fully subordinated

Cash in your hand at closing: $29,750,000. The remaining $5,250,000 is a five-year, unsecured, subordinated loan to a leveraged company, paying $367,500 a year until a single balloon payment at maturity. The only honest way to value it is to discount it at a rate that reflects what it is, rather than taking it at face value because the term sheet does.

Discount rate applied to the note Present value of the note Total consideration Effective multiple
Face value (what the term sheet implies) $5.25M $35.00M 7.00x
10% — a healthy borrower, note paid on schedule $4.65M $34.40M 6.88x
14% — subordinated, unsecured, illiquid $3.99M $33.74M 6.75x
18% — blockage risk priced in $3.44M $33.19M 6.64x
Note never repaid $0 $29.75M 5.95x

The 7.0x offer is a 6.6x to 6.9x offer. That is not an argument against ever taking a note — it is an argument for negotiating the multiple on the cash at closing, and for pricing the note as the credit instrument it is before you agree to the headline number. Run your own EBITDA through the business valuation calculator to see what the closing-cash multiple alone would buy, and read how PE firms value a company for the adjustments a buyer will make to the EBITDA both figures rest on.

You cannot set the interest rate wherever you like

A seller note has to carry adequate stated interest or the IRS will impute it. The test is whether the stated principal exceeds the present value of everything the contract promises, discounted at a test rate drawn from the applicable federal rates — short-term for terms up to three years, mid-term for three to nine, long-term beyond that. The IRS publishes those rates as a revenue ruling every month (IRS, Applicable Federal Rates).

Where the contract falls short, part of what you thought was principal is recharacterized as interest — ordinary income to you, at a materially higher rate than the capital gain underneath it. There is a relief ceiling on the test rate: for seller-financed sales of $7,296,700 or less, the test rate "can't be more than 9%, compounded semiannually" (IRS Publication 537). A lower-middle-market company selling for $35,000,000 is nowhere near that ceiling, so the full rate applies.

The practical point is narrower than it sounds: a zero-coupon or token-coupon seller note does not convert into a bigger capital gain. It converts into a smaller one with an ordinary-income tail attached.

The note is also the buyer's second escrow

This is the part sellers find out late. Buyers routinely negotiate a right of set-off: the ability to reduce what they owe on the note by the amount of any indemnity claim or purchase-price adjustment they assert. Not a claim they have proved — a claim they have asserted.

Stack that against what is already being held back. In lower-middle-market deals, SRS Acquiom reports that virtually all transactions carry at least one escrow or holdback, the median general indemnity escrow runs 12.5% of transaction value against 10% across all deals, 92% of deals include a purchase price adjustment, and nearly half carry a separate escrow for that adjustment at a median of 1.23% (SRS Acquiom, Lower Middle Market M&A). Add a 15% seller note with an unrestricted set-off right on top and well over a quarter of your price is contingent on claims the buyer gets to raise.

The fix is drafting, not goodwill: no set-off unless the claim has been finally adjudicated or agreed, a cap on what can ever be offset, and a requirement that the escrow be exhausted first.

Seller note, earnout, rollover: three different animals

Seller note Earnout Rollover equity
What decides payment A fixed schedule, if cash flow and the senior lender allow it Performance targets the buyer now controls The price at the buyer's eventual exit
Priority Debt, below senior, above equity A contractual claim, usually unsecured Last in line
Return while you wait A coupon, when it is not blocked Nothing Nothing, unless there is a distribution
Typical tax character Installment gain plus ordinary interest Installment gain plus imputed interest Usually deferred at closing
Main risk Credit and subordination The buyer's post-close conduct Time, leverage and the preference stack

A note is the most predictable of the three and still the second-least liquid. An earnout is worth about 21 cents on the dollar across private-target deals; rollover equity converts only when the sponsor sells. If an offer contains all three, strike them all out and look at what is left: that is the number you are actually being paid.

The tax arrives on its own schedule

A seller note makes you an installment seller. Under the installment method you recognize gain as payments come in, in proportion to your gross profit percentage, and each payment splits into interest income, return of basis, and gain (IRS Publication 537). Spreading the gain is usually the point of agreeing to the note at all. Three things complicate it:

  • The deferral is not free. Once the sales price exceeds $150,000 and the face amount of your outstanding installment obligations exceeds $5,000,000, you owe the government annual interest on the tax you deferred (26 U.S. Code § 453A). The management buyout comparison works that charge through on a $10.5M note line by line.
  • You cannot quietly borrow against it. Pledge the note as security for a loan and the net proceeds are treated as a payment received on the note, accelerating the gain you were deferring. Publication 537 puts the pledge rule at sales prices over $150,000; the statute repeats it in § 453A(d).
  • The coupon is ordinary income. Interest on the note is taxed at ordinary rates, not the capital gains rate applied to the principal underneath it. On a 7% coupon, that difference is the larger part of what the coupon is supposed to compensate you for.

Electing out of the installment method is possible, and sometimes right — you pay the whole tax in the year of sale and hold a note with no § 453A drag. It is a decision to make with your own tax advisor before the term sheet is signed, because the election is due with that year's return and can be revoked only with IRS consent.

What to negotiate, in order

  1. Less note, more cash. On the arithmetic above, a dollar at closing is worth roughly $1.15 to $1.50 of five-year subordinated note. Trade length, coupon, anything, for a bigger number at closing first.
  2. The subordination terms, not the coupon. Cap payment blockage at a fixed number of days in any twelve-month period and limit how many times it can be invoked. An uncapped blockage right makes the maturity date advisory.
  3. Security. A second lien on the company's assets, or a pledge of the buyer's equity in the holding company, turns an unsecured promise into a claim with something behind it. Expect the senior lender to resist; ask anyway.
  4. No unilateral set-off. Only adjudicated or agreed claims, only after the escrow is exhausted, only up to a cap.
  5. Amortization over a bullet. Principal that comes back on a schedule shortens your exposure. A cash sweep — a share of excess cash flow applied to your note — does the same thing without a fixed obligation the lender will block.
  6. Information rights. Quarterly financials and covenant compliance certificates. You are a creditor now; creditors get to see the numbers.
  7. Transferability. The right to sell or pledge the note, subject to reasonable consent, gives you an exit that does not depend on the buyer's cash flow.

All seven belong in the letter of intent. By the time the subordination agreement is being negotiated between your lawyer and the senior lender, the leverage has moved.

Frequently asked questions

What is seller financing in a business sale? The seller lends the buyer part of the purchase price instead of being paid it at closing, documented as a promissory note. The buyer repays it from the company's cash flow over a defined term, almost always behind the senior lender that funded the rest of the deal.

How much of the price is usually a seller note? It varies with how much the senior lender will advance and how far apart the two sides are on value. The more useful question is not the percentage but the terms: a 10% note on full standby for five years is a bigger concession than a 20% note that amortizes and carries a second lien.

Is a seller note the same as an earnout? No. A seller note is debt with a fixed schedule; an earnout is a contingent payment that depends on performance the buyer now controls. A note can be blocked, but it cannot be missed by one percent. Earnouts in a business sale covers the difference in what each actually pays.

How is a seller note taxed? Principal is reported under the installment method, so gain is recognized as payments arrive. Interest is ordinary income. Above $5,000,000 of outstanding installment obligations, § 453A adds an annual interest charge on the deferred tax. Price all three with a tax advisor before agreeing to the structure.

Can I refuse to provide seller financing? Often, by changing the buyer rather than the clause. A note exists because the buyer's equity and the senior debt together do not reach your price. A different capital structure closes that gap without turning you into a lender — every exit option sets out the routes, and the types of buyouts shows which ones lean hardest on seller paper.

The bottom line

A seller note converts part of your price into a credit exposure to a company you no longer run, on terms the senior lender gets to police. Discount it, write the subordination and set-off terms yourself, and judge the offer on what the closing cash alone would mean as a multiple.

The deeper point is why the note is there at all. Seller paper fills the gap between what a buyer's equity and bank debt will fund and what the company is worth. An Independent Buyout closes that gap differently: the company's own borrowing capacity funds the purchase at a valuation set the way a sponsor would set it, with your own leadership team as the buyer, so the value is paid rather than lent back.
