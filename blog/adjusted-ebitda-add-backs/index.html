Adjusted EBITDA Add-Backs: What Buyers Accept

By Michael Chasen · Updated October 24, 2026

An adjusted EBITDA add-back survives diligence if the expense it removes will not happen again under the buyer’s ownership, and if you can prove it from the general ledger. Everything else is a negotiating position. One-time transaction fees, a settled lawsuit, the salary of a family member who is leaving, a discontinued product line — those hold. Anything the business needs in order to keep operating — commissions, recruiting, relocation, legal fees in the ordinary course, costs that recur at irregular intervals — gets struck, and it gets struck after the price has already been set on it.

That is the whole test, and it is worth understanding precisely, because the add-back schedule decides more money than the multiple negotiation that follows it. At a 6.0x multiple, every $100,000 of add-back a buyer accepts is $600,000 of enterprise value; every $100,000 they reject is $600,000 off a price you have already agreed.

What an add-back is, and what it is not

Reported EBITDA is what your accountant produced. Adjusted EBITDA is reported EBITDA plus the expenses a new owner would not incur, minus any income a new owner would not receive. The buyer applies the multiple to the adjusted number, not the reported one, which is why the schedule matters. How private equity values a company walks the full four-step sequence — EBITDA, normalization, multiple, comparables; this post is about step two in detail.

Two things an add-back is not. It is not a tax position: the expense stays deductible, and nothing here changes your return. And it is not a forecast. An add-back removes a cost that is in the historical numbers. A cost you expect to avoid next year is a projection, and buyers in the lower middle market do not pay multiples on projections.

The test buyers borrow from the SEC

Private M&A has no rulebook for add-backs. Public-company reporting does, and the quality of earnings providers who review your numbers have largely imported it, because the question is identical: when is an adjusted earnings figure a fair picture of the business, and when is it a flattering one?

Regulation G makes it unlawful to present a non-GAAP measure that is misleading, and the SEC staff has written down what that means for adjustments. Question 100.01 of the staff’s Non-GAAP Financial Measures Compliance and Disclosure Interpretations says that “presenting a non-GAAP performance measure that excludes normal, recurring, cash operating expenses” necessary to operate the business can violate the rule. The sentence that does the real work is the next one: the staff treats “an operating expense that occurs repeatedly or occasionally, including at irregular intervals, as recurring.”

Read that twice, because it is the opposite of how most owners use the word. “Non-recurring” in a seller’s schedule usually means it did not happen last year. In the staff’s framing — and in a buyer’s — it means it will not happen again. A legal dispute every three years is recurring. An equipment failure that lands at unpredictable intervals is recurring. The irregularity is not the point; the inevitability is.

Question 100.04 closes the other common door: adjustments that change how something is recognised or measured — pulling revenue forward, switching an expense from accrual to cash basis — are “individually tailored” and presumptively misleading, however the arithmetic is explained.

None of this binds a private seller. It is simply the clearest public statement of the standard your buyer’s diligence team is applying, and it is free to read.

Three add-backs the SEC staff has struck, on the record

Comment letters between the SEC staff and public filers are published on EDGAR, and they are the closest thing this market has to case law on add-backs. Three are worth knowing, because the same categories show up in lower-middle-market schedules every week.

  • Sales commissions. In a February 2021 response letter, Bill.com addressed staff comments on adjustments for sales commissions and service costs, which the staff read as normal, recurring cash operating expenses. The company agreed to “revise its calculation method to remove such adjustments from its calculation of non-GAAP metrics in its future periodic reports and earnings releases.” The read-across: cost of winning revenue is cost of revenue, whatever line it sits on.
  • Acquired in-process R&D. In February 2022, the staff questioned Cardiovascular Systems’ adjustment for an IPR&D charge from an asset acquisition, noting it “appears to be similar to research and development expenses” the company incurs in the ordinary course. Buying capability rather than building it does not make the spend extraordinary.
  • Recruiting, relocation and sign-on bonuses. In August 2024, the staff told BRC Inc. that its Adjusted EBITDA “includes several adjustments that appear to relate to normal operating expenses necessary to operate your business” — among them executive recruitment, relocation and sign-on bonuses, ordinary legal costs, and start-up costs on a new production line. The company agreed to stop adjusting for several of them.

The pattern across all three: the expense was genuinely unusual for that period, and it was struck anyway, because the activity that produced it is part of running the business.

Owner compensation: the one everyone sizes wrong

This is the largest add-back in most lower-middle-market schedules and the most commonly reduced. The mistake is almost never bad faith; it is arithmetic. Owners add back what they pay themselves. The defensible number is the excess over what the role would cost to fill at market.

If you take $600,000 and a professional president for the same company costs $280,000 fully loaded, the add-back is $320,000, not $600,000. If you are doing three jobs — chief executive, head of sales, and the person who signs every purchase order — the replacement cost is the cost of all three roles, and the add-back shrinks further while the business gets more honest about its owner dependence.

The tax code frames the same idea for a different purpose: 26 U.S. Code § 162(a)(1) allows a deduction for “a reasonable allowance for salaries or other compensation for personal services actually rendered.” Reasonable, measured against services actually rendered, is exactly the standard a buyer will hold you to. Support it with a compensation study or two or three real job postings for the equivalent role in your market, and it holds. Support it with your opinion, and it gets halved.

Family members on payroll follow the same rule, and they are easier: if a relative draws $120,000 for work the business genuinely does not need, the whole $120,000 comes back — provided the role actually ends at closing and the buyer can see that it does.

The gap is $851,000 of EBITDA. At 6.0x that is $5.1M of enterprise value, and the timing is what makes it expensive: the letter of intent was signed against $5.57M, so the correction arrives as a re-trade eight weeks in, when the seller has stopped running a process and has no competing bid left to use. What signing the LOI actually locks in covers why that moment is so one-sided.

Note the two lines nobody wants to discuss. The related-party rent is a negative adjustment the seller omitted entirely — common wherever an owner holds the real estate in an affiliate, and standard enough in some sectors to have its own arithmetic, as the restaurant valuation page sets out. And the second-facility start-up cost was struck not because it was fake, but because the buyer’s own model opens a third facility in year two.

The evidence standard

An add-back you cannot evidence is an add-back that disappears. The schedule a buyer’s quality of earnings provider will accept has four columns for every line: the amount, the general ledger accounts it ties to, the months it fell in, and the document that proves it will not recur. The last column is the one sellers skip and the one that decides the outcome.

  • Owner and family compensation — payroll records plus a market compensation study or comparable job postings.
  • Personal expenses — invoice-level detail. A round number for “personal travel” invites a round reduction.
  • Settled litigation — the settlement agreement and a release, showing the matter is closed.
  • Discontinued operations — a departmental P&L, plus evidence the leases and contracts are actually terminated.
  • Transaction costs — engagement letters and invoices dated inside the measurement period.

The practical move is to commission your own sell-side quality of earnings review a year or more before the data room opens, so the soft lines fail on your timetable rather than in a live negotiation. That is why a QoE sits early in the sequence in exit planning for business owners rather than inside the deal. It also lets you fix the underlying facts — put a written market-rent lease in place, take the relative off payroll, stop running the boat through the company — while there is still time for clean months to accumulate.

Why the multiple matters less than the schedule

Owners spend their energy on the multiple because it is the number everyone quotes. The schedule is the larger lever and the one you control. The ranges in our EBITDA multiples by industry reference move perhaps half a turn on anything you can change before a sale; a defensible add-back schedule routinely moves adjusted EBITDA by 10–20%, and it moves it against the whole multiple. You can test both effects on your own numbers with the business valuation calculator — run it once on reported EBITDA and once on the adjusted figure you can actually prove, and the spread between the two is what the schedule is worth.

The bottom line

Build the add-back schedule before a buyer builds it for you, size every line to what you can prove rather than what you would like, and apply the recurring test honestly: not “did this happen last year” but “will this happen again under a new owner”. A schedule that survives intact is worth more than a half-turn on the multiple, and it removes the single most common reason a signed letter of intent gets repriced.

The schedule matters regardless of who buys you, because every structure prices off the same adjusted number. An Independent Buyout is no exception: the financing is sized off the company’s own defensible cash flow, the equity moves to a trust owned by the employees, existing leadership keeps running the business, and there is no sponsor and no exit clock attached to the money — but the EBITDA it is all built on still has to hold up to diligence.
