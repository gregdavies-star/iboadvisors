Adjusted EBITDA Add-Backs: What Buyers Accept

By Michael Chasen · Updated October 23, 2026

An add-back is a line you add to reported profit to show what the business would earn under a new owner: the part of your salary that is really a distribution, the family member on payroll who does not work there, the lawsuit that settled once and will not recur. Buyers accept the add-backs that are owner-specific, documented in the general ledger, and genuinely finished. They strike the ones that are a forecast wearing an accountant’s clothes — run-rate savings from a plan not yet executed, a “one-time” cost appearing for the third consecutive year, a bad quarter normalized away.

What matters is not the dollar. It is the dollar times the multiple. At 6.5x, every dollar of add-back the buyer strikes in week six of diligence takes $6.50 of enterprise value off the price — and it takes it after you have already told your spouse what the company is worth.

The arithmetic nobody runs before the process starts

Take an invented company — a specialty contractor with $5,000,000 of reported EBITDA and no real-world counterpart — whose owner takes an add-back schedule of $1,400,000 into diligence. Here is how a competent buy-side accountant works down the list.

Proposed add-back Amount Outcome
Owner compensation above a benchmarked market salary $350,000 Accepted
Family members on payroll with no operating role $120,000 Accepted
Personal vehicles, club dues and travel charged to the company $85,000 Accepted
Litigation settled and released, one matter, closed in 2024 $240,000 Accepted
Rent paid to the owner’s property LLC above market $105,000 Accepted
Run-rate savings from a purchasing program starting next quarter $260,000 Struck
“One-time” ERP implementation costs, third consecutive year $140,000 Struck
Normalization of a weak quarter attributed to weather $100,000 Struck

Reported EBITDA of $5,000,000 becomes $6,400,000 on the seller’s schedule and $5,900,000 on the buyer’s. At 6.5x, that is an enterprise value of $41,600,000 against $38,350,000. The three struck lines cost $3,250,000, which is more than most owners spend on every advisor in the transaction combined. Run the same exercise on your own numbers with the business valuation calculator, and use the EBITDA multiples by industry table to put a defensible multiple on the result rather than the one you have in your head.

The five add-backs that usually survive

There is one test behind all of them: could a stranger reconstruct this number from the general ledger without talking to you? If the answer is yes, it is an adjustment. If it requires your explanation, it is an argument.

  • Owner compensation above market. The add-back is the gap between what you pay yourself and what it would cost to hire your replacement. It survives when it is benchmarked against a published compensation survey for the role, the industry and the region — and it shrinks fast when the owner is genuinely running the company day to day, because then the replacement is expensive.
  • Discretionary personal expenses. Vehicles, country club memberships, personal travel, a phone plan for the family. These survive when each one ties to a coded account and an invoice. They die when they are presented as a round estimate.
  • Related-party rent off market. This cuts both ways, and the second direction is the one sellers forget. If you pay your own property LLC above market, the excess adds back. If you pay below market — common where the real estate is held personally and the rent was set in 2009 — the buyer will adjust EBITDA down to a market rent, and that adjustment is not optional.
  • Closed, released, single-instance matters. A settled piece of litigation with a signed release. A flood. A regulatory penalty paid once. The word doing the work is “closed”: an ongoing dispute is a liability, not an add-back.
  • Professional fees tied to a discrete event. The failed acquisition, the restatement, the one-off systems conversion. Note the trap in the table above: the same ERP line in three consecutive years is a recurring cost of running the business, whatever the invoice says.

The ones that get struck, and why

Public companies that report adjusted EBITDA have to defend their adjustments to the SEC, and the staff’s Compliance & Disclosure Interpretations on non-GAAP financial measures are the clearest public statement anywhere of where the line sits. None of it binds a private sale. All of it is what the buyer’s accountants were trained on, which amounts to the same thing across a diligence table.

Four of the staff’s positions map directly onto the add-back schedules owners bring to market:

  • Recurring costs cannot be excluded (Question 100.01). Presenting a measure that “excludes normal, recurring, cash operating expenses” necessary to operate the business can be misleading — and the staff defines the term broadly: “The staff would view an operating expense that occurs repeatedly or occasionally, including at irregular intervals, as recurring.” Occasional is still recurring.
  • Periods have to be treated alike (Question 100.02). Adjusting a charge in the current period without adjusting a similar item in the prior period is a problem unless the change is disclosed and explained. On a sell-side schedule, this is the year where the add-backs suddenly triple.
  • Charges without gains (Question 100.03). Adjusting out the non-recurring losses while leaving the non-recurring gains in is the single easiest thing for a buy-side accountant to find, because they are looking for it.
  • Tailored accounting is not adjustment (Question 100.04). Changes to GAAP recognition or measurement — accelerating revenue recognized ratably, switching revenue from net to gross, moving from accrual to cash — are “considered individually tailored and may cause the presentation of a non-GAAP measure to be misleading.”

And one more worth taping to the wall, Question 100.06: a measure can still be materially misleading notwithstanding “even extensive, detailed disclosure about the nature and effect of each adjustment.” A long footnote does not rescue a bad add-back. It just tells the buyer where to look.

What a real credit agreement does with add-backs

If you want to see how sophisticated money actually treats this, read a credit agreement rather than an advisory brochure. The definition of “Consolidated EBITDA” in the third amended and restated credit agreement Gen Digital filed as an exhibit to its 2026 annual report runs to twelve separate “plus” clauses and four “minus” clauses. Two of them are worth an owner’s time.

Clause (xi) permits “run rate” cost savings, operating expense reductions, revenue enhancements and synergies — but only where they are “reasonably identifiable and factually supportable”, projected in good faith from actions already taken, committed to or substantially begun, within 24 months, calculated on a pro forma basis as if realized on the first day of the period. And then it caps them: standalone cost-saving add-backs “shall not exceed 30% of Consolidated EBITDA for such period (calculated after giving effect to any such add backs and all other add backs for such period)”.

Clause (xii) is the one that should change how you prepare. It permits adjustments “evidenced by or contained in a due diligence quality of earnings report” prepared by a “big four” firm or another acceptable accounting firm, and “consistent with Regulation S-X”. The agreement does not ask the borrower to argue for its add-backs. It names the document that counts as evidence.

Two lessons transfer straight down to a $5M-EBITDA business. Add-backs are not treated as illegitimate — they are a negotiated, capped, evidenced category. And the evidence standard is a quality of earnings report, not a spreadsheet from your controller. Lenders apply the same discipline when they size the debt in a dividend recapitalization or a buyout: the leverage multiple attaches to an EBITDA figure they have adjusted, not the one you proposed.

What the evidence says about aggressive schedules

Two public data points are worth more than any practitioner anecdote. The first is the price of preparation: GF Data analyzed 360 transactions completed since the third quarter of 2024 and found that sellers who commissioned a sell-side quality of earnings report saw TEV/EBITDA multiples of 7.4x on average, against 7.0x for those who did not — with the effect most notable on deals above $50 million of enterprise value, while smaller deals “tended not to experience a valuation boost”. In the same piece, Forvis Mazars’ Scott Linch puts sell-side QoE usage at “at least 90% of PE-backed deals” he sees, against “only about 50% of lower middle-market founder-led businesses”. A buyer who does this forty times a year is meeting a seller who is doing it once.

The second is what happens to add-backs after the deal closes. The Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis working paper “EBITDA Add-backs in Debt Contracting: A Step Too Far?” (Faria-e-Castro, Gopalan, Pal, Sánchez and Yerramilli, 2022) used the 2013 Interagency Guidance on Leveraged Lending as a shock and found that loans with more permissive add-backs are more likely to end in delinquency, default and borrower downgrades — and that lead arrangers are more likely to sell down their share of them. That is the syndicated loan market, not lower-middle-market M&A, so read it as direction rather than as your number. The direction is unambiguous: the market has learned what padded EBITDA is worth, and prices it accordingly.

Build the schedule before anyone asks for it

  • Start three years out, not three weeks. Adjustments documented contemporaneously survive; adjustments reconstructed from memory in a data room do not.
  • One line per adjustment, each tied to a GL account. Not categories. Not “personal expenses, approximately.”
  • Benchmark the compensation add-back externally. A published survey for your role, industry and region beats your assertion about what a replacement costs.
  • Adjust in both directions. Put the below-market rent, the under-funded maintenance capex and the deferred bonus pool on the schedule yourself. A seller who has already found the negatives is believed on the positives.
  • Get the sell-side quality of earnings report done early. Its real value is not the document: it is finding the $500,000 problem while you still have a year to fix it, rather than in week six of exclusivity when your only options are to concede or to blow up the deal. How PE firms value a company walks through where the QoE sits in the buyer’s sequence.
  • Do not pad. One indefensible line costs you credibility on the whole schedule, and the buyer re-prices every other adjustment at the same discount.

When a buyer will not credit an add-back but cannot disprove it either, the gap often gets papered over with contingent consideration rather than price — which is how a disputed adjustment becomes an earnout you have to work three more years to collect. Worth knowing before the conversation starts: how to sell a business to private equity sets out the rest of the sequence, and selling to private equity, pros and cons covers what the headline number is actually worth once the structure is applied to it.

Where the add-back schedule lands in an Independent Buyout

None of this is specific to a private equity sale. Adjusted EBITDA is the denominator of every structure on the menu: it sets the price in a full sale, the amount a lender will advance in a recapitalization, and the valuation in an Independent Buyout. What changes in an IBO is who is sitting opposite you when the schedule gets tested: the financing is sized off the company’s own cash flow, existing leadership stays in place, and there is no sponsor working a return model that improves every time an add-back comes out. The arithmetic is the same. The incentive across the table is not.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between EBITDA and adjusted EBITDA? EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, taken from the financial statements. Adjusted EBITDA is that figure after add-backs for costs a new owner would not carry and after adjustments for costs the current owner is not carrying but should be. Every price in the lower middle market is quoted off the adjusted number, which is exactly why it is contested.

Is there a limit to how many add-backs I can take? There is no rule in a private sale, but there is a practical ceiling. Credit agreements write it down: the one above caps standalone cost-saving add-backs at 30% of Consolidated EBITDA. A schedule that lifts reported EBITDA by more than about a quarter invites line-by-line scrutiny of all of it, including the lines that would have passed on their own.

Will a buyer accept my add-backs because my CPA signed off? A compilation or a review is not a quality of earnings report and does not test add-backs at all. Your CPA attests to the financial statements; the buy-side accountant is testing whether each adjustment is owner-specific, documented and non-recurring. Those are different jobs, and the second one is the one that moves the price.

Should a $3M-EBITDA company pay for a sell-side quality of earnings report? The GF Data finding above is honest about this: the multiple benefit showed up most clearly above $50 million of enterprise value, and smaller deals “tended not to experience a valuation boost”. The argument at the lower end is not the multiple. It is that you find your own problems first, which is worth more than the fee the first time a buyer retrades a deal over something you could have fixed.

The bottom line

Add-backs are not a trick and they are not free. They are the one part of your valuation you control entirely before a buyer appears, and the one part that gets audited line by line once one does. The owner who walks in with three years of contemporaneous documentation, an externally benchmarked compensation figure and the negative adjustments already on the page keeps nearly all of the schedule. The owner who walks in with a tab built last month keeps the half that is provable and loses the multiple on the rest.
