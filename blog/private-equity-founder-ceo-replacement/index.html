Will Private Equity Replace the Founder CEO?

By Michael Chasen · Updated November 16, 2026

Usually, yes — and much earlier than owners expect. In the best dataset that exists on the question, 71% of companies bought by private equity hired a new CEO before the sponsor exited. The more useful finding sits underneath that one: the most common moment for the change is the first year of ownership, where it has nothing to do with how the company is performing and everything to do with a decision the sponsor had already made when it signed.

So the question a founder should be asking in diligence is not “will you fire me in year three?” It is “who is your plan for this chair on day one, and what happens to my equity if the answer is not me?” Those are two different conversations, and only one of them gets a straight answer.

Where the 71% comes from

Paul Gompers, Steven Kaplan and Vladimir Mukharlyamov studied 193 US companies with enterprise value above $1 billion that private equity firms bought between 2010 and 2016, following each one until it was sold or went bankrupt (“The Market for CEOs: Evidence from Private Equity”, April 2022, a working paper the authors label preliminary). Their headline numbers:

  • 137 of the 193 companies — 71% — changed CEO before sale or bankruptcy.
  • The rate rises with time owned: almost 80% for companies bought in 2010–2012, 67% for 2013–2014, 66% for 2015–2016.
  • Almost 75% of the new CEOs were external hires, and 67% were complete outsiders with no prior board seat or employment at the company.
  • Of those outside hires, 69% came most recently from a public company and 32% from an S&P 500 company.

Two caveats matter before you apply any of that to yourself. These are billion-dollar deals, so most of the companies were already institutional — professional boards, outside directors, a CEO who was an employee rather than the person who founded the business. And there is no equivalent dataset for the lower middle market, where the CEO is usually also the seller. What transfers from the research is the mechanism and the timing, not the percentage.

The change happens at the deal, not in year three

The same paper breaks CEO changes down by when they happened. Of the 134 changes with outcome data, 39 came in the first year and 72 within the first two; 62 came later than that. And the early ones are associated with the best deals, not the worst: buyouts that brought in an external CEO during the first year averaged a 3.09x multiple on invested capital, against 2.35x for external CEO changes generally and 2.16x for those made after two years. The authors' reading is the obvious one — a change that close to closing is not a reaction to results, it is the plan.

A second study points the same way from a different angle. Francesca Cornelli and Oğuzhan Karakaş, looking at UK buyouts of public companies, found a CEO change in about 52% of transactions during the transition to private ownership — but once the company settled under its new owner, annual CEO turnover fell to 9.2%, against 14.5% at the same companies before the buyout and 14.4% at matched companies that stayed public (“CEO Turnover in LBOs: The Role of Boards”). The same paper cites Acharya, Kehoe and Reyner (2009) finding 39% CEO turnover within the first 100 days of an LBO, and Gong and Wu (2011) finding 51% within two years of announcement.

Put the two together and the picture is consistent: private equity is not trigger-happy with CEOs mid-hold — it is decisive at the change of ownership. The risk to a founder is concentrated in the weeks around closing and the first twelve months after it, which is precisely the window in which the owner has the least leverage and the most goodwill towards the people who just wired the money.

Why a sponsor replaces a founder

In a survey of 79 private equity firms cited in the Gompers paper, 58% said they typically replace senior management before or after their initial investment, and almost 43% named replacing the CEO or CFO as an important source of value creation. That is not hostility to founders; it is a view about what the next five years of the company require:

  • The job changes at closing. Monthly reporting packs, a lender's covenant tests, a board that meets on a schedule, an integration plan if the thesis is buy-and-build. What actually happens after private equity buys your company is a different job from the one most founders have been doing, and plenty of founders do not want it.
  • The thesis may need a specialist. A roll-up needs someone who has integrated acquisitions before. A margin-expansion plan needs someone who has run that playbook. Sponsors hire externally three times out of four because they are buying a track record in a specific manoeuvre.
  • The sponsor has a bench. Operating partners, executives it has backed before, and a search firm on retainer. You are not being measured against yourself; you are being measured against the person on that list.

It is worth knowing that the mid-hold version of this goes badly often enough to be documented. In an AlixPartners survey of more than 100 private equity and portfolio-company leaders, performance after an unplanned CEO exit got worse in 46% of cases and improved in about a quarter, and 83% of respondents said forcing a CEO out adds to the holding period (reported by Consultancy.uk, 10 April 2018). A longer hold is not a neutral outcome for you either, because your rolled equity is sitting inside it — see how long private equity actually holds a company.

Nothing protects the chair except the documents

Founders routinely leave the signing dinner believing they have an understanding about staying on. An understanding is not a document, and three documents decide this:

  • The shareholders agreement and the board. Officers are appointed and removed by the board, so the composition of that board is the whole question — including in minority deals, where it is set out in the governance schedule rather than the share register. Board seats, veto rights and consent lists covers what to read there.
  • The employment agreement. Is there a fixed term or is it at will? How is “cause” defined, and how much of it is subjective? What severance is payable, and what does the non-compete stop you doing for how long afterwards?
  • The equity documents. This is the one most sellers skim, and it is where the money is.

For a concrete example of how the equity side is written, the public record is better than any summary. The letter of appointment between Rainbow Capital Group (the holding company of the Wella group, backed by KKR) and its chairman, filed as an exhibit to Wella's registration statement, sets out the leaver mechanics in full (SEC, Exhibit 10.19, filed 31 August 2026). In that document:

  • A Good Leaver is someone whose role ends through death, permanent ill health or disability, or termination by the company other than for cause. Everyone else, plus anyone entering bankruptcy proceedings, is a Bad Leaver.
  • The company holds a call option over up to 100% of the equity for twelve months after the role ends.
  • A Good Leaver's vested equity is bought at the higher of fair market value and cost; the unvested portion is bought at cost.
  • A Bad Leaver's equity, vested and unvested alike, is bought at the lower of cost and fair market value.
  • Investment equity vests 25% a year over four years and vests in full on an exit; the incentive units additionally require a liquidity event before they pay anything.

Read that list again as a seller. “Terminated without cause” is the good outcome. Resigning because you no longer recognise the job is the bad one, and it is priced at the lower of cost and value.

What a leaver clause costs: a worked example

Invented numbers, to show the arithmetic. A company with $5M of adjusted EBITDA sells at 7.0x, so $35M of enterprise value, $5M of existing debt, $30M of equity value. The founder takes cash for most of it and rolls $6,000,000 into the new holding company, on terms like the ones above: 25% vesting a year, a call option on departure. Eighteen months later the sponsor installs its own CEO. By then the holdco equity has appreciated 60%, so the stake would be worth $9.6M if it were fully vested and nobody called it.

The gap between the top row and the bottom one — $2.7M on a $6M rollover — is the price of a clause most sellers never negotiate, triggered by an event they were told would not happen. Rolled dollars and management incentive units are usually papered differently, and purchased equity ordinarily gets better leaver treatment than an incentive plan does; the Wella letter is a reminder that investment equity can carry vesting and call rights too. Find out which bucket your rollover sits in before you agree the headline number, and read what rollover equity is actually worth alongside it.

Four questions to ask before you sign

  • “In your last five platform deals, who was running the company two years after closing?” Sponsors know this answer precisely. It is a factual question about their history, not a hypothetical about yours.
  • “Who appoints and removes the CEO under the new documents?” Ask for the governance schedule, not a description of it.
  • “What exactly is cause, and what is the severance if it is not cause?” A tight, objective definition of cause is worth more than a bigger title.
  • “If I leave, at what price is my rolled equity bought back, and when?” Good leaver and bad leaver, vested and unvested, with the call window spelled out.

None of this is a reason to walk away from a private equity process; it is a reason to price the risk. If you want to see what the headline number looks like before any of these clauses touch it, start with a valuation range for your business, then read the honest ledger on selling to private equity.

Frequently asked questions

Does private equity always replace the founder?
No. In the large-buyout dataset above, 29% of companies kept the same CEO all the way to exit, and a founder who wants the post-close job and can do it is frequently the cheapest, fastest option available to a sponsor. But 71% is the base rate to plan against, not 50%.

Can I negotiate a guaranteed term as CEO?
You can negotiate an employment agreement with a fixed term and a defined severance, and you should. What you cannot negotiate is a promise that the board will not exercise its right to appoint officers; the protection is priced in the severance and the leaver terms, not in the job title.

Is it different in a minority deal?
The governance schedule matters more than the percentage. A minority investor with board seats and a consent list can shape the management question without owning the majority of the shares.

What if I want to step back anyway?
Then the CEO question is a negotiating asset rather than a threat: a sponsor paying for a transition it has to run itself will discount the price for it, so a credible second-in-command built before the process is worth real money.

The bottom line

The founder-CEO question is decided at the deal table and documented in the equity schedule, and both of those happen before anyone finds out whether you would have been good at the job under new ownership. Seventy-one percent is the number to carry into the room. The work is to make sure the documents price your departure honestly if it comes, and to stop treating the chair as something that comes with the shares.

It is also the clearest difference between a sponsored deal and an Independent Buyout. There is no fund with a bench of operators and no sponsor underwriting a management change as a source of value: the company is bought by a trust, the trust holds control, and the existing team carries on running the business — which is the point of the structure rather than a concession inside it.
