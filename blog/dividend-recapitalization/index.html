Dividend Recapitalization Explained: Borrowing Against Your Own Company

By Michael Chasen · Updated October 18, 2026

A dividend recapitalization is a loan. The company borrows against its own cash flow and pays the proceeds out to its shareholders as a distribution. Nobody buys anything. No equity changes hands, no buyer joins the cap table, no board seat gets created, and no diligence process ends in a purchase agreement. You still own exactly the percentage you owned the week before - of a company that now owes several times its annual profit to a lender.

That is the whole trade, and it is worth stating bluntly because the structure gets sold as though it were an exit. It is not an exit. It is an advance against one, taken from the business rather than from a buyer, and the business repays it whether or not next year goes the way the model said it would.

How much a dividend recap actually pays

The size of the check is set by what a lender will advance against trailing EBITDA, less whatever debt the company already carries, less fees. Nothing else.

For a sense of where the market sits, GF Data’s contributed set has run total debt at an historical average of 3.7x trailing twelve-month EBITDA, with senior debt at 2.7x in the first quarter of 2025 rising to 2.8x by the fourth; platform buyouts bottomed at 3.2x total debt during 2025. Those are acquisition multiples, which matters: in a buyout a sponsor is injecting fresh equity underneath the debt. A dividend recap injects none. Most lenders therefore size a recap below what they would fund for the same company in a change-of-control deal, and the gap is the single most common reason an owner's expectation and the term sheet do not meet.

Take an invented company - $6,000,000 of adjusted EBITDA and $4,000,000 of existing funded debt, with no real-world counterpart - and assume a lender will go to 3.0x total leverage.

Line Amount
New facility at 3.0x $6,000,000 $18,000,000
Less: refinance existing debt ($4,000,000)
Less: arrangement fee, legal, quality of earnings, solvency opinion ($900,000)
Cash distributed to the owner $13,100,000

If you want the EBITDA figure a lender would actually underwrite before running this arithmetic on your own business, how PE firms value a company covers the pricing process, adjusted EBITDA add-backs covers the schedule itself - including the 15 to 20 percent ceilings lenders write into their own credit agreements - and the business valuation calculator will give you a sector-adjusted starting range.

The number that gets left out: what the remaining equity is worth

Price the same invented company at 6.8x and its enterprise value is $40,800,000. Before the recap, the equity is worth $40,800,000 less the $4,000,000 of debt, or $36,800,000. After the recap, the same equity is worth $40,800,000 less $18,000,000, or $22,800,000.

Add the $13,100,000 you received and you are holding $35,900,000 against $36,800,000 before. The $900,000 difference is the fees. A dividend recap does not create value; it converts illiquid value into cash and leaves the risk behind on a smaller equity base. That is not an argument against doing one. It is an argument against treating the distribution as a gain, which is how it tends to get described at the dinner table.

What it costs to carry

At an all-in rate of 9 percent, $18,000,000 of debt costs $1,620,000 a year in interest. Add 5 percent annual amortization - $900,000 - and fixed charges are $2,520,000 against $6,000,000 of EBITDA, before capital expenditure and cash taxes. On the pre-recap balance sheet the same two lines came to roughly $560,000. Debt service goes up more than fourfold on the day the wire clears.

Then there is the covenant. If the credit agreement sets a total leverage test at 3.25x, the company breaches it when EBITDA falls to $18,000,000 divided by 3.25, or $5,538,000. That is a 7.7 percent decline. One bad quarter, one lost customer, one tariff year. Before the recap, the same covenant on $4,000,000 of debt would have tolerated EBITDA falling by nearly 80 percent. The headroom is what you actually sold, and it does not appear anywhere on the term sheet.

How the distribution is taxed

This is entity-specific enough that the only responsible version is the general shape, with the detail left to a tax advisor who has seen the cap table.

For a C corporation paying out of earnings and profits, the distribution is a dividend. The top federal rate on qualified dividends is 20 percent, and a 3.8 percent net investment income tax applies above $200,000 of modified adjusted gross income for a single filer or $250,000 filing jointly - dividends are explicitly inside the definition of net investment income. At 23.8 percent, the $13,100,000 above leaves about $9,980,000.

Where a distribution is not paid out of earnings and profits, the treatment changes. The IRS puts it plainly: such a distribution is generally a return of capital that "reduces your stock's adjusted cost basis", and once basis reaches zero, any further nondividend distribution is taxable as a capital gain. Pass-through entities follow their own basis rules. The practical consequence is the same in every case: a recap that looks tax-efficient today has usually spent basis you were going to want later, when you sell for real.

Why so many of these are happening right now

Dividend recaps are a sponsor instrument before they are an owner instrument, and sponsors have been leaning on them because the exit market stopped cooperating. Dechert's 2025 review notes that private equity exit routes slowed, holding periods lengthened, and recaps let a fund return cash to its investors without selling the company. Volume followed: $22.4 billion from 1 January to mid-February 2025, against $14.0 billion in the same stretch of 2024. Leveraged loans account for 91 percent of sponsored recap volume, and average pricing on a recapitalization-related bond deal fell to 7.36 percent in 2025 from 8.38 percent in 2024.

Across the full year, PitchBook data cited by Capstone Partners puts sponsored dividend recap loan issuance at $74.3 billion in 2025, up 11 percent after a 326 percent rise in 2024, with first-quarter 2026 issuance down 54.5 percent to $11 billion.

For an owner weighing one, the reason behind the surge is more useful than the total. A fund doing a dividend recap is usually doing it because it cannot sell the company at the price it needs. If you rolled equity into a sponsor deal, a recap on your own company is a signal about the timing of your second bite - rollover equity and the second bite covers what that stake is actually waiting on, and what happens after a private equity buyout follows the hold period year by year.

The exposure nobody puts in the pitch: solvency and § 548

A dividend recap is a transfer out of the company for which the company receives nothing. That is the precise fact pattern the fraudulent transfer rules exist to police.

Under 11 U.S. Code § 548(a)(1), a bankruptcy trustee can unwind a transfer "made or incurred on or within 2 years before the date of the filing of the petition" where the debtor "received less than a reasonably equivalent value in exchange for such transfer" and was insolvent, became insolvent as a result, was left with "an unreasonably small capital", or intended to incur debts "beyond the debtor's ability to pay" as they matured. The money has already been spent by then; the claim is against the person who received it.

That is why lenders and boards commission a solvency opinion alongside a recap. Capstone Partners describes it as an independent, pro forma test of whether the company still has assets exceeding liabilities at fair value, enough capital to fund operations, and the cash to meet its obligations as they mature - valuable for "risk mitigation and governance protection" rather than as approval of the deal. The firm points to the Lucky Bucks case, where a 2021 recap was followed by a 2023 Chapter 11 and noteholders alleged the dividend was a fraudulent transfer, with the litigation still live as of April 2026. Two years is a long window in a business cycle. Get the opinion.

The honest comparison is between a dividend recap and a minority recapitalization, because both leave you in charge. The recap pays faster, costs no equity and asks no permission; the minority recap brings in money that does not have to be repaid and a partner who shares the downside, at the price of a consent list. Majority recapitalization versus a full sale runs the next step up. Types of buyouts places the leveraged recapitalization among the structures where somebody does actually buy something.

When a dividend recap is the right answer

Three conditions, and they want to be true together. The business throws off cash predictably enough that a covenant with 8 percent of headroom is not a coin toss - recurring revenue, long contracts, low customer concentration. The money is for something that does not need to earn a return, such as taking a chunk of your net worth out of a single asset. And you are not planning to sell within a couple of years, because the debt you just added comes straight off the equity value a buyer pays for, and the basis you just spent is not there to shelter the gain.

If any of those is shaky, the structure is doing something other than what it says. Borrowing against a cyclical business to fund a distribution is not liquidity planning; it is a leveraged bet on next year placed by someone who has already taken their chips off the table.

Frequently asked questions

Is a dividend recapitalization the same as a leveraged recapitalization? Close enough in ordinary use. "Leveraged recapitalization" describes any material shift of the capital structure toward debt; a dividend recap is the version where the borrowed money leaves the company as a distribution to shareholders rather than funding a buyback, an acquisition or growth.

Can a company do a dividend recap without a private equity sponsor? Yes. Lenders underwrite the cash flow, not the shareholder. A founder-owned business with stable EBITDA and a clean balance sheet can raise one. What an owner usually lacks is the sponsor's practice at negotiating the covenant package, which is where the real terms live.

How much can I take out in a dividend recap? New money is the lender's total leverage multiple times EBITDA, less existing debt, less fees. On the invented $6,000,000-EBITDA company above at 3.0x, that is $13,100,000. Expect a recap multiple below what the same lender would fund for an acquisition, because no new equity is going in underneath it.

Does a dividend recap hurt a future sale price? Not the enterprise value, which is set off EBITDA. It reduces what you receive at closing, because the buyer assumes or repays the debt out of the purchase price. It can also cost you optionality: a company near its covenants has less room to invest in the growth that would have raised the multiple.

The bottom line

A dividend recapitalization is the fastest way to get a large amount of cash out of a company you intend to keep, and the only one that asks nobody's permission. It is also the only structure on the menu where you keep every bit of the risk and hand over every bit of the headroom. Taken against a business with contracted, predictable cash flow, it is a reasonable piece of personal balance-sheet management. Taken against a business whose EBITDA moves with the economy, it is a way of being the first creditor paid and the last equity holder standing.

The Independent Buyout exists for the owner who looks at that trade and concludes the cash is worth having but the risk is not worth keeping: the financing is still sized off the company's own cash flow, but the equity moves to a trust owned by the employees, existing leadership keeps running the business, and there is no sponsor and no exit clock attached to the money.
