Private Equity Buyout: What It Is and How It Works

By Michael Chasen · Updated October 4, 2026

A private equity buyout is the purchase of a controlling stake in a company by an investment fund, paid for mostly with debt raised against the company's own cash flow, with the explicit intention of selling again in roughly three to seven years. Every other feature of the deal — the price, the governance, the role you keep — follows from those two facts: borrowed money and a clock.

The word “buyout” sounds like a single event; it is really a multi-year arrangement that starts with a wire. What follows is who the buyers are, how the debt gets sized against your EBITDA, what an owner actually banks, and what changes on the Monday after close.

The types of buyout, and who is actually buying

“Buyout” covers several structures that behave very differently for a seller. The distinction that matters is not the label but who writes the cheque and where the debt sits afterwards.

TypeWho buysWhat it means for the owner
Leveraged buyout (LBO)A private equity fundThe standard case. Control changes hands, the debt lands on the company's balance sheet, and you typically roll some equity and stay for a transition.
Management buyout (MBO)Your existing management teamContinuity, but the team rarely has the capital, so the question becomes who finances them. How a management buyout gets financed works through the options.
Management buy-in (MBI)An outside executive or small teamA new operator buys in and runs the business. Usually backed by a fund or a lender, and the riskiest of the three for continuity.
Secondary buyoutOne private equity fund, from anotherWhat happens to most sponsor-owned companies at the end of the hold. If you rolled equity in round one, this is where the second bite is paid.
Add-on (bolt-on)A sponsor-owned platform companyYou are bought by a company that a fund already owns. Smaller cheques, faster processes, and your business is absorbed into someone else's systems.
Take-privateA fund buying a listed companyThe headline-grabbing version, and the one least relevant to a private owner — same mechanics, public-company disclosure.

A management buyout and a private equity buyout are often set against each other as if they were opposites. They are not: most MBOs at this size are financed by a sponsor, which makes them an LBO with a friendlier buyer on the letterhead. The thing to establish early is who ends up holding the control rights, not whose name is on the offer.

How the debt is sized — the part nobody shows the seller

The price in a buyout is set by what a lender will advance against your earnings, and lenders work from published supervisory markers. The federal banking agencies' interagency guidance on leveraged lending says the definitions institutions use “commonly contain some combination of” criteria including proceeds “used for buyouts, acquisitions, or capital distributions” and transactions where “Total Debt divided by EBITDA … or Senior Debt divided by EBITDA exceed 4.0X EBITDA or 3.0X EBITDA, respectively”. A buyout of a profitable private company typically meets both, so it is usually classified as leveraged lending from the outset.

Two numbers in that guidance set the practical ceiling on your price. The first: “a leverage level after planned asset sales … in excess of 6X Total Debt/EBITDA raises concerns for most industries.” The second is the repayment test — supervisors look for “the ability to fully amortize senior secured debt or the ability to repay at least 50 percent of total debt over a five-to-seven year period”. That five-to-seven-year repayment window is not a coincidence. It is the same window as the fund's hold period, because both are set by the same arithmetic.

Work it through on a hypothetical. A company with $8M of adjusted EBITDA, bought at 7.0x, is a $56M enterprise value. It carries $6M of existing net debt, so the equity is worth $50M. The buyer funds it like this:

SourcesAmountUsesAmount
New debt (4.5x EBITDA)$36.0MPaid to selling shareholders$50.0M
Rollover equity from the owner$10.0MRepay existing net debt$6.0M
Sponsor cash equity$12.5MFinancing and transaction costs$2.5M
Total$58.5MTotal$58.5M

Note what the sponsor actually put in: $12.5M of its own money against a $56M business. The owner's rolled $10M is nearly as large a cheque. That ratio is the whole point of the structure, and it is covered in detail in how private equity actually finances a buyout.

Now test the debt against the regulators' own standard. Assume a 9.5% blended cost across the stack — a made-up but unremarkable assumption — so interest runs about $3.4M a year. Against $8M of EBITDA, after maintenance capital expenditure of roughly $0.8M and cash tax of about $1.0M, the company has something close to $2.8M a year to put against principal. Repaying half of $36M takes about six and a half years at that rate: inside the window, but only just, and only if nothing goes wrong. This is why every sponsor model assumes EBITDA grows. The plan is not optional, and from the day after close it is your plan too.

What the owner actually banks

The multiple is the number owners repeat; the number that matters is what reaches the account. On the same $56M deal, with a near-zero tax basis and no state income tax:

LineAmount
Enterprise value at 7.0x $8M EBITDA$56.0M
Less existing net debt($6.0M)
Equity value$50.0M
Less rollover into the new company (20%)($10.0M)
Cash consideration$40.0M
Less 8% escrow, released at 18 months($3.2M)
Less sell-side advisory, legal and QoE costs($1.4M)
Less federal tax at 23.8% on the $40M($9.5M)
Net of tax, from the closing payment$25.9M

The tax line is 20% long-term capital gains plus the 3.8% net investment income tax. IRS Topic 409 sets the 20% rate for taxable income above the 15% bracket thresholds, and the net investment income tax adds 3.8% on capital gains above a $250,000 modified AGI for joint filers. Add a state and the effective rate goes higher.

So $25.9M lands against a $56M headline — about 46 cents on the enterprise-value dollar, with $3.2M more arriving later if the escrow releases in full. The single biggest gap is the rollover, and it is not a loss: it is $10M of value moved to a later date and made contingent on the sponsor's plan working. Rollover equity and the second bite explains what has to be true for that $10M to be worth more than $10M, and the pros and cons of selling to private equity runs the same ledger on a deal with no rollover, where the proportion reaching the seller is materially higher. If you want to know where your own multiple is likely to sit before any of this applies, the business valuation calculator puts a sector-adjusted range around your EBITDA, and how private equity values a company covers what moves it up or down.

What changes the Monday after close

Control in a private company is not a percentage; it is a document. Under Delaware law, the default jurisdiction for companies taking institutional capital, “the business and affairs of every corporation organized under this chapter shall be managed by or under the direction of a board of directors” (8 Del. C. § 141(a)). After a buyout the fund appoints that board, and a schedule of reserved matters — new debt, capital expenditure above a threshold, acquisitions, hiring and firing the CEO, distributions, selling the company — needs its consent regardless of what you still own. What a minority private equity stake really controls works through a typical consent list line by line; the majority version is the same list with fewer negotiated carve-outs.

The operating changes are more predictable than owners expect: monthly reporting on a sponsor's template within ten business days, a thirteen-week cash flow, a budget the board approves, a CFO upgrade in the first year, and a buy-and-build plan if your sector supports one. What happens after private equity buys your company covers the first twenty-four months in detail. Where the add-on strategy is the whole thesis — multi-unit restaurant groups are a clear example, where a platform is assembled from independents and sold at a larger-company multiple — expect integration work to outrank everything else on the agenda.

When a buyout is the right answer

It fits when you want most of your money now, you are genuinely ready to answer to a board, and the business has the cash flow stability to carry four to five turns of debt through a bad year. It fits badly when the company is your life's work and you expect to keep running it your way, when earnings are cyclical enough that a covenant breach is a live possibility, or when the people who built the business will be worse off than you are.

The honest alternatives are worth pricing before you run a process. A minority recapitalization takes a smaller amount off the table and leaves you in the chair. The full list of alternatives to selling to private equity sets out what each one pays. Common questions

What is the difference between a buyout and an acquisition? An acquisition is any purchase of a company. A buyout means a purchase of control financed substantially with debt raised against the acquired company itself. A strategic acquirer pays from its own balance sheet and keeps the business; a buyout fund borrows against yours and has to sell.

How long does a private equity buyout take? Six to nine months from engaging an advisor to money in the account is a normal range for a company of this size: eight to ten weeks of preparation and quality-of-earnings work, six to eight weeks to a letter of intent, then sixty to ninety days of exclusivity for confirmatory diligence and financing. Deals that move faster usually did the preparation first.

Do I have to stay after a buyout? Almost always for a transition, often for longer, and the terms are negotiated rather than assumed. If you roll equity, your second bite depends on the plan being delivered, which gives the sponsor a strong interest in keeping you involved and gives you a strong interest in the employment agreement being specific.

Is 7x a fair multiple? It depends almost entirely on size, growth and sector, and on how defensible your adjusted EBITDA is under a quality-of-earnings review. The multiple quoted in a first conversation is not the multiple in the final purchase agreement; what sits between them is diligence.

The bottom line

A private equity buyout is a financing structure wearing the clothes of a sale. The fund is not buying your company so much as borrowing against it, and the terms that decide whether the deal was good for you — how much debt the business has to carry, how much of your consideration is deferred into a rollover, who approves next year's budget — are all set before the multiple is agreed. Owners who negotiate the multiple and accept the structure are negotiating the smaller number.

There is a version of this that keeps the liquidity and drops the outside fund. An Independent Buyout makes your own leadership team the buyer, financed against the business and priced the way a sponsor would price it, so the cash arrives without a fund holding the consent list or the exit clock.
