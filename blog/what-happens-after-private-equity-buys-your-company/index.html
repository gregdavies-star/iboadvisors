What Happens After Private Equity Buys Your Company

By Michael Chasen · Updated September 9, 2026

Three things change the day after closing, and everything else follows from them. The board becomes the decision-making body instead of you. The debt used to buy the company sits on the company's own balance sheet, so debt service becomes the first claim on cash flow. And a clock starts, because the fund has to sell the business again to earn its return.

What that looks like in practice: a value-creation plan inside the first hundred days, a reporting calendar you have never had to keep before, a fee agreement between your company and the fund, add-on acquisitions, a real chance of a new CEO, and a second sale several years out that you will not control. Here is each of those, with what the public record shows.

The first hundred days

The diligence work does not stop at closing; it turns into an operating plan. Expect a monthly close on a fixed calendar with a board package attached, a rolling cash forecast, a short list of tracked KPIs, and named owners and deadlines for each workstream in the value-creation plan. Finance is usually the first function to get rebuilt, because the reporting the fund needs is more granular than what most founder-run companies produce.

None of this is punitive. It is the machinery a fund needs to manage a portfolio and report to its own investors. But it is a genuine change in how the company runs, and it is the change owners most consistently underestimate when they picture life after the deal.

The debt is on the company, not the fund

The single most misunderstood feature of a buyout is where the money comes from. The fund contributes equity; the rest is borrowed, and the borrower is your company. We walk through the mechanics in detail in how private equity actually finances a buyout, but the post-close consequence is what matters here: interest and mandatory principal payments come out of the business before anything else, and the credit agreement sets covenants the company now has to test against every quarter.

Practically, that changes how capital gets allocated. Discretionary spending - a new facility, a hiring plan, a piece of equipment you would previously have bought out of cash flow - now competes with debt service and needs approval from people who read the covenant package before they read the business case.

Your company starts paying the fund

Many buyouts come with a monitoring or management services agreement between the sponsor and the acquired company. The company pays the fund an ongoing fee, usually described as compensation for advisory and consulting services. These agreements are ordinary market practice, and they are also the subject of one of the better-documented enforcement records in the industry.

In October 2015 the SEC announced that three private equity fund advisers within The Blackstone Group agreed to pay nearly $39 million - $26.2 million in disgorgement, $2.6 million in prejudgment interest and a $10 million civil penalty - to settle charges related to monitoring fees charged to portfolio companies. According to the SEC, the agreements typically covered advisory and consulting services for a ten-year period, and the firm terminated them early and accelerated payment of the future fees ahead of portfolio company sales and IPOs, in some cases for services that would not be provided (SEC press release 2015-235).

The seller's takeaway is not that fees are scandalous. It is that a fee agreement between the fund and your company is a term to see and negotiate before signing, not a document you meet for the first time at the closing table.

The CEO seat is not guaranteed, and it is usually the fund's call

Owners who plan to keep running the business after a sale should look hard at this data. In AlixPartners' eleventh annual private equity leadership survey, published in March 2026 and drawing on 427 responses from 174 private equity leaders and 253 portfolio company executives, 65% of private equity firms reported CEO turnover during the holding period, and only 9% said their firms rarely replace CEOs. The same research found 83% of private equity executives agree that unplanned CEO turnover lengthens holding periods (AlixPartners).

The prior year's edition of the same survey, fielded from October to December 2024 across 161 private equity executives and 200 portfolio company leaders, put it more bluntly: 86% of CEO turnover is driven by the private equity firm rather than initiated by the CEO (AlixPartners).

If staying in the chair matters to you, that is a negotiation, not an assumption - and the leverage to negotiate it exists only before you sign.

Your company becomes a platform or an add-on

Buy-and-build is no longer a strategy some funds use; it is the default. Add-on acquisitions accounted for 72.9% of all buyouts in 2025, holding steady with the five-year average (Cherry Bekaert, Private Equity Report: 2025 Trends and 2026 Outlook).

Which side of that statistic your company lands on determines the next few years. As a platform, your company is the acquirer: you inherit integration work, more debt, and a management team that suddenly needs to absorb other businesses. As an add-on, your company is folded into someone else's platform, and the systems, brand and reporting lines you built get replaced by theirs. Both paths can pay well. Neither resembles the independent company you sold. The reason the model works for the buyer is arithmetic we cover in how private equity firms value a company: smaller companies trade at lower multiples than the platform they get bolted onto.

The balance sheet can be re-levered to pay the fund

A dividend recapitalization is, in the plain description of one law firm's market summary, "a financing strategy through which a private company incurs new debt to pay a cash dividend to its shareholders" (Dechert). The company borrows; the owners take cash out; the debt stays behind.

It is not a fringe practice. Dividend recapitalization volume reached $22.4 billion between January 1 and mid-February 2025 alone, up from $14.0 billion in the same period of 2024, with leveraged loans making up 91% of sponsored dividend recap volume (Dechert). If you rolled equity into the deal, you receive your share of the dividend. You also still own a slice of a company that now carries more debt than the one you sold.

How long until they sell again

The pitch is usually a three-to-five-year hold. The market has moved. The median holding period for private equity-backed portfolio companies has reached 6.0 years - the longest on record in a data set covering exits from 2000 through 2025 (Private Equity Info).

That number matters most to whatever you leave on the table. Rollover equity is illiquid until the fund decides to sell, and the decision is theirs, not yours. What actually happens to rollover equity covers how the second bite tends to work out in practice.

A worked example: what year three looks like

The numbers below are invented to show the arithmetic, not drawn from any transaction. Assume a company with $8.0M of EBITDA sold at 8.5x, so an enterprise value of $68M, funded 55% with debt - $37.4M - at 9% cash interest, with 1% annual mandatory amortization and a $500K annual sponsor fee.

Year-three cash walk Amount
EBITDA $8.00M
Cash interest (9% on $37.4M) ($3.37M)
Mandatory principal amortization (1%) ($0.37M)
Sponsor monitoring fee ($0.50M)
Capital expenditures ($1.20M)
Cash taxes ($0.90M)
Cash available for growth $1.66M

Roughly a fifth of EBITDA is left to run the company with. The same business with no acquisition debt and no sponsor fee, carrying the same capex and about $1.9M of taxes, would have had close to $4.9M of discretionary cash. Neither figure is good or bad on its own - the leverage is what makes the fund's return work, and it may make yours work too. But it explains why post-close life feels tighter than the model implied, and why capital requests start getting declined. Before you compare offers, it is worth knowing your own baseline: the business valuation calculator gives an EBITDA-multiple range for your industry.

What you can actually negotiate before you sign

  • Board composition and the reserved-matters list. Even a minority investor typically holds vetoes over financing, acquisitions and the timing of a future sale. What "minority stake" really means lists the rights those documents usually cover.
  • The monitoring or management fee agreement. Ask to see it, cap it, and address what happens to unpaid future fees on an early exit.
  • Capital expenditure thresholds. The dollar figure above which you need approval decides how much of your job survives the deal.
  • Rollover terms. Information rights, tag-along and drag-along provisions, anti-dilution, and how your equity gets valued in a future sale or a recapitalization.
  • Leadership protections. Employment agreements, severance, and the standard the board must meet before making a change.

Frequently asked questions

Do I still run the company after a private equity sale? Day to day, usually yes, within the limits set by the credit agreement and the board's reserved matters. Strategically, no - decisions about acquisitions, financing and a future sale move to the board.

Can a private equity firm replace me as CEO? Yes, and it is common: 65% of private equity firms in AlixPartners' 2026 survey reported CEO turnover during the holding period, and the prior year's survey attributed 86% of that turnover to the firm rather than the CEO.

Will they put more debt on the company? Often. Add-on acquisitions are usually financed with additional debt, and dividend recapitalizations add debt specifically to fund a distribution to shareholders.

How long before the company is sold again? Longer than the pitch. The median holding period is now 6.0 years, the longest on record.

What happens to my employees? The plan is usually to grow the business, which is good for most people in it. The finance function typically changes the most, integration of add-ons can duplicate roles, and the leadership team's incentives get restructured around the fund's exit rather than annual profit.

What are the alternatives if none of this appeals? There are several, and they are rarely all presented at once. Every exit option explained compares them side by side; if a family member is a candidate to take over, start with family business succession planning.

The bottom line

After a private equity sale the company keeps your name on the building and stops being yours to steer. The board decides, the debt sets the priorities, the fee agreement runs between the fund and your business, the CEO seat is the fund's to fill, and the exit happens on a clock now averaging six years. All of that can be a fair trade for the price - as long as you priced it knowingly.

The reason this post exists is that most owners are shown one version of the trade and never the alternatives. An Independent Buyout is one of them: the same leverage and the same market-based valuation, with the company's own leadership and employees as the buyer instead of an outside fund, which means no board seats traded away and no second sale on someone else's timetable.
