Majority Recapitalization: Cash vs Control

By Michael Chasen · Updated September 28, 2026

The short answer

A majority recapitalization is a sale of 51% to 80% of your company's equity to a financial buyer, with the balance rolled into the recapitalized business instead of cashed out. Most of your money comes off the table at closing. You keep a real stake — commonly 20% to 40% — that pays a second time when the sponsor sells. And on the morning after the closing, you no longer control the company.

That last sentence is the one owners skip. A majority recap gets pitched as a partnership, and the economics can be genuinely good, but the word "majority" means what it says: the buyer holds the votes, appoints the board, and sets the exit date. If staying in charge is the point of the exercise, this is the wrong structure — a partial sale built around governance rather than percentage is the one to negotiate instead.

Majority recap, minority recap, outright sale

Three structures get described with overlapping language. What separates them is not how much cash arrives; it is who decides things afterwards and when the rest of your money shows up.

Minority recapitalization Majority recapitalization Outright sale
Equity sold Typically 20%–49% Typically 51%–80% 100%
Cash at close A slice Most of the enterprise value All of it, less escrow and any earn-out
Who controls the board You, subject to a consent list The sponsor The buyer
What you still own The majority A rolled minority stake Nothing
Second payday Yes, on a timetable you influence Yes, on the sponsor's timetable No
Usual reason to do it Growth capital plus some liquidity De-risk now, stay for one more cycle You are finished

The nearest neighbor is the minority deal, and the difference is not marginal. A minority recapitalization leaves you holding the majority of the equity and the chair; a majority recap hands both across the table in exchange for a much larger check. Owners who want the check and assume they kept the chair are the ones who are unhappy in year two.

What a sponsor will actually pay

Pricing in this market is not mysterious. GF Data, which collects completed private-equity-sponsored transactions in the $10 million–$500 million enterprise value range, reported that average purchase price multiples for 2025 held steady year-over-year at 7.2x trailing 12-month adjusted EBITDA across 297 completed deals. Within the year the number moved: average multiples rose to 7.5x in the third quarter, up from 6.9x in the second.

An average across a $10M–$500M universe is a starting point, not your number. Size, growth rate, customer concentration and sector all move it, sometimes by two or three turns — the bands by industry spread a long way around 7.2x, and asset-heavy, labor-intensive sectors such as multi-unit restaurant groups price well below it. Two things are worth doing before you take a multiple seriously: understand how a sponsor normalizes EBITDA before applying any multiple at all, and run your own figures through the business valuation calculator so the first number you hear is not the first number you have thought about.

The arithmetic on a $6 million EBITDA company

The numbers below are invented to show the shape of the structure. They are not a quote, a projection, or a description of any transaction.

Take a company with $6 million of normalized EBITDA, no existing debt, and a negligible tax basis in the stock. Price it at the 7.2x market average and the enterprise value is $43.2 million. Assume the lenders will advance 3.5x EBITDA — an assumption, not a market statistic — and the sponsor asks you to roll 30%.

Line Amount Where it comes from
Enterprise value (7.2x $6M) $43,200,000 Market multiple applied to normalized EBITDA
Acquisition debt (3.5x) $21,000,000 Lenders, secured against the company's cash flow
Equity in the recapitalized company $22,200,000 Enterprise value less the debt
Your rolled 30% $6,660,000 Reinvested, not received
Sponsor's 70% $15,540,000 Fund equity
Cash to you at close $36,540,000 Enterprise value less the rollover
Federal tax on the cash, at 23.8% ($8,696,520) 20% long-term capital gains plus 3.8% NIIT
Net federal cash $27,843,480 State tax stacks on top of this

The 3.8% is the net investment income tax, which the IRS applies above $200,000 of modified AGI for a single filer and $250,000 filing jointly. Note what the debt does to the shape of the deal: the sponsor is writing a check for $15.5 million to gain control of a $43.2 million business, because the borrowing sits on your company's balance sheet, not the fund's. Your rolled stake is now behind $21 million of debt that was not there last week.

Why the rolled portion usually is not taxed at closing

The single best feature of a majority recap is that the rollover can generally be structured so no tax is due on it until you finally sell. The usual mechanism is a partnership. Under 26 U.S.C. § 721(a), "no gain or loss shall be recognized to a partnership or to any of its partners in the case of a contribution of property to the partnership in exchange for an interest in the partnership" — which is why so many sponsor deals are papered through an LLC holding company taxed as a partnership. Subsection (b) carves out contributions that would make the vehicle an investment company.

The corporate route is narrower. Section 351(a) gives the same nonrecognition treatment for property transferred to a corporation "solely in exchange for stock," but only if the transferors are "in control (as defined in section 368(c)) of the corporation" immediately afterwards — 80% of the voting power and 80% of every other class. A seller rolling 30% into a corporate buyer is nowhere near that threshold on their own, which is precisely why the structure matters more than the headline percentage. Whether any of this applies turns entirely on your own facts and the buyer's chosen vehicle; confirm it with your own tax advisor before you price a deal around it.

What changes the morning after closing

Control does not transfer gradually. It transfers at signing, in a shareholders' agreement that almost nobody reads with the same attention they gave the purchase price. Expect a reconstituted board the sponsor controls, monthly reporting against a budget the board approves, and a consent list covering debt, acquisitions, senior hires, capital expenditure and distributions. The mechanics of that list are the same whether the sponsor holds 51% or 80%, and they are worth reading before you sign: what a private equity stake actually controls goes through the clauses line by line.

The broader change is operational. The company becomes an asset with a scheduled disposal date, and the plan that follows — add-on acquisitions, systems, reporting, sometimes a new CEO — is set out in what actually happens in years one through seven after a buyout. One clause deserves specific attention: the drag-along. When the sponsor decides to sell, your 30% goes with it, at the price and on the terms the sponsor negotiates.

The second bite is a range, not a number

Rollover equity is usually sold on a single scenario: the sponsor grows the business, sells in five years, and your stake is worth several times what you rolled. That scenario is real. So are the others, and leverage makes the spread between them wider than owners expect. Taking the same hypothetical forward five years, with debt paid down out of cash flow in the two healthier cases:

Year 5 scenario EBITDA Exit multiple Debt remaining Your 30%
Plan works $9.0M 7.2x $10.0M $16,440,000
Flat, multiples compress $6.0M 6.5x $10.0M $8,700,000
Earnings fall, little paydown $4.5M 6.5x $16.0M $3,975,000

Against $6.66 million rolled, that is a 2.5x, a 1.3x, or a 40% loss. And the table flatters the downside, because it assumes your rolled equity ranks alongside the sponsor's. Frequently it does not: the fund takes preferred stock with an accruing return that is paid out first, so in the flat case the common may recover far less than the arithmetic above suggests. How rollover equity behaves over a sponsor's hold period is the part of the deal sellers most consistently misjudge, and the security it sits in is the first thing to ask about.

When a majority recap is the right structure

It fits when three things are true at once. You want most of your net worth out of one illiquid asset and you want it now. You genuinely believe the business is worth more in five years than today, and you want exposure to that. And you are willing to run the company — or hand it to someone else — under a board you do not control.

It fits badly when the reason you are selling is that you want to stop working, because the buyer is pricing management continuity into the multiple. It fits badly when the business is at a cyclical peak, because the rollover is a bet placed at the top. And it fits badly when what you actually wanted was liquidity without a new boss, which is a different problem with several answers — every exit route compared side by side is the place to start.

Frequently asked questions

What is a majority recapitalization? A transaction in which an investor buys a controlling stake — usually 51% to 80% — of a privately held company, funded partly with debt placed on the company, while the existing owner reinvests the remainder of their equity in the recapitalized business rather than taking it in cash.

How is a majority recap different from a minority recap? Control. In a minority recapitalization you keep more than half the equity and the board seat that goes with it; in a majority recap you do not. The cash at closing is larger and the governance you retain is smaller.

Is a recapitalization better than an outright sale? It depends on whether you want a second payday more than you want to be finished. A recap converts part of your proceeds into a five-year, leveraged, illiquid bet on the same company. If that bet is one you would make with cash you already had, the structure is rational; if it is not, take the full sale.

Do I pay tax on the equity I roll over? Usually not at closing, if the rollover is structured to qualify for nonrecognition — most commonly under section 721 through a partnership vehicle. The cash portion is taxable in the year of the sale. Neither point is automatic; it depends on the vehicle and your own facts.

How much equity do sponsors ask a seller to roll? There is no fixed figure, and the request is negotiable. What matters more than the percentage is which security you roll into and where it sits in the waterfall: rolling a smaller amount into the same class as the fund can be worth more than rolling a larger amount into common that sits behind a preferred return.

The bottom line

A majority recapitalization is an honest structure that is frequently sold dishonestly. The cash is real, the tax deferral on the rollover is real, and a sponsor with capital and add-on experience can genuinely build something larger than you would have built alone. What is not real is the suggestion that you are still running your own company. You are running someone else's, with a minority stake in it, until they decide to sell.

If the appeal is the liquidity rather than the partnership, there is a structure that separates the two. An Independent Buyout makes a trust held for your own employees the buyer, financed against the business the way a sponsor would finance it, at a valuation set by independent appraisal — so the cash arrives without a fund holding the consent list, the drag-along, or the exit clock.
