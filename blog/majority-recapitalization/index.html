Majority Recapitalization: Sell 70%, Roll 30%

By Michael Chasen · Updated October 6, 2026

A majority recapitalization is a transaction in which an outside investor — almost always a private equity fund — buys more than half of your company, funds part of the purchase with debt the company then carries, and leaves you holding a minority stake that pays out when the investor sells. You get most of your money now. You get a second payment later, if the plan works. You stop being the person who decides.

The structure is sold on the first two sentences and bought on the third. What follows is the arithmetic: where the purchase price comes from, what “rolling 30%” is worth against the enterprise value, and what the second payment looks like in three honest scenarios.

What a majority recapitalization is, and what it is not

A recapitalization is simply a change in how a company is financed. Mercer Advisors' primer on the family describes a majority recapitalization as a transaction where an investor acquires a controlling interest of more than 50% while the existing owners or management team retain a minority stake. Three structures in the same family get confused with it constantly:

  • A minority recapitalization sells 20–49% to a new investor. You keep the majority of the equity and, if the consent list is short, the majority of the authority.
  • A leveraged recapitalization raises the company's debt to buy back shares or restructure ownership. No new investor necessarily joins the cap table.
  • A dividend recapitalization borrows money purely to pay shareholders a distribution. Ownership percentages do not move at all.

Only the majority recap does all three things at once: new control holder, new debt, and a real cash payment to you. In the market shorthand, MidStreet's guide notes the owner typically retains 10–30% of the business and that the sponsor “will usually plan to sell the entire business 3–7 years after acquiring the majority stake”.

Where the money comes from: a worked example

Take an invented company — not a quote, a projection, or a description of any transaction. It does $6M of adjusted EBITDA and carries no funded debt. GF Data's sponsored-deal universe puts the $25M–$50M enterprise value band at 6.8x, so the enterprise value is $40.8M.

The sponsor does not write a $40.8M check. It borrows against your cash flow and funds the rest with fund equity, which is how private equity finances a buyout in every deal of this shape. Sized at 3.5x EBITDA, the senior debt is $21M. That leaves $19.8M of equity in the new capital structure:

Fees, legal and a reps-and-warranties holdback come off that $34.9M before it reaches your account, and the holdback sits in escrow for a year or more. But the number worth staring at is the rollover. You agreed to “keep 30%”. Thirty percent of the post-close equity is $5.9M, which is 14.6% of the $40.8M enterprise value — because the debt consumed half the capital structure before the equity was divided. The percentage you negotiated and the share of the business it represents are two different numbers, and nobody at the closing dinner will point at the gap.

If you do not know roughly what your own enterprise value is, run your EBITDA through the business valuation calculator before you let anyone else anchor the conversation.

How much leverage is normal

Lenders' appetite sets the debt line, and the debt line sets how much of the price is cash versus rollover. A 2013 interagency notice from the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, the Federal Reserve and the FDIC gave bank examiners the vocabulary the market still uses. It defined a leveraged transaction as one where “the borrower's Total Debt divided by EBITDA or Senior Debt divided by EBITDA exceed 4.0X EBITDA or 3.0X EBITDA, respectively”, and told examiners that “generally, a leverage level after planned asset sales in excess of 6X Total Debt/EBITDA raises concerns for most industries”.

That is the band a lower-middle-market recap lives in: comfortably a leveraged transaction at 3.5x, well inside the 6x line. The practical consequence is that the debt is sized against your cash flow, not the fund's, and the company services it whether or not the growth plan lands.

What the second bite is actually worth

The rollover is the whole case for a recap over a clean sale, and it is the part that gets quoted as a certainty. It is not one. Assume the sponsor's $13.9M comes in as preferred equity with an 8% accruing dividend — a common structure, and one that pays the sponsor's capital and its accrual out of any exit before the common splits anything. After five years that preference has grown to roughly $20.4M. Here is the same 30% stake in three outcomes:

Against a rollover booked at $5.9M, the range runs from losing 60% of it to roughly doubling it. The flat case is not a disaster scenario — it is the company performing exactly as well as it does today, with debt paid down out of cash flow. That is what a preference does: it moves the first $20.4M of any outcome away from the common stock, so the owner's rolled stake is a leveraged bet on growth, not a proportional share of the business. The mechanics of rollover equity and the second bite are worth reading line by line before you agree to the percentage.

Recapitalization versus sale: the four differences that decide it

Cash certainty. A full sale at the same 6.8x pays $40.8M, all of it priced today. A recap pays $34.9M today and puts $5.9M on a five-year outcome you no longer control.

Who decides. In a recap the sponsor holds the board. You may keep the CEO title, but the budget, the hiring plan and the timing of the next sale sit with people who answer to a fund's own clock. This is the point owners underestimate most reliably, and it is set by the consent list and board composition rather than the percentage — see what a private equity investor's veto list actually covers.

What happens next. A recap is the beginning of a new owner's plan, not the end of yours. Add-on acquisitions, a new CFO, monthly reporting packs and a management fee against EBITDA are standard; what happens after private equity buys your company is a separate post because it is a separate experience.

Tax timing. A sale is taxed now. A properly structured rollover usually is not — which is the next section, and the one most often described loosely.

Why the rollover is usually deferred, not forgiven

The appeal of a rollover is that you reinvest pre-tax dollars. That depends entirely on how the new holding company is organized.

If the holdco is a corporation, the governing provision is IRC § 351(a): “No gain or loss shall be recognized if property is transferred to a corporation by one or more persons solely in exchange for stock in such corporation and immediately after the exchange such person or persons are in control” — and control there means “at least 80 percent of the total combined voting power” under § 368(c). In a majority recap you plainly do not hold 80% afterward, so the deferral has to come from treating you and the sponsor's contributing entity as one transferor group, which is a drafting exercise with conditions.

That is a large part of why so many of these deals use an LLC taxed as a partnership as the acquisition vehicle. IRC § 721(a) is far less demanding: “No gain or loss shall be recognized to a partnership or to any of its partners in the case of a contribution of property to the partnership in exchange for an interest in the partnership.” No 80% test, no control group to assemble.

Two cautions. Deferral is not forgiveness: the rolled basis carries forward and the tax arrives at the second sale. And the cash portion is taxable at close regardless. Confirm the structure with your own tax advisor against your own facts before you treat the rollover as tax-free in your planning.

When a majority recap fits, and when it does not

It fits when you want the majority of your net worth out of one illiquid asset, you genuinely believe the business is worth materially more in five years than today, and you are willing to run it for someone else while that happens. It fits when the growth plan needs capital and acquisition experience you were not going to buy on your own.

It fits badly when you are close to stopping, because a rollover only pays if you are around for the hold and sponsors price the deal assuming you are. It fits badly when the business is already running at its ceiling, because a flat five years makes the rollover the worst-paid part of the transaction. And it fits badly when what you actually wanted was liquidity without a new boss — in which case the question is whether a structure further down the list serves you better. Frequently asked questions

What is a majority recapitalization? A transaction in which an investor buys more than 50% of a company, funds part of the price with debt the company carries afterward, and leaves the existing owner with a minority stake that pays out at the investor's eventual sale. Owners commonly retain 10–30%.

How is a majority recapitalization different from a sale? Legally it is a sale of control; commercially the difference is that you keep a stake and keep working. You trade some cash certainty today for a second, unguaranteed payment later, and you hand over the board either way.

What is a leveraged recapitalization? A transaction that raises the company's debt to buy back shares or restructure ownership, without necessarily adding a new equity investor. A dividend recapitalization is the narrower version: borrow, distribute the proceeds, change no ownership percentages.

Do I keep control after a majority recap? No. You may keep your title and your day-to-day authority, but the sponsor appoints the board majority and the consent list governs anything significant. If keeping control is the requirement, selling part of your business and keeping control is a different structure with different arithmetic.

Is rollover equity taxed at closing? Usually not, if the vehicle and the paperwork are built for it — § 721 for a partnership vehicle, § 351 for a corporate one. The cash portion is taxed at close in either case, and the deferred gain comes due at the second sale. Confirm your own facts with your tax advisor.

The bottom line

A majority recapitalization suits an owner who wants most of the money now, believes the next five years are the best five years, and does not mind reporting to a board. It suits badly an owner who wanted liquidity and assumed the rollover made the loss of control temporary. The preference, the debt and the sponsor's exit clock decide the second payment, and none of the three is yours to set.

An Independent Buyout starts from the other end of that trade: it uses a leveraged structure to buy some or all of your ownership without putting an outside financial sponsor on your board, so the control question and the liquidity question are not bundled together.
