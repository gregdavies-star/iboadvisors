Majority Recapitalization: How Selling 51-80% Actually Pays

By Michael Chasen · Updated October 1, 2026

The short answer

A majority recapitalization sells control of your company – usually 51% to 80% – to an outside investor while you keep the balance as rollover equity in the recapitalized business. You bank most of the value today, you stay on the cap table, and the person who decides when the company is sold next is no longer you.

It is the most common structure private equity actually offers a lower-middle-market owner, and it is routinely described as "selling part of the company." That description is accurate about the percentage and misleading about the consequence. At 51% the buyer owns the board, the budget, the debt policy, and the exit timetable. The 20-49% you keep is a financial instrument, not a seat at the head of the table. If keeping the steering wheel is the point, a minority recapitalization is the structure you are looking for, and what the percentage actually decides is worth reading before you negotiate either one.

What a majority recapitalization actually is

The mechanics are a leveraged buyout in which the seller is also one of the buyers. A new holding company is formed. It raises debt against the operating company's cash flow, the sponsor writes an equity check, and you contribute part of your existing stake into the new holdco instead of cashing all of it out. The combination buys 100% of the old company. One industry guide puts the standard split plainly: the investor "acquires 51-80% of a company while the owner keeps 20-49% as rollover equity" (CT Acquisitions).

Rollover is no longer an exotic term in the lower middle market – it is close to standard. Drawing on SRS Acquiom deal-points studies, PitchBook commentary and its own buy-side data, CT Acquisitions puts rollover in roughly 70-85% of US lower-middle-market private equity buyouts, against about 55% in 2019, and sizes it at 10-30% of net consideration, rising to 20-30% in the $15M-$50M EBITDA band (CT Acquisitions, founder rollover equity benchmarks). If you are being shown a majority recap, you are being shown the market's default deal, not a bespoke one. What the rollover is really worth is the part of the conversation that gets the least scrutiny and deserves the most.

The capital structure, with real numbers

The company below is invented. These are arithmetic on a hypothetical, not a quote and not a description of any transaction.

Take a business with $5 million of normalized EBITDA and no funded debt. Through the first nine months of 2025, GF Data recorded average purchase multiples of 6.8x EBITDA in the $25M-$50M enterprise value band, with total debt at 4.0x EBITDA and senior debt at 3.4x in that same band (Capital Pad, citing GF Data). Apply them:

  • Enterprise value: 6.8 × $5M = $34.0M
  • New debt raised: 4.0 × $5M = $20.0M
  • Total equity in the new holdco: $34.0M − $20.0M = $14.0M
  • Your rollover at 25%: $3.5M, leaving the sponsor to fund $10.5M
  • Your cash at close: $34.0M − $3.5M = $30.5M, before fees, escrow and tax

Two things in that list are easy to skip past. The first is that the company you now own a quarter of carries $20 million of debt it did not have a week ago – debt raised to pay you. The second is that your $3.5M is the most junior money in the structure. It sits behind $20M of lenders and, in most deals, behind the sponsor's own preferred return. How private equity finances a buyout walks the full capital stack and explains where preferred stock changes the waterfall. Before you accept anyone's multiple, run your own EBITDA through the business valuation calculator so the negotiation starts from a number you built.

What the rollover is actually worth

The second bite is sold as upside. It is upside multiplied by leverage, which means it is also downside multiplied by leverage. Hold the exit five years out – reasonable, given that Bain reports global buyout assets sold in 2025 had been held around seven years on average and With Intelligence puts median hold at exit at 5.4 years in 2024 (Capital Pad) – and run three cases on the same $3.5M stake.

Year-5 case EBITDA Exit multiple Enterprise value Net debt Equity value Your 25%
Plan hits $8.0M 6.8x $54.4M $10.0M $44.4M $11.1M
Flat, multiple slips $5.0M 6.0x $30.0M $14.0M $16.0M $4.0M
Down year, multiple slips $4.0M 6.0x $24.0M $16.0M $8.0M $2.0M

A $3.5M stake that returns somewhere between $2.0M and $11.1M is not a bonus. It is a concentrated, illiquid, levered bet on a company you no longer run, held for a period you do not control. The plan case is genuinely good – $30.5M banked plus $11.1M later beats $34.0M today by a wide margin even after tax. The flat case is roughly break-even on money that sat still for five years. Judge the structure on the middle row, not the top one, and note that the $30.5M you banked at close would have been earning something in the meantime too.

Recapitalization vs sale: the comparison that matters

Owners usually frame this as recap versus sale. The more useful frame is how much control and how much cash each structure moves, because every route trades one against the other.

Outright sale Majority recap (75/25) Minority recap (30%)
Cash at close ~$34.0M ~$30.5M ~$10.2M
What you still own Nothing 25% of a levered holdco 70% of the company
Who controls the board The buyer The sponsor You, subject to consent rights
Who decides when it sells next Not your problem The sponsor's fund clock Negotiated, usually with a put or drag
Your role afterwards Transition, then out Usually asked to stay 3-5 years Keep running it

The row that surprises people is the fourth. In a majority recap you have not delayed the sale of your company; you have handed its timing to someone whose fund has a defined life. What happens after a private equity buyout covers the reporting cadence, the value-creation plan and the governance that arrives with the wire. The pros and cons of selling to private equity weighs the whole trade on the same kind of numbers.

Leveraged recapitalization: the same word, a different deal

"Recapitalization" describes any change to the mix of debt and equity on a balance sheet, so the word covers transactions with very different consequences.

  • Majority recap: a new controlling owner arrives; you roll a minority stake. Control changes hands.
  • Minority recap: an investor buys 20-40%; you keep the majority and the board. Control does not change, but consent rights do.
  • Leveraged recap: the company borrows and uses the proceeds to repurchase equity. Ownership concentrates among whoever does not sell.
  • Dividend recap: a leveraged recap where the borrowed money is distributed rather than used to buy shares. No equity changes hands at all.

The last two are the only ways on this list to take cash out without adding a partner, and the price is that you keep every unit of risk you had plus the covenants. If the attraction of a majority recap is the cash rather than the partner, a leveraged recap is the cheaper comparison – and the full list of alternatives to selling to private equity sets both against the strategic sale, the management buyout and the ESOP.

The tax question, and the trap underneath it

Rollover equity is attractive partly because it is usually not a taxable event on the rolled portion. Where the new holdco is a partnership or LLC taxed as one, the governing rule is blunt: "No gain or loss shall be recognized to a partnership or to any of its partners in the case of a contribution of property to the partnership in exchange for an interest in the partnership" (26 U.S. Code § 721(a), with an exception in § 721(b) for transfers to what would be an investment company). You pay tax on the cash you took and defer on the equity you kept. Whether your deal qualifies depends entirely on how the holdco is formed and what you contribute, which is a question for your tax counsel before the letter of intent, not after.

The trap is on the company's side of the ledger. Under 26 U.S. Code § 163(j), the deduction for business interest is capped at business interest income plus 30% of adjusted taxable income plus floor plan financing interest, with disallowed interest carried forward. For tax years beginning after 2021, adjusted taxable income is computed without adding back depreciation, amortization or depletion – so the 30% test runs against something closer to EBIT than EBITDA. On a capital-intensive business, that can leave part of the interest on the $20M of new debt non-deductible, which raises the cash tax bill of the company whose equity you just rolled into. Smaller taxpayers meeting the § 448(c) gross receipts test are exempt; most companies at this size are not.

Who a majority recap fits

It fits the owner who wants most of the money now, genuinely wants an institutional partner, has a management team that can absorb a board and a reporting pack, and is comfortable that someone else will choose the next buyer. It fits particularly well where the growth plan needs capital and acquisitions that the company cannot self-fund, because that is exactly what a sponsor is good at. How private equity values a company explains what makes a business worth a premium multiple in that model.

It fits badly where the owner's real objective is to stay in charge. Selling 51% to keep 49% is not a compromise between selling and not selling; it is a sale with a retained investment attached. A minority stake, even a large one, comes with a list of vetoes rather than a vote that decides anything.

Frequently asked questions

What is a majority recapitalization? A transaction in which an investor acquires a controlling stake – typically 51% to 80% – funded with new debt on the company plus an equity check, while the seller rolls the remaining 20% to 49% into the recapitalized business.

Is a recapitalization better than a sale? It depends on which number you are optimizing. An outright sale pays more cash at close and ends your exposure. A majority recap pays slightly less today and adds a levered second bite whose value, on the worked example above, ranged from $2.0M to $11.1M on a $3.5M stake.

How much cash do I actually receive? Enterprise value less the portion you roll, then less fees, escrow or representation-and-warranty insurance, any working-capital adjustment, and tax on the cashed-out share. In the example, a $34.0M enterprise value produced $30.5M before those deductions.

Do I keep running the company after a majority recap? Usually for a while. Sponsors typically want the owner or senior management through a three-to-five-year plan. You keep the operating role; you lose the deciding vote.

Is rollover equity taxed at closing? Generally not on the rolled portion if the structure qualifies – § 721 for partnership rollovers – but qualification is structural and specific, so price it with your own tax advisor.

What is the difference between a majority recap and a leveraged recap? A majority recap brings in a new controlling owner. A leveraged recap uses borrowed money to buy back equity without introducing one, so you keep control and take on the covenants instead.

The bottom line

A majority recapitalization is a good structure honestly described and a bad one when it is sold as "keeping a piece." You are selling the company and reinvesting part of the proceeds into a levered version of it, run by someone else, on their schedule. If the numbers in the middle row of that table are acceptable to you, it is a sound deal. If they are not, the question is not how to negotiate a better rollover – it is whether you need an outside owner at all. An Independent Buyout sizes the financing the way a sponsor would but leaves no fund on the cap table, and the side-by-side arithmetic against a sponsor deal shows where the two diverge.
