Majority Recapitalization: Selling Control, Staying In

By Michael Chasen · Updated October 13, 2026

A majority recapitalization is the sale of 51% to 80% of your company's equity to an outside investor - usually a private equity fund - funded largely with debt placed on the business, in which you keep the remaining minority stake and, most of the time, keep running the company. Control transfers at close. The word "recapitalization" is doing a lot of work in that sentence, and the first job of this piece is to take it back out.

Owners hear "recap" and think financing event: new money in, balance sheet restructured, same people in charge. That is a fair description of a minority recapitalization, where an investor buys 20% to 49% and you keep the votes. At 51% it is a control sale with a staged payout. Nothing about the label changes who appoints the board the morning after closing.

Majority recap, minority recap, leveraged recap, full sale

Four structures get called recapitalizations in the same conversation, and they differ on two axes only: who controls the company afterwards, and whether a new owner joins the cap table at all.

Structure What the owner sells Who controls the company after
Minority recapitalization 20-49% of the equity to a new investor You, subject to a negotiated consent list
Leveraged recapitalization Nothing - the company borrows and pays a dividend You, subject to the lender's covenants
Majority recapitalization 51-80%, with the balance rolled into the new company The investor, through the board
Full sale 100%, usually with an escrow and sometimes an earnout The buyer, and you are gone

A majority recap sits one step from a full sale, not one step from a minority one. The useful question is not "how much am I selling" but "what does the 20-49% I keep turn into" - and that is an arithmetic question with a surprisingly wide range of answers.

The arithmetic on an invented company

Take a business with $5 million of normalized EBITDA and no funded debt. These are made-up numbers for a made-up company, not a quote and not a description of any transaction.

Through the first nine months of 2025, deals in the $25-50 million enterprise value band priced at an average of 6.8x EBITDA and carried total debt of 4.0x EBITDA, with 3.4x of that senior (GF Data figures compiled by Capital Pad). Use both. At 6.8x the company is worth $34 million. At 4.0x the buyer puts $20 million of new debt on it, which leaves $14 million of equity to fund the $34 million purchase.

You roll 25%. Here is the first thing that surprises people: 25% of the equity is $3.5 million, not 25% of $34 million. The rollover is a quarter of a leveraged equity stub, because the debt sits ahead of it. Your cash at close is $34 million less the $3.5 million you rolled - about $30.5 million before fees, escrow and tax. The investor funds the other $10.5 million of equity.

Now the second bite, five years out, under three futures. In each one the company is sold again at the same 6.8x, so the only things moving are EBITDA and how much debt got repaid. Assume the investor's $10.5 million went in as preferred equity with an 8% accruing dividend plus 75% of the common - a standard structure, and the single most important term in the document.

Year 5 EBITDA grows to $7.5M EBITDA flat at $5M EBITDA falls to $4M
Enterprise value at 6.8x $51.0M $34.0M $27.2M
Debt remaining $10.0M $14.0M $16.0M
Equity value $41.0M $20.0M $11.2M
Preferred paid first (8% accrued) $15.4M $15.4M $11.2M (all of it)
Common left to split $25.6M $4.6M $0
Your 25% of the common $6.4M $1.2M $0

Three observations. The upside case returns 1.8x on the rolled $3.5 million over five years, which is a good outcome and a fraction of what the owner would have made owning 100% of the same growth. The flat case destroys two thirds of the rolled value even though the company is worth exactly what it was worth at close - that is the preference, not the business. And the down case wipes the rollover out entirely while the investor still recovers most of its money, which is precisely what a liquidation preference is designed to do.

Run the same three columns without the preference - the investor's $10.5 million in common alongside yours - and the flat case pays your 25% about $5 million instead of $1.2 million. Same company, same price, same percentage. The term sheet did that, and it is the line to negotiate hardest. The mechanics of the rolled stake are worth understanding on their own; the second bite is real money and it is not a sure thing.

What actually transfers at close

The percentage is the headline and the governing documents are the deal. At 51% or more, the investor appoints the board majority, and the board - not the shareholder meeting, and not you - directs the company. Expect, in roughly this order of impact on your working life:

  • A board you no longer control, usually three investor seats, one or two management seats, sometimes an independent. Decisions you used to make in a hallway become agenda items.
  • A reserved-matters list covering budget approval, capital expenditure above a threshold, hiring and firing senior management, new debt, acquisitions and any sale. These are the same consent rights a minority investor negotiates, except now they sit with the party that also holds the votes.
  • An employment agreement for you, with a term, a non-compete and a definition of cause. You are now an executive of a company you used to own, and the agreement is the only thing that says what your job is.
  • Monthly reporting on the investor's calendar, a budget defended annually, and a lender who gets the same package. What changes after a buyout is mostly cadence and accountability, and it starts in week one.
  • A hold period you do not set. The fund has its own clock, typically five to seven years, and your remaining stake is sold when the fund sells. Drag-along rights make that mandatory.

None of this is hidden - it is all in the documents, and every item is negotiable at the margin. The error is treating the governance package as paperwork that follows the price, when for most owners it decides whether the next five years are tolerable.

The tax treatment of the rolled stake

The cash portion is taxable in the year you receive it. The rolled portion is usually structured so that it is not, by contributing your retained equity to the new holding company rather than selling it. Where that holding company is a partnership or LLC, the governing provision is 26 U.S. Code § 721, which says that "no gain or loss shall be recognized to a partnership or to any of its partners" on a contribution of property in exchange for a partnership interest (Cornell Law School, 26 U.S. Code § 721).

Two cautions. The deferral is a feature of a particular structure, not of rollovers generally - it has to be built, and buyers sometimes propose structures where part of the rollover is taxable at close. And deferral is not forgiveness: the tax arrives at the second bite, on a stake whose value the table above shows is anything but certain. Price the rollover as pre-tax and at risk, then ask your tax advisor what the structure in front of you actually does.

When a majority recap is the right answer

It fits an owner who wants most of their net worth out of one illiquid asset now, has a real use for a partner with capital behind acquisitions or expansion, and is genuinely willing to report to a board for five years. Those three have to be true together. The structure also suits a company whose growth plan needs money the owner will not personally guarantee.

It fits badly when what the owner actually wants is liquidity without a boss. That is a different transaction, and there are several: a minority recap, a dividend recap, a staged sale to management, or a partial sale structured to keep control. The worst version of a majority recap is the one an owner signs because the process produced only one kind of buyer - which is a function of who ran the process. Before any of it, get an independent read on the number: the valuation calculator gives a sector-adjusted range, and how a fund actually values a company explains the adjustments a buyer will argue about.

Majority recap versus an Independent Buyout

Both get the owner a large cash payout and leave the company running. They differ on who ends up holding the keys. In a majority recap, an outside fund holds control and a preference, and your remaining stake is exposed to both. In an Independent Buyout, the buyer is built around the company's existing leadership, the business stays independently run, and there is no fund clock setting the exit date. The right comparison is not which one is better in the abstract but which one prices better for your specific company after governance and the preference are priced in - the same discipline the pros and cons of selling to private equity demands of any term sheet.

Frequently asked questions

Is a majority recapitalization the same as selling my company? Legally it is a sale of control, with part of the consideration taken in equity of the new company instead of cash. You keep a stake and usually your job; you do not keep the final say.

How much of a majority recap is paid in cash at close? Whatever percentage you sell, less fees, escrow and tax. On the invented $5 million EBITDA company above - $34 million of value, 25% rolled - that is roughly $30.5 million gross, before those deductions.

Can I negotiate the liquidation preference? It is one of the few terms where the answer meaningfully changes the outcome, as the three columns above show. A 1x non-participating preference, a cap on accrual, or straight common all exist in real documents.

Does the debt go on my company or the buyer's balance sheet? On the company. The acquisition debt is serviced by the business you still work at, which is why the 4.0x figure above matters as much as the 6.8x one.
