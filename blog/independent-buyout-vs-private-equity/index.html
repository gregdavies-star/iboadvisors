Independent Buyout vs. Private Equity: How Owners Keep Control and Capture More Value

By Michael Chasen · Updated August 27, 2026

Private equity doesn't buy your company. They take a mortgage out on it.

That's the part most owners don't fully understand until they're already at the closing table. A PE fund shows up with a term sheet, a headline valuation, and a story about "partnering" with you for the next chapter. What actually happens is closer to a leveraged purchase: the fund puts debt on your business, uses your own cash flow to service it, and keeps a controlling say in how the company is run until they exit. An Independent Buyout (IBO) is built to get you a similar liquidity event - without handing that mortgage, or the control that comes with it, to an outside fund.

Here's the head-to-head, on the five things that actually matter: who the buyer is, how the deal is financed, who controls the company afterward, how the sale is taxed, and how long it takes to actually get your money.

Who the buyer actually is

In a private equity sale, the buyer is an outside financial sponsor - a fund raising capital from its own limited partners, with a mandate to generate a return within a defined hold period, typically three to seven years, before selling again (Nate Lind, "How to Evaluate a Private Equity Rollover vs. an All-Cash Sale"). Their incentives are set by their own investors, not by what's good for your company or your team over the long run.

In an Independent Buyout, the buyer is internal: the leadership team that already runs the business day to day. There's no fund, no outside investment committee, and no exit clock ticking in the background dictating decisions.

How the deal is financed

A private equity firm typically buys 60 to 80% of a business for cash today, using debt placed on the company itself, and asks the seller to roll the remaining 20 to 40% into the new deal as equity - what's often called the "second bite of the apple" (Nate Lind; GF Data via GP Ventures). Rollover shows up in roughly 29% of deals tracked by GF Data, and market surveys put the typical rollover range at 20-40% of deal value (GP Ventures). That rollover is real skin still in the game - you don't get that piece until the fund exits again, years later, and there's no guarantee the second exit is as clean as the first.

An Independent Buyout is financed against the business's own value using a structure that's existed in the federal tax code since 1984 - Internal Revenue Code Section 1042 - rather than through outside fund capital or a large personal loan from the leadership team (26 U.S. Code § 1042, Cornell Law School). The financing comes from the company you built, not from a fund that now owns a piece of it.

Who controls the company after close

This is where the mortgage analogy earns its keep. Even when a PE fund holds a minority stake, it frequently negotiates board seats and veto rights over major decisions - capital raises, add-on acquisitions, executive hires, budget approval (Montague Law, "VC Portfolio Company Governance"; LawGratis, "Private Equity Governance in UK Portfolio Companies"). You may still be running the business operationally, but you're now answering to a board seat that didn't exist a year ago, on decisions you used to make alone.

In an Independent Buyout, the existing leadership team keeps running the company - no outside board seat, no outside veto. That's the whole point of the structure: it's designed so leadership can Retain Leadership over the decisions that matter, instead of ceding that authority to a financial sponsor whose fund has its own three-to-seven-year clock.

How the sale is taxed

A standard sale to a private equity fund is a taxable event. The seller owes capital gains tax on the proceeds in the year of the sale, same as any other stock or asset sale.

An Independent Buyout can be structured for materially different tax treatment. Depending on how the deal is built and the seller's specific facts, an Independent Buyout can be structured to let an owner sell some or all of their company tax free. This isn't a blanket guarantee - every situation is different, and this should be confirmed with a tax advisor before assuming a specific outcome for your business.

Timeline to full liquidity

In a PE deal, you get a cash payment at close for the majority stake, then wait out the fund's hold period - often three to seven years - before your rollover equity converts to cash at the second exit, and even then only if that second sale actually clears at a good multiple (Nate Lind). On top of that, roughly 18% of private-target deals now include an earn-out - a portion of the purchase price contingent on the business hitting performance targets after closing (2025 ABA Private Target Mergers and Acquisitions Deal Points Study, via Iconic) - which means part of your "sale price" isn't actually yours until you clear someone else's hurdles.

An Independent Buyout is structured up front. The liquidity terms are set at closing, not contingent on a fund-controlled second sale that may or may not happen on your timeline.

Why you haven't heard about this option before

Most advisors and brokers get paid by the deals they broker - private equity and strategic-buyer transactions. Nobody in that circle gets paid to show you Door 3. An Independent Buyout isn't a fringe idea; it's a financing mechanism that's been sitting in the tax code for four decades, applicable to owners with $3 million or more in EBITDA and a leadership team capable of running the business without the owner in the room every day.

Frequently asked questions

Is an Independent Buyout slower or riskier than a private equity sale? Not inherently. The liquidity terms in an IBO are set at closing, the same as most of a PE deal's upfront cash - the difference is that an IBO doesn't tie a second chunk of your proceeds to a fund's multi-year hold period and eventual re-sale.

Do I have to sell 100% of the company in an Independent Buyout? No. An Independent Buyout can be structured to let an owner sell some or all of their company, depending on how much liquidity is wanted now versus how much upside the owner wants to keep going forward.

Does the leadership team need to put up their own cash? No, and that's a key difference from a conventional management buyout. The purchase is financed against the value of the business itself, not through large personal loans from the leadership team.

Is the sale really tax-free? It can be structured that way depending on the seller's specific facts and how the deal is built. This isn't automatic - confirm your specific situation with a tax advisor before assuming any particular outcome.

What size company actually qualifies? IBO Advisors generally works with companies at $3 million or more in EBITDA, evaluated individually based on the business's specific facts and leadership bench strength.

The bottom line

Private equity and an Independent Buyout can produce a similar-looking number at close. What happens after close is where they diverge completely - who holds the debt, who sits on the board, how much of your proceeds are locked up in someone else's hold period, and what you owe the IRS. An IBO is built to get you a private equity outcome without the private equity firm: real liquidity, leadership retained, terms set up front.
