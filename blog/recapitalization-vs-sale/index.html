Recapitalization vs. Sale: Majority Recaps, Leveraged Recaps, and What Each One Pays

By Michael Chasen · Updated October 2, 2026

The short answer

A sale changes who owns your company. A recapitalization changes what your company owes and who owns which slice of it - and it can pay you most of the value of the business without transferring the whole thing. On the same company, an outright sale pays the most cash and ends your involvement; a majority recapitalization pays nearly as much and leaves you with a minority stake and a new board; a leveraged recapitalization pays the least cash and leaves you with the company, the covenants, and nobody to answer to.

Which one is right turns on three numbers you can calculate before you take a meeting: how much debt your cash flow can carry, how much of the proceeds you need in hand rather than on paper, and what the equity you keep is worth once the debt sits on top of it. The arithmetic below runs all three routes on one invented company.

What "recapitalization" actually means

The word gets used loosely, which is part of why owners cannot tell what they are being offered. Internal Revenue Code § 368(a)(1) lists seven kinds of corporate "reorganization", and subparagraph (E) is simply "a recapitalization" (Cornell Law School, 26 U.S. Code § 368). No definition follows. In practice it means rearranging the right-hand side of the balance sheet - equity for debt, one class of stock for another, one set of shareholders for another.

Commercially, four different transactions travel under the name, and they pay very differently:

  • Minority recapitalization. An outside investor buys 20-49% for cash. You keep majority ownership and, subject to the consent list, keep running the company. The mechanics are in minority recapitalization explained.
  • Majority recapitalization. An investor buys 51-80%, you roll the rest. This is what most private equity "recaps" are. You get most of the cash and lose control of the board.
  • Leveraged recapitalization. The company borrows against its own cash flow and uses the money to buy back shares from you. No new shareholder appears. Also called a debt recap.
  • Dividend recapitalization. The same borrowing, distributed to you as a dividend rather than used to redeem stock. Economically close to a leveraged recap; taxed differently, as the section below explains. It is route six in the seven alternatives to selling to private equity.

Only the first two bring in a partner. The last two are financing transactions that happen to pay you - and the ones almost nobody puts in front of an owner, because no equity changes hands and so no one earns an equity fee.

The arithmetic on one $6 million EBITDA company

The numbers below describe an invented company. They are arithmetic on a hypothetical - not a quote, a projection, or a description of any transaction.

Take a business with $6 million of normalized EBITDA and no funded debt. GF Data's figures for the first nine months of 2025 put the $25M-$50M enterprise value band at 6.8x EBITDA, with total debt of 4.0x EBITDA and senior debt of 3.4x in that same band (Capital Pad, citing GF Data). So the company prices at $40.8 million, lenders will support roughly $24.0 million of total debt in a sponsored structure, and about $20.4 million of senior debt without one. Run your own range first in the business valuation calculator, and get to a defensible normalized EBITDA figure before you trust any multiple.

Route A: an outright sale of 100%

You sell every share for $40.8 million. The buyer funds it with roughly $24.0 million of debt and $16.8 million of equity - and if it is a sponsor, the debt lands on your former company, not on the fund. No further stake, no covenants, no second payment to wait for.

Route B: a majority recapitalization at 70/30

Here is the part owners consistently get wrong. "Selling 70%" does not mean receiving 70% of the value.

The buyer acquires the company at the same $40.8 million, funded with $24.0 million of debt and $16.8 million of equity. You roll 30% of that equity - $5.04 million - and the sponsor writes a cheque for the other 70%, $11.76 million. Add it up: $24.0 million of debt plus $11.76 million of sponsor equity plus your $5.04 million rollover equals the $40.8 million price.

Your cash at close is the price less your rollover: $35.76 million, or 88% of the enterprise value, for a transaction that left you owning 30% of the company. The debt, not the sponsor's equity, is what buys you out. What you now own is 30% of a business carrying $24.0 million of borrowings, and what happens to rollover equity over a hold period is the single most misjudged line in this structure. You also inherit a board: even a minority holder negotiates vetoes over budgets, debt, hires and the timing of the next sale, and at 70% the list is not a negotiation.

Route C: a leveraged recapitalization with no new shareholder

Without a sponsor sitting behind the credit, lenders generally stop at senior capacity - 3.4x in this band, or $20.4 million. The company borrows it and redeems that much of your stock. You take $20.4 million in cash and still own 100% of the equity, which is now worth the $40.8 million enterprise value less the $20.4 million of new debt: about $20.4 million.

No partner, no consent list, no clock. In exchange you are running a leveraged company. At an illustrative 9% blended cost - a rate to get quoted rather than assumed - $20.4 million of debt costs about $1.84 million a year, roughly 31% of EBITDA leaving before tax, capex or working capital. A soft year that would have been merely disappointing becomes a covenant conversation.

The tax rule that decides a leveraged recap: § 302

When a company hands money to a shareholder in exchange for stock, the default assumption is that the shareholder has sold something. The code does not agree. Under 26 U.S. Code § 302, a redemption is treated as a sale or exchange only if it clears one of four tests - and the one that matters in a recap is § 302(b)(2), the "substantially disproportionate" test. It requires that after the redemption your voting stock ratio be less than 80% of what it was before, and that you own less than 50% of the total combined voting power. The other three routes to exchange treatment are a redemption "not essentially equivalent to a dividend", a complete termination of your interest, or a partial liquidation.

Now apply that to Route C. A sole shareholder who owns 100% before the redemption and 100% after is at 100% of their prior ratio - nowhere near below 80% - and still holds every vote. All four tests fail. For a C corporation that is the whole ballgame: the $20.4 million is a distribution, taxed as a dividend to the extent of earnings and profits, with no offset for your basis in the shares.

Change the entity and the answer changes. For an S corporation with no accumulated earnings and profits, 26 U.S. Code § 1368(b) provides that a distribution "shall not be included in gross income to the extent that it does not exceed the adjusted basis of the stock", and any excess "shall be treated as gain from the sale or exchange of property". You recover basis tax-free first, then take capital gain. Same $20.4 million, materially different after-tax result, decided by a form you filed years ago. Price a recap with your own adviser and your actual basis schedule before you price it with a lender.

When a recapitalization beats a sale

The case for a recap is rarely about price. It is about what happens to the part you keep, and about timing you control.

A majority recap asks you to leave $5.04 million on the table in exchange for a second payment at the sponsor's exit. That exit has been getting further away. Bain & Company's 2026 outlook reports that "holding periods at exit are hovering around seven years, up from an average of five to six years from 2010 to 2021", against a backlog of roughly 32,000 unsold portfolio companies worth about $3.8 trillion and distributions stuck at 14% of net asset value, below 15% for four straight years. Dry powder of $1.3 trillion is why the offers keep coming. Your rolled 30% is a claim on an exit the industry is struggling to produce.

So the question resolves roughly like this. If you want out and the price clears what you need, sell - weighed honestly against the pros and cons of selling to private equity. If you want liquidity and intend to keep building, a recap is the right family of structures, and the only real question left is whether you will accept a partner to get a bigger cheque. If not, the leveraged route pays less and costs you no control. If so, read the governance schedule before the valuation: how much you can sell and still control the company is answered by the shareholders' agreement, not the cap table.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a recapitalization and a sale? A sale transfers ownership of the business. A recapitalization changes the company's capital structure - the mix of debt and equity and who holds each - and pays you out of the proceeds. The distinguishing feature is that the company continues under substantially the same management with a different balance sheet.

What is a majority recapitalization? A transaction in which an investor acquires 51-80% of the equity and the owner rolls the balance. On a $40.8 million company with 4.0x of debt capacity, a 70/30 majority recap pays about $35.76 million of cash at close and leaves the owner with 30% of a company carrying $24.0 million of borrowings.

Is a leveraged recapitalization taxable? Usually yes, and the character depends on your entity. A redemption that fails all four tests in § 302(b) is treated as a distribution rather than a sale, which for a C corporation means dividend treatment with no basis offset. An S corporation with no accumulated earnings and profits recovers basis first under § 1368(b) and takes capital gain above it.

The bottom line

Recapitalization versus sale is not a choice between cash and no cash. All three routes pay. They differ in how much arrives at close, what you are left holding, and who decides when the rest of your money becomes real. On a $40.8 million company the spread between the most cash and the least is $20.4 million - and the route that pays the least is the only one that leaves you in charge afterwards.

There is a fourth route that does not appear in most of these conversations. An Independent Buyout finances the purchase the way a sponsor would size a leveraged buyout, but makes your own leadership team the buyer through a trust - so the liquidity resembles a majority recap while no outside fund ends up holding the consent list or the exit clock.
