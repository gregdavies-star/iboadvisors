Does Private Equity Replace the CEO After a Buyout?

By Michael Chasen · Updated November 18, 2026

Usually, yes — but not at closing, and not because you did anything wrong. In the largest study of the question, 71% of private-equity-owned companies hired a new chief executive at some point under that ownership, and more than three-quarters of the replacements were hired from outside the company. The decision almost never happens in the first few months. It happens somewhere between the first missed budget and the second year.

If you are weighing a sale and you intend to keep running the business, this is the term that matters most and is written down least. Nothing in the purchase agreement says the sponsor will replace you. Nothing needs to.

What the deal records actually show

Most of what gets written about this is survey opinion. The exception is Gompers, Kaplan and Mukharlyamov, “The Market for CEOs: Evidence from Private Equity” (NBER working paper 30899, January 2023), which traced actual appointments rather than asking people to estimate. Three findings carry:

  • 71% of the companies hired new CEOs under private equity ownership.
  • More than 75% of those new CEOs were external hires, and 67% were, in the authors’ phrase, complete outsiders — no prior role as a director or executive of the business.
  • The contrast is with large public companies, where 72% of new CEOs are internal promotions. Private equity does the opposite of what the S&P 500 does.

Read the caveat before you panic. The sample is US buyouts with enterprise value above $1 billion, completed between 2010 and 2016, followed to the end of 2021 (as summarised by Adastrum Consulting, October 2026). Those are not $5M-EBITDA companies, and a founder-owned business is not a corporate carve-out. But the direction of travel is the same at every size, and the authors’ conclusion is the uncomfortable part: they read the pattern as evidence that firm-specific human capital is relatively unimportant to a private equity owner. Everything you know about your customers, your people and your market is, in their framing, worth less to the buyer than general experience running a larger company.

The survey data points the same way. AlixPartners’ 11th annual Private Equity Leadership Survey, published 25 March 2026 across 427 respondents — 174 from private equity firms and 253 portfolio company executives — found that 65% of private equity firms report CEO turnover during the holding period, and that only 9% say their firms rarely replace CEOs. On the other side of the table, 38% of portfolio company executives said they worry about losing their jobs.

When it happens: almost never at close

An older AlixPartners survey — 104 respondents, reported in August 2017 — put rough timings on it: around 15% of CEOs depart soon after the acquisition, 37% have gone by the end of year one, more than half by year two, and 73% by the time the sponsor exits (reported by Consultancy.uk, 21 August 2017). Those are practitioners’ estimates rather than deal records, and they are nine years old, so treat the shape rather than the decimals. The shape has not changed.

Why the delay? Three reasons, and none of them is sentiment.

The sponsor underwrote the plan with you in the chair. The investment committee memo that approved the price assumed continuity. Replacing the CEO in month two is an admission that the memo was wrong.

The first six months are about plumbing. A monthly reporting pack, a thirteen-week cash flow, a lender covenant calculation, an integration playbook for the first add-on. Nobody changes the driver while the dashboard is being installed.

You are still being paid to stay. An earnout, a rolled stake and an unvested incentive plan all work as retention. They are also, as the table further down shows, the thing that makes leaving expensive.

The real decision point is the second or third board meeting at which the numbers miss the plan. At that meeting there is a management problem to solve and exactly one lever the board controls directly.

Why the board is the mechanism, not the shareholding

Owners tend to argue this in terms of percentages — “I’m rolling 20%, they can’t just fire me.” They can. The chief executive is appointed and removed by the board, and in a control sale the buyer appoints the board. Your 20% buys you economics, not a vote on who runs the company. That separation is the single most misread feature of a sponsored deal, and it is the same mechanism described in what a minority investor’s board seats and veto rights actually do — except that in a majority sale it runs entirely one way.

Two different documents decide two different things, and conflating them is how owners get surprised:

DocumentWhat it decidesWhat it does not decide
Shareholders’ agreementWhat your rolled stake is worth, when it can be sold, whether you are dragged along, what you must consent toWhether you keep your job
Employment agreementYour title, term, salary, severance, non-compete, and the definition of “cause”What your equity is worth
Incentive plan and leaver provisionsHow much of your upside survives a departure, and at what price unvested equity is bought backWhether the board needs a reason

The leverage put on the business at close is what turns a judgement call into a scheduled one. A company carrying bank debt has covenant tests every quarter, and a covenant test is a calendar entry at which someone must explain a variance. How private equity actually finances a buyout walks through the capital stack that creates those dates.

What a replacement costs you: a worked example

Invented company, invented numbers. $6,000,000 of adjusted EBITDA, sold at 7.0x for a $42,000,000 enterprise value, debt-free and cash-free. The owner takes 80% in cash and rolls 20%. The sponsor funds the purchase with $21,000,000 of debt and $21,000,000 of equity, so the rolled $8,400,000 buys 40% of the new equity. A management incentive plan is carved out at 10% of the equity; the owner-CEO is granted 4%, vesting in five equal annual tranches. Six years later the company’s equity is worth $45,000,000.

Now run three endings. The owner stays to the exit; the owner is replaced in month 20 and classified a good leaver; the owner is replaced in month 20 and classified a bad leaver, with the shares called at original cost.

LineStays to exitReplaced, good leaverReplaced, bad leaver
Cash at close$33,600,000$33,600,000$33,600,000

Two things fall out of that table, and they are not the ones owners expect.

Losing the job is cheap. Losing the classification is not. Being replaced as a good leaver costs $318,000 against staying for six more years — less than a year of salary. Being replaced as a bad leaver costs $8,442,000. The entire exposure sits in the definition of “cause” and in whether the call price is cost or fair market value. That is a drafting negotiation, conducted in the last two weeks before signing, usually while everyone is arguing about the working capital peg.

(Note what the rolled 40% became: after the 10% incentive pool, 36%. Dilution for management equity is normal and is rarely flagged at signing. The rolled stake also carries whatever preferred return sits above it — see how rollover equity and the second bite really work.)

The hold length decides whether any of this matters. Six years is roughly where completed exits now sit, and the private equity holding period has been stretching, which means a founder who leaves in year two can spend another four years as a minority shareholder in a company run by someone the board chose. If you want to sanity-check the entry multiple the whole exercise hangs on, the business valuation calculator gives a sector-adjusted starting range.

Why founders specifically

It is rarely performance in the ordinary sense. The job changes underneath the person holding it.

A founder-run company at $6,000,000 of EBITDA typically runs on the founder’s judgement, relationships and memory. Eighteen months after a buyout, the same company has a lender, a monthly board pack, a budget variance process, a bolt-on acquisition pipeline and a five-year plan it is being measured against quarterly. Those are different jobs. Someone who is excellent at the first is not automatically good at the second, and more to the point, the sponsor has a roster of people who have demonstrably done the second at a company twice the size.

Three specific triggers show up again and again:

  • The first integration. Add-on acquisitions are where most sponsor value-creation plans live, and integrating an acquired business is a skill most founders have never had to use.
  • The reporting demand. A board that wants a variance explanation inside five working days will replace a CEO who takes three weeks, regardless of how the company is actually performing.
  • The second-tier bench. If the CFO and the head of sales both report into the founder’s head rather than into a process, the sponsor will fix that — and the fix usually starts at the top.

None of that is an argument that the founder was bad at the job. It is an argument that the buyer bought a different job and is staffing it accordingly. What happens after private equity buys your company covers the rest of the operating changes that arrive in the same window.

What to settle before you sign

  • An exhaustive “cause” definition with a cure period. Not “conduct detrimental to the company”. A closed list, written offences only, 30 days to cure anything curable, and a board supermajority to invoke it.
  • Leaver classification tied to that definition. If you are not terminated for cause as defined, you are a good leaver. Remove board discretion from the sentence entirely.
  • Fair market value, never cost. Any call on your shares should be priced off an independent valuation, with a defined process for disputing it.
  • Acceleration on a termination without cause. At minimum, the tranche you are inside. Full acceleration if the termination happens within twelve months of a sale.
  • Information rights that outlive your employment. Quarterly financials and notice of a sale process for as long as you hold equity. Without this you will learn about your own exit from a letter.
  • A written 100-day plan naming who decides what. Most unplanned departures start as an unresolved argument about authority that nobody wrote down.

Frequently asked questions

Does private equity always replace the CEO?
No, but it is the base case rather than the exception. 71% of companies in the largest deal-record study hired a new CEO under private equity ownership, and only 9% of private equity firms told AlixPartners they rarely replace CEOs.

Will they replace me at closing?
Almost never. The older AlixPartners survey put early departures at around 15%, rising to 37% by the end of year one. The decision usually follows a missed plan, not the signing.

Does rolling equity protect my job?
No. Shares and employment are separate contracts. A rolled stake affects what you are paid; the employment agreement and the board decide whether you stay.

Is it different in a minority deal?
Materially, yes — the board is not the buyer’s to appoint. But a minority recapitalisation still carries a consent list and often a board seat, and the removal right can be negotiated into it. Read the consent schedule, not the ownership percentage.

What if I want to leave?
Then the same leaver provisions decide what your equity is worth, which is why the drafting matters whichever direction the departure comes from. Resigning without “good reason” is frequently a bad-leaver event.

The bottom line

The question owners ask is “will they replace me?” The better question is “what does it cost me if they do?” — because the answer to the first one is probably, eventually, yes, and the answer to the second is entirely within your control at the drafting table. In the example above the job itself was worth $318,000. The classification was worth $8,442,000.

Negotiate the cause definition, the leaver mechanics and the call price with the attention most owners spend on the multiple. And be honest with yourself about the premise: if the reason you are selling is that you want to keep running the company, a structure that hands the board to someone else is a strange way to go about it.
