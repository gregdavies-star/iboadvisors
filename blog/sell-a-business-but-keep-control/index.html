How to Sell a Business but Keep Control

By Michael Chasen · Updated September 27, 2026

The short answer

You cannot sell control and keep control. What you can sell is something else: a slice of the equity, a claim on the cash flow, or the whole company to a buyer whose structure leaves the board where it already is. At $3M+ of EBITDA, four routes do that — a minority recapitalization, a dividend recapitalization, a staged sale to your own management team, and an Independent Buyout. Each one pays a different amount and takes a different thing away.

The mistake that costs owners the most is not picking the wrong route. It is assuming the ownership percentage decides who runs the company. It does not.

Control is a document, not a percentage

Owners arrive at this question with a number in their head: keep 51% and nothing changes. In practice the shareholders' agreement decides what happens, and it routinely gives an investor holding 20–40% a veto over debt, acquisitions, the annual budget, senior hires, distributions and any sale of the company. A majority holder who has signed that document does not control the company in any sense an operator would recognise. What a minority private equity stake really controls works through which items on that list are genuinely negotiable.

So the test for any structure below is not "what percentage do I keep?" It is three questions: who appoints the board, what is on the consent list, and who can force a sale and when. A route that leaves all three with you has preserved control. A route that leaves you 70% of the stock and none of the three has not.

Route 1: A minority recapitalization

You sell 20–49% of the equity for cash and keep the majority, the chief executive's chair, and the day-to-day. This is the most direct answer to the question and the one most owners mean when they ask it.

What it costs is governance. Any outside equity holder will negotiate consent rights, and the smaller the stake the more aggressively they are negotiated, because the consent list is the only protection a minority holder has. It is also normal for a minority stake to be bought at a discount to the per-share control value, so 30% of the company does not reliably fetch 30% of the enterprise value. The mechanics of a minority recap cover how the stake is priced and where the terms are set.

The variable worth more than the price is the identity of the buyer. A fund with a ten-year life has an obligation to get its money back out of your company on a schedule; a family office or an independent sponsor generally does not.

Route 2: A dividend recapitalization

The company borrows against its own cash flow and distributes the proceeds to you. No stock changes hands. No new shareholder appears. You keep 100% of the equity and 100% of the upside, and the only new party at the table is a lender.

This is not an exotic manoeuvre — it is what the private equity industry did all through 2025 when it could not sell companies. Sponsors paid themselves $43.6 billion of dividends out of the US broadly syndicated loan market that year, the highest annual figure since the financial crisis and 24% above the prior high-water mark of $35.1 billion set in 2021, with average institutional spreads on those deals compressing to 323 basis points (ABF Journal, citing PitchBook LCD).

What you have actually done is convert part of your equity into leverage on a business you still own. The same reporting cites research finding that post-recap total debt averages 84% above pre-recap levels and that the probability of financial distress rises to roughly 2.4 times the targeted-firm mean; working capital cushions shrink and the tolerance for a bad year goes with them. Tax treatment turns entirely on entity type and stock basis — the distribution can be a return of basis, a dividend, or a capital gain — so price it with your tax advisor before you price it with a lender.

Route 3: A staged sale to management

Sell a tranche to your leadership team now, the rest later. You keep the majority and the board until the final tranche, the buyer is a group you already trust, and the customers never find out the company changed hands.

The constraint is always financing. SBA 7(a) lending tops out at a $5 million maximum loan amount and lists changes of ownership, complete or partial, as an eligible use (U.S. Small Business Administration). Against a $30M+ company that is a rounding error, so the gap is filled with seller paper: the tranche you "sold" is largely a note you are holding, subordinated to the bank, repaid out of the same cash flow you used to own outright. How management buyouts actually get financed walks through the capital stack, and management buyout versus private equity sets the continuity against the cheque size.

Route 4: An Independent Buyout

An Independent Buyout sells the stock to a trust held for the employees, but sizes the financing the way a sponsor would size a leveraged buyout rather than funding it gradually out of cash flow the way a conventional ESOP does. It belongs in this list for a counter-intuitive reason: you sell the whole company and the governance does not move.

The National Center for Employee Ownership puts it plainly — "the operational control of an ESOP company rests with the board and management," the board appoints the trustee, and in practice "the trustees vote for a slate of board members nominated by the board." Trustees "almost never seek to be involved in business decisions of any kind," and in a closely held company employees can direct the trustee's vote only on limited matters, "most notably the sale of all or substantially all the assets of the firm" (NCEO). There is no fund on the cap table, so there is no consent list, no investor-appointed director, and no external clock on when the company gets sold next.

There is also a tax reason to look at it. Internal Revenue Code section 1042 lets a qualifying seller defer capital gains where the trust owns "at least 30 percent" of the stock immediately after the sale and the proceeds are reinvested in qualified replacement property inside a window that "begins 3 months before the date on which the sale of qualified securities occurs and which ends 12 months after" it (Cornell Law School, 26 U.S. Code § 1042).

The limit to be honest about is that control here means board continuity, not ownership: the stock is gone, and a board can change its mind about management. What it does not require is a new shareholder with a veto. How an IBO is structured covers the mechanics and the side-by-side against a sponsor deal runs the arithmetic.

What each route pays: a worked example

The numbers below describe an invented company. They are arithmetic on a hypothetical, not a quote, a projection, or a description of any transaction.

Take a business with $5 million of normalized EBITDA and no funded debt, valued at 6.5x, or $32.5 million. Assume lenders will advance 3.25x EBITDA — about $16 million — against the business in any leveraged structure.

Route Illustrative cash at close What you still own Who decides after close
Minority recap, 30% sold ~$9.8M before any minority discount 70% of the equity You, subject to the investor's consent list
Dividend recap at 3.0x $15M distributed to you 100% of the equity You, subject to the lender's covenants
Staged MBO, 40% now $5M bank-funded; the rest on a seller note 60% of the equity plus the note You, until the later tranches close
Independent Buyout Sized against the full $32.5M valuation, majority in cash None of the stock; the note and the board seat The board and management

The routes that keep the most equity pay the least cash, and the gap is large: a 30% minority sale is under a third of what a full-value structure produces.

Then apply tax. A seller in the top bracket pays 20% on long-term capital gain — the rate that "applies to the extent that your taxable income exceeds the thresholds set for the 15% capital gain rate" (IRS Topic No. 409) — plus the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax on capital gains above a modified adjusted gross income threshold of $250,000 for a married couple filing jointly (IRS). That is 23.8% federal before state tax, so the $9.8M minority cheque is closer to $7.4M in hand on a near-zero basis. Before comparing any of this, get a defensible enterprise value: the business valuation calculator puts a sector-adjusted range around your own EBITDA in a couple of minutes.

The five terms that actually take control away

Whichever route you pick, these are the provisions to read before the price:

  • The consent list. Every item on it is a decision you no longer make alone. Argue about the list, not the percentage.
  • Board composition and the tiebreaker. An independent seat a minority holder gets to approve is not independent.
  • Drag-along rights. The clause that turns someone else's decision to sell into your decision to sell.
  • Puts and redemption deadlines. An investor with the right to be bought out on a date has handed you a refinancing problem with a timer on it.
  • The definition of "cause." A wide cause definition in an employment agreement is a removal mechanism wearing different clothes.

They are also the terms that decide how much of any deferred payment you ever see, which is the question rollover equity and the second bite turns on.

Frequently asked questions

Can I sell part of my business and still be the boss? Yes, and it is the most common version of this transaction. But "the boss" is defined by the shareholders' agreement, not by keeping 51%. Read the consent list before you read the price.

How much cash can I take out without selling any equity? As much as a lender will advance, which for a healthy lower-middle-market company is commonly two to three-and-a-half times EBITDA. That cash comes with covenants, debt service, and materially less room for a bad year.

Is a minority recapitalization better than a full sale? It is better if the second bite matters more to you than the first, and worse if you need the money now. A minority sale converts part of the company into cash at a discount and keeps the rest exposed to the same risks you already carry.

Can I sell 100% of the company and still run it? Only where the buyer has no reason to replace you. In a trust-owned structure the board appoints management and the trustee stays out of operating decisions, which is why an ESOP or an Independent Buyout can look like a full sale on the cap table and like continuity in the building.

The bottom line

Every route that lets you keep control charges you for it, and the invoice arrives in one of two forms: a smaller cheque, or a consent list. Owners who cost both forms make good decisions here. Owners who anchor on the ownership percentage sign a document that gives away the thing they thought they were protecting. For the wider menu including a straight sponsor sale, the guide to every exit option lays it out and alternatives to selling to private equity costs the non-sponsor routes side by side.

Before settling for a partial answer, cost the structure that pays like a full sale and leaves the governance alone. An Independent Buyout makes your existing leadership team the buyer, financed against the business itself, at a valuation set the way a sponsor would set it — with no outside fund holding the consent list or the exit clock. The pros and cons of selling to private equity read differently once a full-value alternative is on the table.
