Quality of Earnings Report: What a Buyer’s Accountants Actually Test

By Michael Chasen · Updated October 31, 2026

A quality of earnings report is a transaction-focused study of your financial statements, commissioned by a buyer or by you, that rebuilds reported profit into the number a buyer is willing to multiply. It is not an audit and it produces no opinion. It produces three things: an EBITDA bridge from what your books say to what a stranger will underwrite, a proof of cash that ties those earnings to your bank statements, and a net working capital analysis that sets the peg you will be measured against at closing.

Those three outputs decide two separate numbers. The first is the price. The second is how much of the price survives the post-closing true-up. Most sellers prepare for the first and find out about the second after the wire has cleared.

What is actually in the report

The structure is consistent across firms. Warren Averett’s description of its own engagements is representative: a quality of earnings analysis covers adjusted EBITDA, proof of cash and working capital, built from the P&L, the balance sheet and the tax returns, usually across the last two to three years.

  • The EBITDA bridge. Reported EBITDA, then three stacked layers of adjustment: the ones management proposes, the ones diligence finds on its own, and pro forma adjustments for things that have changed but are not yet in the trailing numbers. Each line lands in the final figure or it does not, and the detail of which survives is in what add-backs survive diligence.
  • Proof of cash. The analysis compares the cash actually moving through the bank statements against the revenue and expenses in the financials, adjusting for accruals. Baker Tilly puts the reason bluntly in its own guidance: the diligence professional “should be performing a proof of cash”, because “if the cash cannot be proved, then the buyer cannot be sure of anything.”
  • Net working capital. Current assets and liabilities, month by month, plus the cash cycle behind them. This is the input to the peg. Baker Tilly again: “Evaluating the accuracy of the net working capital accounts should not be overlooked,” and the adjustments made there can move earnings too.

It is not an audit, and the difference runs the wrong way for sellers

Owners who have been through a clean audit often assume a quality of earnings review is a lighter version of the same exercise. It is the opposite. An audit tests whether historical statements are fairly stated within a materiality threshold that scales with the size of the business. A quality of earnings study is a consulting engagement, not an attest service — Baker Tilly notes that this gives “flexibility in the approach and scope,” and, critically, that “the materiality is much lower in a quality of earnings study than an audit.” Warren Averett makes the other half of the trade explicit: the analysis “is not an audit and, therefore, no opinion is given.”

So you get a document with no opinion attached, written to a tighter threshold than your auditors apply, by a firm whose client is usually the person paying you. A $60,000 misclassification your auditor would wave through as immaterial is a $60,000 line item in the bridge — and at a mid-single-digit multiple, that is several hundred thousand dollars of price.

A bridge on an invented company

Take a made-up manufacturer: $38,000,000 of revenue, $3,800,000 of reported EBITDA, and a seller’s add-back schedule of $650,000 assembled by the CFO before going to market. The buyer’s diligence firm accepts some of it and strikes the rest.

Line Amount Outcome in diligence
Reported EBITDA $3,800,000 Starting point
Owner compensation above market $220,000 Accepted — supported by a third-party comp study
One-time litigation defence costs $150,000 Accepted — tied to invoices and a settled matter
Personal vehicles, travel and club dues $60,000 Accepted — itemised in the general ledger
“Non-recurring” recruiting and severance $140,000 Struck — appears in all three years
ERP implementation, capitalised portion expensed $80,000 Struck — no documentation separating it from ongoing IT
Diligence adjustment: unrecorded PTO accrual –$90,000 Added by the buyer’s team, not the seller’s
Adjusted EBITDA the buyer will underwrite $4,140,000 Against the seller’s own figure of $4,450,000

At 6.5x, the seller’s schedule was worth $28,925,000 and the surviving one is worth $26,910,000. The $310,000 gap between the two EBITDA figures cost $2,015,000 of enterprise value, and none of it was argued about — it was decided by whether a document existed. Run your own figure through the valuation calculator and against the multiple ranges by industry to see what a comparable gap is worth in your sector.

The working capital peg is the second negotiation

The same report sets the net working capital target. The mechanism is simple: the parties agree a peg, usually an average of the trailing months, and at closing the delivered balance is compared to it. Deliver less and the price drops dollar for dollar.

Here is the trap, and it is the reason the bridge and the peg have to be read together. Suppose diligence adds that $90,000 PTO accrual. If the accrual is applied to every historical month used to build the peg and to the closing balance sheet, the peg falls by $90,000, the delivered amount falls by $90,000, and the true-up is neutral — you pay for the accrual once, through the multiple. If it is applied only to the closing balance sheet, you pay for it twice: once in the price and again in the adjustment. Nothing in the report flags this. It is a drafting point in the purchase agreement, and the time to make it is before signing.

What the post-closing mechanism will and will not fix

Sellers often assume the true-up is a general-purpose correction, and that if something in the diligence file turns out to be wrong, the independent accountant appointed under the agreement will sort it out. Delaware has said otherwise, in a case large enough that the reasoning is worth borrowing.

In Chicago Bridge & Iron Company N.V. v. Westinghouse Electric Company LLC (Supreme Court of Delaware, No. 573, 2016, decided 27 June 2017), the buyer submitted objections that would have moved the net working capital amount by roughly $1.93 billion, on the argument that the seller’s historical accounting had not complied with GAAP. The Supreme Court reversed the Court of Chancery. Summarising the holding, Jones Day notes that the Court found the independent auditor had no “wide-ranging brief to adjudicate all disputes”; its authority was “one confined to a discrete set of narrow disputes,” and it was acting “as an expert, not an arbitrator.”

The practical reading for a $3M+ EBITDA seller is not about the billions. It is that the accounting arguments you want to win have to be won in the diligence process, in writing, before the agreement is signed. The narrow mechanism that follows closing is for measuring an agreed definition, not for relitigating what the definition should have been. Whatever is unresolved at signing gets handled the other way — by an earnout, an escrow, or a holdback, all of which move the money to the far side of the closing.

Should you commission one yourself?

The honest answer depends on size, and there is public data on exactly where the line falls. GF Data looked at 360 transactions completed since the third quarter of 2024 and found that sellers who used a sell-side quality of earnings report averaged 7.4x TEV/EBITDA against 7.0x for those who did not — but the benefit was concentrated in deals above $50 million of enterprise value, and smaller deals generally saw no valuation boost at all. In the same reporting, Scott Linch of Forvis Mazars Capital Advisors estimates that at least 90% of the private-equity-backed deals he sees use a sell-side report, against roughly 50% of lower-middle-market founder-led businesses — his estimate from practice rather than a measured figure.

Read together: below about $50 million of enterprise value, buy the report for speed and credibility, not for multiple expansion. A company at $4,000,000 of EBITDA is usually below that threshold, and a seller who pays for a report expecting half a turn of multiple is buying the wrong thing. What the report does buy at that size is a diligence process that does not stall, and the chance to find the $140,000 of recruiting costs that appear in all three years before a buyer finds them and re-prices around them.

On timing, Warren Averett puts the majority of middle-market analyses at three to four weeks, with management meetings running anywhere from two hours to two days. Add revision cycles and it is comfortably a six-week item, which is why it belongs before the process rather than inside it. On fees, be sceptical of every published range: nearly all of them come from firms selling the service, and they span an order of magnitude. Ask for a fixed fee against a written scope that names the periods, whether a proof of cash is included, and whether the working capital analysis will be delivered in a form your counsel can put into the purchase agreement.

Frequently asked questions

Is a quality of earnings report the same as an audit? No. An audit gives an opinion on whether historical statements are fairly stated. A quality of earnings study is a consulting engagement with no opinion, run at a much lower materiality threshold, and aimed at whether earnings are sustainable and convert to cash.

Who pays for it? Whoever commissions it. A buy-side report is paid for by the buyer and written for the buyer; a sell-side report is paid for by the seller before going to market. A sell-side report does not remove the buyer’s own diligence — it gives the buyer’s team a tested starting file instead of a stack of exports.

How far back does it go? Typically the last two to three years, sometimes on a trailing-twelve-month basis updated as the process runs long.

Can the findings change the price after we have signed? Through the working capital true-up and any escrow or earnout, yes, within the limits the agreement sets. The broad accounting arguments are meant to be settled before signing, which is what the Delaware decision above turns on.

Does an internal sale need one? If debt is funding it, yes. A lender sizing facilities against the company’s own cash flow underwrites the adjusted figure, not the reported one, and will usually name the report it wants to see.

The bottom line

A quality of earnings report is not a verdict on your company. It is a rebuild of your earnings by someone with no reason to be generous, delivered to whoever paid for it. The seller who reads it as a price negotiation conducted in documents — and who has those documents assembled before anyone asks — keeps the number the schedule was supposed to produce. The one who treats it as an accounting formality finds out what their EBITDA is worth at the same moment the buyer does. If you want the fuller picture of how the figure gets built in the first place, start with how private equity values a company and how a sale process actually runs.

The same report does the same job in an Independent Buyout, with one difference in who it is written for. The financing is sized against the company’s own adjusted cash flow, so the surviving EBITDA sets what the structure can pay the owner at close — but there is no sponsor on the other side of the table using the findings to re-trade a signed letter. The equity moves to a trust owned by the employees and the existing team keeps running the company.
