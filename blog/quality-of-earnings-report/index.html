Quality of Earnings Report: What a QoE Tests

By Michael Chasen · Updated November 1, 2026

A quality of earnings report — a QoE — is an accounting firm’s independent reconstruction of what a business actually earns, built for a transaction rather than for the tax return. It takes reported EBITDA apart and tests each piece: whether the revenue is real and sits in the right period, whether the cash ever arrived, which adjustments survive scrutiny, and how much working capital the company needs to keep running. It is not an audit and nobody issues an opinion. An audit asks whether last year’s statements are fairly stated under GAAP. A QoE asks whether the next owner can count on the earnings they are being asked to pay a multiple of.

If your company does $3M+ of EBITDA and you sell it, a QoE will be performed on your business. The only open questions are who commissions it, how early, and who reads the findings first.

Audit, review, QoE: three documents, three questions

Document Question it answers What you get
Audit Are the financial statements fairly stated in accordance with GAAP? An opinion, signed, covering a historical period
Review or compilation Is anything obviously wrong on the face of the statements? Limited assurance, or none at all
Quality of earnings What does this business repeatably earn, and what does it take in working capital to earn it? A findings report and a databook: an EBITDA bridge, a monthly profit-and-loss by line, a working capital schedule, no opinion

Owners who have been audited for years are often surprised that the audit buys them very little in diligence. An audit is run to a materiality threshold set at the company level; a buyer paying seven times earnings cares about items an audit would never chase, because $100,000 of annual EBITDA that an auditor would wave through is $700,000 of price. The two documents are not substitutes, and a clean audit does not shorten a QoE.

What a QoE actually tests

Proof of cash. The first and most basic workstream: tie recorded revenue and expenses back to the bank statements, month by month, for the trailing two or three years. It is unglamorous and it is where the ugly surprises live — deposits recorded twice after a bank feed is re-imported, cash sales that never reached the account, intercompany transfers booked as income. If the proof of cash does not tie, nothing downstream of it is worth reading.

Revenue quality and cut-off. When is revenue earned, and is it in the right month? Companies that bill in advance, ship in stages, or invoice on milestones routinely have revenue sitting a period away from where it belongs, which distorts every trailing-twelve-month figure built on it. The SEC’s own non-GAAP guidance draws the line in the clearest available language: adjustments that have “the effect of changing the recognition and measurement principles required to be applied in accordance with GAAP” are “considered individually tailored and may cause the presentation of a non-GAAP measure to be misleading”, and the staff names the exact move private sellers make by accident — “changing the basis of accounting for revenue or expenses in a non-GAAP performance measure from an accrual basis in accordance with GAAP to a cash basis.” Billings are not revenue. A QoE will say so in writing.

The add-back schedule. Every adjustment gets a line, a source document and a verdict. This is the part sellers think a QoE is, and it is one workstream of five; which adjustments hold up is a long enough subject to have its own post on what survives diligence. The SEC’s standard is the one a diligence team applies in substance: a measure that “excludes normal, recurring, cash operating expenses necessary to operate a registrant’s business” is the kind of measure that misleads, and an expense “that occurs repeatedly or occasionally, including at irregular intervals” counts as recurring.

Normalised working capital. How much cash the business must keep tied up in receivables and inventory, net of payables, through a full seasonal cycle — and what the trend has been. This feeds the peg, and the peg is where a QoE quietly earns or costs a seller real money. See below.

Concentration, margin bridge and pro forma items. Who the revenue comes from and whether it is contracted; what moved gross margin between years and whether it was price, mix or a one-off; and which pro forma adjustments — a mid-year price increase, a customer won in month nine — are annualised, how, and with what evidence. These sit in a separate schedule, labelled, because a buyer will discount them differently from historical earnings.

A QoE on an invented company

Take a specialty distributor with no real-world counterpart: $4,200,000 of reported EBITDA, a $600,000 add-back schedule its owner considers conservative, and a market multiple of 7.0x. The owner’s arithmetic is $4,800,000 × 7.0 = $33,600,000.

A sell-side QoE run six months before the data room opens finds two things. First, $120,000 of the schedule is a “one-time” freight surcharge that also appears in two of the previous four years, so it is a cost of doing business and comes out. Second, a cut-off error has pulled $180,000 of January revenue into December; at a 30% contribution margin, $54,000 of EBITDA moves out of the trailing twelve months with it.

Line Owner’s figure After the QoE
Reported EBITDA $4,200,000 $4,200,000
Add-backs claimed $600,000 $480,000
Revenue cut-off correction — ($54,000)
Adjusted EBITDA $4,800,000 $4,626,000
At 7.0x $33,600,000 $32,382,000

That is a $1,218,000 difference, and a seller’s instinct is to call the QoE expensive. Run the other version. The same two findings surface in week six of the buyer’s diligence instead, after a letter of intent has been signed at $33,600,000 and the owner has stopped talking to other bidders. Now the price moves down by the same $1,218,000, except that it moves under exclusivity, with the buyer’s accountants holding the pen, and every other figure in the data room is re-read in the light of two that were wrong. The findings are identical. The negotiating position is not.

The working capital peg is the line sellers underestimate

Deals in this market are done on a cash-free, debt-free basis with a normalised working capital target — the peg. Deliver more working capital than the peg at closing and the price goes up dollar for dollar; deliver less and it comes down. Nothing is multiplied. It is cash off the wire.

Stay with the invented distributor. Its receivables plus inventory less payables averaged $5,200,000 over the trailing twelve months, but the business is seasonal: through the three months around a December closing it runs at $4,700,000. Set the peg on the twelve-month average and the seller delivers $500,000 less than the target at close and writes it off the price. Set it on the months that actually surround the closing date — which is what the seasonality supports — and the shortfall is nothing.

$500,000 of cash, decided by which averaging convention goes into the purchase agreement. It is the single highest-value hour in the whole QoE, and it is settled by the working capital schedule in the databook, not by argument on a call. A buyer with a QoE and a seller without one are not negotiating that point on equal terms.

Lenders name the QoE in their own loan documents

Credit agreements are public record, and they show how much weight an institution puts on this one document. Oil-Dri Corporation of America’s credit agreement, as conformed through its Ninth Amendment dated October 7, 2026, builds the report straight into the definition of Consolidated EBITDA: clause (s) adds back “adjustments reflected in any quality of earnings report prepared by a nationally or regionally recognized accounting firm, in connection with any acquisition or other investment permitted under the terms of this Agreement consummated after the Sixth Amendment Effective Date”.

Read what that does. The lender is not asking the borrower to argue for its adjustments; it is naming the document that counts as evidence, and sizing the debt off the earnings that document supports. The same logic reaches a private seller directly. The bank financing your buyer applies this test to your numbers, and the debt it will advance is calculated on the EBITDA a QoE stands behind — which is why how a buyout gets financed and what your earnings survive diligence at are the same question asked twice.

Sell-side or buy-side

A buy-side QoE is commissioned by the buyer, scoped to the buyer’s risks, and reported to the buyer. You will see the conclusions when they arrive as a price adjustment. A sell-side QoE is commissioned by the seller before the process starts, and its findings land while you still have time to fix the underlying records and all of your leverage intact.

GF Data looked at 360 transactions completed since the third quarter of 2024 and found that sellers who used a sell-side QoE saw average TEV/EBITDA multiples of 7.4x against 7.0x for those who did not, with the benefit most notable on deals above $50 million of enterprise value and no notable boost below that. In the same reporting, one practitioner puts the speed effect plainly: “I’d say that over 90% of the time, it moves the deal faster than it would’ve [gone] without it.” Speed matters more than the multiple on a lot of deals. Every additional week under exclusivity is a week in which something can change the buyer’s mind.

Expect a five-figure fee and a process measured in weeks rather than days. There is no published fee survey for lower-middle-market QoE work, so treat any precise average you are quoted as an extrapolation from the quoting firm’s own invoices, and get two or three proposals with the scope written down — years covered, monthly detail, working capital analysis included or not.

What a QoE will not do for you

  • It is not assurance. No opinion is issued and none is implied. A QoE is an analysis, performed under a consulting engagement, and its value is in the schedules rather than in anyone’s signature.
  • It does not stop the buyer running its own. A sell-side report shortens the buyer’s work and frames the questions; it does not replace the buyer’s diligence, and a buyer that wants to rely on your report will ask your firm for a reliance letter on its own terms.
  • It cannot fix records it has to work from. If the books are on a cash basis, the inventory has never been counted, or personal and company spending run through one account, the first QoE is a remediation project. That is an argument for commissioning it early, not for skipping it.
  • It will not flatter you. The findings are the findings. A seller who commissions a report hoping for a higher number, and then suppresses it when the number comes back lower, has paid to learn something and then paid again in diligence.

How to read your own QoE before a buyer does

Four things to look at first, in order:

  • The EBITDA bridge. Reported to adjusted, line by line, with the firm’s verdict on each. Any line marked “per management” without a supporting document is a line a buyer will strike.
  • The proof of cash variances. Months where recorded income and the bank do not tie. Each one needs an explanation you can give in a sentence.
  • The working capital schedule. The twelve-month average, the trend, and the seasonal shape. Decide which averaging convention you will argue for before anyone drafts a purchase agreement.
  • The pro forma and run-rate page. Everything the buyer will discount hardest, in one place. If it carries more than a small share of your adjusted EBITDA, your number depends on things that have not happened yet.

Then put the result where it belongs in the sequence. How PE firms value a company shows what happens to that number once a buyer has it; the business valuation calculator will give you a range to test your own adjusted figure against; and exit planning for business owners puts the QoE a year or two before the data room, which is the only place it is worth what it costs.

Frequently asked questions

Is a quality of earnings report the same as an audit? No. An audit gives an opinion on whether historical statements are fairly stated under GAAP. A QoE gives no opinion; it rebuilds earnings for a buyer’s purposes, at a level of detail an audit does not reach, and it covers things an audit ignores entirely, such as the working capital peg and the sustainability of margin.

Do I need a sell-side QoE at $3M of EBITDA? It is a judgment call at the bottom of the range and close to automatic above $5M. The GF Data finding above shows the valuation benefit concentrated in deals above $50 million of enterprise value, so a seller in the $3M-$5M band should weigh it mainly as diligence insurance and speed rather than as a multiple-buying exercise — and should still do the underlying work, because the buyer’s accountants will.

Can the buyer use my sell-side report? Often, in part. The buyer’s firm will usually read your databook, re-perform the pieces it cares about, and scope its own work around what you have already done. Formal reliance is a separate negotiation between the buyer and your accounting firm, with its own letter and its own limits on liability.

What if the QoE finds something bad? Then you found it on your own clock, which is the entire argument for running one. A problem found before the market knows your company is for sale is a problem with options: fix it, restate it, or price it in and disclose it. The same problem found in week six of exclusivity is a retrade. For a sense of what that costs, the contested amount usually does not disappear — it reappears as an earnout or a larger escrow, and you are asked to prove it after closing.

The bottom line

A quality of earnings report is not a document that makes your business worth more. It is a document that decides whether the number you believe is the number a stranger can verify — and it does that either on your schedule, before anyone has signed anything, or on the buyer’s, after you have stopped talking to everyone else. The work is the same work. Only the timing, and the leverage, are yours to choose.

The same report does the same job in an Independent Buyout, where the financing is underwritten against the company’s own adjusted cash flow: the earnings a QoE stands behind set what the structure can pay the owner at close. The difference is what sits on the other side of the table — the equity moves to a trust owned by the employees, the existing team keeps running the business, and there is no sponsor and no exit clock attached to the money.
