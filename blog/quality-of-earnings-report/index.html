Quality of Earnings Report: What a QoE Actually Finds

By Michael Chasen · Updated November 2, 2026

A quality of earnings report — a QoE — is an independent accounting firm’s reconstruction of what your company actually earned, commissioned by a buyer or a lender and used to set the price they will pay. It is not an audit, it is not optional in a sponsor-led process, and it is not a formality. It is the document in which your adjusted EBITDA stops being a number you produced and becomes a number somebody else agrees with, and every dollar it removes is multiplied by the purchase multiple on its way out of your proceeds.

This is what a QoE tests, what happens when its findings land late, and what the public data says about commissioning one yourself before a buyer does.

A QoE is not an audit, and the difference works against the seller

Owners with audited financials often assume a QoE is a formality. It is the opposite. Baker Tilly puts the distinction plainly: “a quality of earnings is a consulting engagement, not an attest service, providing flexibility in the approach and scope” and “the materiality is much lower in a quality of earnings study than an audit” (Baker Tilly, “Ten considerations in a quality of earnings study”).

Three consequences follow from that, and they all point the same way:

  • No materiality shelter. An auditor can pass over a $120,000 misclassification. A QoE analyst cannot, because $120,000 of EBITDA is $840,000 of enterprise value at 7.0x.
  • No fixed scope. An audit follows a standard. A QoE goes wherever the buyer’s thesis says the risk is — a single customer, a single plant, a single year of bonus accruals.
  • No benefit of the doubt on your adjustments. Baker Tilly’s instruction on seller add-backs is that they be validated with original documentation and that “Estimates should not be accepted.”

An audit, in other words, is an opinion on the financial statements you prepared. A QoE is an argument about the price.

What a QoE actually tests

The work is narrower than owners expect and deeper than they are ready for. Five workstreams carry almost all of the price risk.

What the QoE tests What settles it
Proof of cash. Bank statements tied back to recorded revenue and expenses, month by month. Baker Tilly is blunt that “the due diligence professional should be performing a proof of cash.” Twelve to thirty-six months of statements for every account, reconciled. Gaps here taint everything downstream, because the whole report rests on cash being real.
Revenue recognition and cutoff. Whether revenue sits in the period the work was done, tested against your own stated policy and against GAAP. Shipping documents, signed acceptances, contract terms. A December invoice for a January delivery moves earnings out of the trailing twelve months the price is built on.
Add-backs. Each adjustment in your schedule, tested for whether it is genuinely non-recurring and genuinely yours to claim. Engagement letters, leases, comp studies, invoices tied to a matter that has ended. Which adjusted EBITDA add-backs survive is its own discipline, and it is the single largest source of disputed value.
Net working capital. A trailing average of the working capital the business needs to run, which becomes the peg you have to deliver at closing. Aged receivables and payables, inventory counts, and accruals that were never booked — unrecorded bonuses are the classic finding.
Debt-like items. Obligations that are not on the debt schedule but behave like debt: deferred revenue, accrued PTO, deferred compensation, unfunded repairs, related-party balances. Every one of these is a dollar-for-dollar reduction in what reaches you, and unlike an EBITDA finding it is not multiplied — which is the only good news in this row.

What a finding costs: the arithmetic

Take an invented company with no connection to any real transaction. It reports $8.0M of adjusted EBITDA and signs a letter of intent at 7.0x, an enterprise value of $56.0M. The buyer’s QoE lands six weeks later.

Finding EBITDA effect Enterprise value at 7.0x
Reported adjusted EBITDA $8.00M $56.0M
Owner compensation added back in full, against a $350,000 market cost for the general manager the buyer will have to hire ($0.25M) ($1.8M)
Related-party rent added back at the full rent rather than the spread over an appraised market rate ($0.10M) ($0.7M)
Revenue cutoff: December invoices for January deliveries, moved out of the trailing twelve months ($0.20M) ($1.4M)
“One-time” legal fees that appeared in each of the last three years ($0.15M) ($1.0M)
QoE-adjusted EBITDA $7.30M $51.1M

$700,000 of findings, $4.9M of price. The ratio is the only thing worth memorising: at a 7.0x multiple, every $100,000 the QoE disallows costs you $700,000, and the multiple private equity applies to your company is itself partly a judgment about how well the numbers held up. A schedule that loses four lines invites a re-read of everything else in the data room, and that re-read shows up in structure — more escrow, more holdback, a larger earnout — rather than in a line anyone names out loud.

The timing is what makes it expensive. A finding delivered before you go to market is a bookkeeping correction. The same finding delivered under exclusivity, with a signed LOI and no competing bidder left in the room, is a renegotiation you have almost no leverage in.

The second price: the working capital peg

The QoE does not stop at EBITDA. Its net working capital analysis sets the target you must hand over at closing, and anything you deliver below the peg comes straight out of the proceeds. This is now near-universal and it runs against sellers more often than for them.

SRS Acquiom’s 2026 study of more than 1,570 private-target acquisitions closed between 2020 and Q3 2025, with an aggregate value of roughly $385 billion, found that “92% of all deals during the survey period had purchase price adjustment provisions” and that “51% of final adjustments were in the buyer’s favor, compared to 38% in the seller’s favor” (DealLawyers.com, “Purchase Price Adjustments: SRS Acquiom Working Capital PPA Study”). The asymmetry in how claims get contested is sharper still: “22% of adjustment claims with initial buyer-favorable adjustments were disputed, compared to only 6% of initial seller-favorable adjustments.”

The sizes are smaller than the EBITDA findings but not trivial. The same study puts the average buyer-favorable final adjustment at “0.76% of the transaction value” and the median at “0.27% of the median transaction value”. On the $51.1M deal above, 0.76% is about $390,000 — and “The average buyer-favorable final adjustments soaked up 77% of the PPA escrow”, so most sellers who lose this one lose most of the money set aside for it.

Your lender reads the same report

A QoE is not only a buyer’s tool. In a leveraged deal the debt is sized off adjusted EBITDA, so the lender needs to know which adjustments it is willing to underwrite — and large credit agreements now say so in the definitions. Gen Digital’s amended credit agreement permits add-backs “evidenced by or contained in a due diligence quality of earnings report” made available to the administrative agent and prepared by a big-four firm or another firm acceptable to it, consistent with Regulation S-X (Gen Digital Inc., Third Amendment to Amended and Restated Credit Agreement, Exhibit 10.36, definition of “Consolidated EBITDA”).

Read that from the seller’s chair. The QoE number is not just the number the buyer negotiates against; it is the number the debt is sized against, which means it sets the ceiling on what any leveraged structure can pay you. A private equity buyout is funded mostly with borrowed money raised against your own cash flow, so a QoE finding compounds: it lowers the price, and it lowers the amount of that price a lender will fund.

Should you commission your own?

A sell-side QoE is the same exercise run by your own firm, on your timetable, before the buyer’s analyst has a signed LOI to leverage. GF Data looked at 360 transactions completed since Q3 2024 and found that sellers who used a sell-side QoE averaged 7.4x TEV/EBITDA against 7.0x for those who did not, with the benefit concentrated in deals above $50 million of enterprise value and little measurable lift on smaller ones (Middle Market Growth (ACG), GF Data on quality of earnings reports).

Treat the 0.4x as an association rather than a mechanism — sellers organised enough to commission a QoE tend to be organised in other ways a buyer pays for. The more useful numbers in the same reporting are the behavioural ones: practitioners quoted there estimate that at least 90% of PE-backed sellers run one, while only about half of lower-middle-market founder-led businesses do, and they recommend starting three to six months before the process opens. If you are selling to a sponsor, the asymmetry is the point — they have read their own report and you have not.

What a sell-side QoE buys is not a higher number. It is the chance to fix findings while they are still cheap, before anyone has leverage over you. On a $50M deal the fee is a rounding error against a single disallowed add-back.

What to do before anyone opens the file

  • Reconcile cash first. If the bank statements do not tie to the general ledger, nothing after that matters.
  • Build the add-back schedule as an evidence file, not a spreadsheet. One document per line: the lease, the engagement letter, the comp study, the invoice. Lines you cannot document should come out before someone else takes them out.
  • Fix the cutoff. Revenue recognised a month early is the cheapest finding to prevent and one of the most expensive to concede.
  • Book the accruals you have been skipping. Unrecorded bonuses and PTO are found in essentially every first-time diligence process.
  • Know your own working capital pattern. Twelve months of monthly net working capital, seasonally understood, so the peg is a negotiation rather than a surprise.
  • Know roughly what the number should be worth. The business valuation calculator puts a sector-adjusted range around your EBITDA, which tells you what each disputed line is worth in dollars before you decide which ones to fight for.

Most of this is a year’s work, not a month’s, which is why exit planning belongs well ahead of the process and why selling a business to private equity goes badly for owners who start preparing when the first call comes in.

Frequently asked questions

Who pays for the quality of earnings report? Whoever commissions it. A buy-side QoE is paid for by the buyer and belongs to the buyer — you will usually see the findings, not the report. A sell-side QoE is paid for by the seller, is yours, and can be shared with bidders under a reliance letter. Buyers rarely accept a sell-side report in place of their own, but a credible one narrows what their analyst has left to find.

Is a quality of earnings report required to sell a business? Not by any rule. In practice, any buyer using leverage will run one, because the lender needs the adjusted EBITDA it is underwriting to have an independent firm’s name on it. A cash buyer with no debt can skip it, and few do.

What is the difference between a QoE and an audit? An audit is an attest engagement giving an opinion on whether financial statements are fairly stated, at a materiality level set against revenue or assets. A QoE is a consulting engagement with a much lower materiality threshold, aimed at sustainable economic earnings and cash flow rather than at the statements. Audited financials help — they make the QoE faster — but they do not replace it.

How long does a quality of earnings take? The schedule is driven by how fast you can produce documents, not by the accountants. The practitioners quoted by Middle Market Growth recommend starting a sell-side report three to six months before going to market, which tells you how much of the elapsed time is expected to be yours.

The bottom line

The quality of earnings report is where a sale stops being about the multiple and starts being about the numerator. Owners spend months negotiating 7.0x against 7.5x and then hand over a $700,000 finding without a fight, because the documents that would have settled it were never assembled. The multiple is a market fact you have limited influence over. The EBITDA the multiple applies to is almost entirely yours to document.

The same report does the same work in an Independent Buyout: the financing is sized off the company’s own adjusted cash flow, so the earnings that survive a QoE set what the structure can pay the owner — with the difference that the equity moves to a trust owned by the employees, the existing team keeps running the company, and there is no sponsor and no exit clock attached to the money.
