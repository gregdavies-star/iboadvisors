Does Private Equity Replace the Founder CEO? The Numbers

By Michael Chasen · Updated November 17, 2026

Most founders keep the job through closing, and most sponsors change a CEO at some point before they sell. In AlixPartners’ 11th annual Private Equity Leadership Survey, published in March 2026 from 427 responses — 174 private equity leaders and 253 portfolio company executives — 65% of private equity firms reported CEO turnover during the holding period, and only 9% said their firms rarely replace CEOs.

The academic survey work points the same way from the other end. When Gompers, Kaplan and Mukharlyamov asked 79 buyout investors with more than $750 billion under management what they actually do, in What Do Private Equity Firms Say They Do?, almost 70% said they back the existing management team rather than bring in their own before investing. But 31% recruit their own senior team before the deal, roughly 50% end up doing it after, and combining the two, almost 58% of private equity investors recruit their own senior team at some point in the life of an investment.

So the honest answer to “will they replace me?” is: rarely on day one, and often enough before the exit that you should price it in. The question worth preparing for is not whether it happens but what it costs you when it does, because the answer to that is written into documents you sign at close, while you still have leverage.

Why founders are rarely replaced at close

A sponsor buying a lower-middle-market company is buying something that mostly lives in one person’s head: the customer relationships, the pricing instincts, the reason the gross margin is what it is. Replacing that on day one destroys the thing being paid for. Management continuity is part of what gets underwritten, and the lenders financing the deal ask about it directly — it is one of the standard diligence items in how a buyout gets financed.

There is also a quieter reason. The same survey found that in roughly one-third of their investments, private equity investors expect to create value by changing the CEO or CFO — an expectation they form before they invest. In a third of deals, then, the plan exists at signing. It is simply not on the agenda of the meeting you are in.

What actually triggers the change

Patterns repeat across the market, and none of them is “the sponsor turned out to be dishonest”:

  • The first missed budget. The model that justified the price assumed a growth rate. Miss it twice and the board starts asking whether the company needs a different operator rather than a different plan.
  • The professionalization gap. Monthly close in five days, a real FP&A function, a system of record that is not a spreadsheet. Founders who built a company on judgment often find the reporting load itself is the job they did not want, and the CFO change usually arrives first.
  • Add-on acquisitions. If the thesis is a platform, your job quietly changes from running one company to integrating four. That is a different skill, and both sides often discover it eighteen months in. This is one of the structural changes described in what happens after private equity buys your company.
  • The founder who wanted out anyway. A meaningful share of “CEO turnover” is an owner who agreed to stay two years, stayed two years, and left on schedule. It still counts in the statistics.
  • Disagreement about pace. Leverage changes what prudence looks like. An owner who ran the business debt-free for thirty years and a sponsor underwriting a debt-funded expansion are applying different standards to the same decision.

The clock the 65% runs on

That turnover figure is measured across the whole holding period, and the holding period is long. The current median is six years and the average at exit is closer to seven, as set out in the numbers behind the private equity holding period. Six years is a long time to hold a job at the pleasure of a board you do not appoint.

The cost is not only yours. In AlixPartners’ earlier research, 83% of private equity executives said unplanned CEO turnover lengthens holding periods and nearly half said it reduces returns; the firm’s 2018 survey found CEO turnover was unplanned in 34% of investments. The 2026 survey adds the view from the other chair: 38% of portfolio company executives worry about losing their jobs because of deal-driven disruption, far higher than at companies without private equity investment.

What your employment agreement is actually worth

Owners negotiate the purchase price for months and the employment agreement in an afternoon. The second document decides what the first one is worth if you are let go in year two. The provisions that matter:

  • Term and termination. A three-year term is not three years of security if the agreement also lets the board terminate without cause on notice. Read the two clauses together, never apart.
  • The definition of “cause”. Narrow and objective (fraud, conviction, material breach after a cure period) is the version you want. “Failure to meet performance objectives set by the board” hands the board a cause termination whenever it wants one, and cause usually switches off severance and reprices your equity.
  • “Good reason” resignation. If your title, duties, reporting line or location change materially, a good-reason clause lets you leave and still be treated as terminated without cause. Without it, demotion is a one-way street.
  • Severance. Twelve months of base plus target bonus is a common ask; what matters as much is whether it survives a change of control and whether it is conditioned on a release.
  • The non-compete. You just sold your industry knowledge. Expect a restriction, negotiate its length and its geography, and make sure the clock starts at termination, not at some later date.
  • Leaver provisions on the incentive equity. This is where the money is, and it is covered in the equity documents rather than the employment agreement — which is exactly why it gets read last.

A worked example of what a termination costs

Made-up numbers, realistic shape. A sponsor buys a company at $60M enterprise value. The founder stays as CEO, rolls $8M into the new entity, and is granted 3.0% of a management incentive pool that vests 20% a year across the expected five-year hold. Assume the business performs and the equity is worth $120M at exit, so the full 3.0% would be worth $3.6M.

And in all three rows the rolled $8M is still waiting on a sale that someone else schedules, which is the whole subject of rollover equity and the second bite.

Who can actually remove you: control deals versus minority deals

Hiring and firing the CEO is a board decision, so the question reduces to who appoints the board. In a control sale the sponsor appoints a majority and the board can change the CEO whenever it chooses. In a minority investment the investor does not take the board majority, but removing or replacing the CEO is one of the protective provisions on its consent list — so it cannot install its own CEO, and it can block yours, and usually your successor too. The mechanics are set out in what a minority private equity stake really gives the investor.

This is why “I’m only selling 40%” is a weaker protection than it sounds, and why the consent list deserves the same attention as the price. If you are still working out what the price should even be, run the ranges first in the business valuation calculator, then negotiate the terms that decide what you keep of it.

Five questions to ask before you sign

  • Who has the votes to terminate me, and does any approval of mine attach to that decision?
  • What exactly is “cause”, and does missing a budget fall inside it?
  • If I am terminated without cause, what happens to unvested incentive equity, and is there any acceleration?
  • Can the company repurchase my vested shares, at what price, and on what trigger?
  • In the sponsor’s last three platform investments, is the founder still the CEO? Ask it in the meeting; the answer, and the hesitation before it, are both data.

The bottom line

Nobody buying your company will tell you they intend to replace you, and in most deals they genuinely do not intend it on day one. They intend to hit a return over six or seven years, and if the operator becomes the obstacle to that return, the board does what boards do. Two-thirds of firms report it happening. A third expect it before they invest.

None of that is an argument against selling to private equity — it is an argument for reading the employment agreement and the leaver provisions as carefully as the price, and for weighing the rest of the pros and cons of selling to private equity with your eyes open.
