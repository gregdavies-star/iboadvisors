Seller Financing in a Business Sale: What a Seller Note Actually Pays

By Michael Chasen · Updated October 19, 2026

The short answer

Seller financing is the part of your purchase price you do not get paid. You lend it back to the buyer, usually unsecured, usually ranked behind the bank that funded the rest, and you collect it over three to five years out of the cash flow of a company you no longer control. It is a loan dressed up as a price.

In the lower middle market it is also normal. The Q2 2026 Market Pulse survey, run by the International Business Brokers Association and M&A Source across 255 advisors and 181 closed transactions, found sellers received 83% to 92% cash at close on average, with buyer equity and senior debt accounting for that same share of transaction value; seller financing "accounted for less than 10% of most deals in Q2, somewhat below trend" (IBBA / M&A Source Market Pulse, Q2 2026). So the question is never whether a note appears. It is what the note is worth, and what you gave up to get it.

What a seller note actually is

A promissory note from the buying entity to you, with four variables: principal, stated interest rate, amortization schedule, and security. The first three get discussed at the table. The fourth decides whether you are paid.

Security almost never means what a seller assumes. The senior lender funding the acquisition takes a first lien on everything — receivables, inventory, equipment, the equity of the acquired company. What is left for a seller note is a second position behind a loan sized to absorb most of the collateral value, or no lien at all. Read how private equity actually finances a buyout for how much of the balance sheet the senior facility claims before anyone else is considered.

Subordination is the clause that matters, and it is not about liens

Lien priority decides who gets paid out of collateral in a liquidation. A subordination agreement decides something more immediate: whether you are allowed to be paid at all while the business is still running, and whether you may do anything about it if you are not.

These agreements are public record whenever a party files them. A subordination agreement dated 30 June 2006 among ACT Teleconferencing, a subordinated creditor and Silicon Valley Bank, filed with the SEC, defines the "Standstill Period" as any insolvency proceeding, plus any period starting from a blockage notice by the bank and running until the earlier of 180 days where the bank's notice specifies a payment default, or 120 days for any other event of default. The junior creditor's protections are two sentences: no standstill may run more than 180 days in any 365-day period, and there must be a consecutive 185-day stretch in each 365-day period with no standstill in effect. A separate exhibit states flatly: "There shall be no prepayment of the Subordinated Indebtedness." (SEC EDGAR.)

Translate that into a seller's year. The buyer trips a covenant — not a missed payment, just a leverage ratio in a soft quarter. The bank issues a notice. Your interest stops for up to 120 days, you may not accelerate, you may not sue, and the unpaid interest earns you nothing extra. Twice in a year and most of the note's cash flow has been deferred by a party you never negotiated with. The subordination agreement, not the note, is the document to spend your lawyer's hours on.

Pricing a seller note: a worked example

The company below is invented. These are arithmetic on a hypothetical, not a quote, a projection, or a description of any transaction.

A business does $5 million of normalized EBITDA. The buyer offers 6.0x, or $30 million, structured as $24 million of cash at closing and a $6 million seller note: five years, 6% stated interest, interest-only for two years, then principal amortized evenly across years three to five, fully subordinated to a $15 million senior facility.

The nominal cash flow on the note is $7.44 million. Now discount it at what the risk is actually worth to you — an unsecured junior claim on a leveraged company you no longer run, which you cannot sell and cannot demand early.

How you value the note Present value Total consideration Effective multiple
At its own 6% coupon (what the headline assumes) $6.00M $30.0M 6.0x
Discounted at 10% $5.24M $29.2M 5.8x
Discounted at 14% $4.61M $28.6M 5.7x
Note is never repaid $0 $24.0M 4.8x

Two readings. First, the discount on a performing note is small — 6.0x becomes 5.7x even at a punishing 14% — so a seller note is not automatically a bad trade. Second, the downside is not a discount, it is a cliff: if the business stumbles, the deal you signed is 4.8x. Ask whether the cash at closing alone is a number you would accept. Run your own EBITDA through the business valuation calculator to see the band before a structure is laid over it, and read how PE firms value a company for the adjustments that set the EBITDA the multiple applies to.

The stated rate is not your return

A 6% coupon on a subordinated, unsecured, non-prepayable, standstill-blocked five-year note is not 6%. It is 6% minus the probability-weighted cost of not being paid, minus the option value you gave up when you agreed you could not accelerate. Ask it in reverse: at what rate would you lend $6 million, unsecured, junior to a bank, to a buyer whose decisions you will not control for five years? That is the rate the note should carry, and if the buyer will not pay it, the buyer is telling you the price is too high.

The other deferred pieces of a price are not interchangeable with it. An earnout pays only if the business hits targets somebody else controls. Rollover equity converts when the sponsor sells, on the sponsor's timetable. A seller note is the only one with a fixed amount, a coupon and a maturity date — the most honest of the three, and the one most worth negotiating hard, because its weaknesses sit in the fine print rather than in the concept.

The tax arrives on a different schedule than the cash

A sale with a note is an installment sale: "a sale of property where you receive at least one payment after the tax year of the sale." Gain "must be reported under the installment method" unless you elect out by the due date of the return, including extensions — and once made, that election "can be revoked only with IRS approval" (IRS Publication 537). Three consequences a seller should price before signing, all of them from the same publication:

  • Understated interest gets recharacterized. If the contract does not provide adequate stated interest under section 483 or 1274, part of the principal is treated as interest — ordinary income, not capital gain. A buyer pushing a below-market coupon in exchange for a higher face amount is proposing a tax shift, whether or not either side realizes it. For seller financing of $7,296,700 or less, the test rate "can't be more than 9%, compounded semiannually."
  • Over $5 million of paper carries an interest charge. Where a non-dealer's installment obligations arising in the tax year and outstanding at the close of that year total more than $5 million, and the sales price was over $150,000, the seller must pay interest to the IRS on the deferred tax. The rate is the federal underpayment rate — 7% per year, compounded daily, for individuals in the quarter beginning 1 October 2026 (IRS, IR-2026-98). A $6 million note crosses that line. A $4.9 million note with the balance taken in cash does not.
  • You cannot quietly borrow against it. Under the pledge rule, where the selling price exceeds $150,000, net proceeds of debt secured by the installment obligation may be treated as a payment on it — accelerating the tax without producing the sale. The note is not a liquid asset.

None of that makes an installment sale a bad outcome; deferring gain across years is often the point. It makes the note's after-tax value different from its face, and different again depending on size. The after-tax ledger on a private equity sale works through how much of a headline number survives tax once the structure is applied. Price yours with your own tax advisor before the letter of intent, not after.

What to negotiate, in order

  1. A smaller note. Everything below is damage control. Trade length, trade coupon, trade the amortization schedule — but move dollars to closing first, because a dollar at closing has no subordination agreement attached to it.
  2. The subordination terms, not the lien. Cap the total blockage days per year, require a consecutive clear period, bar repeat notices for the same default, and make blocked interest accrue at a default rate rather than vanish.
  3. A real coupon, paid currently. Current-pay interest is cash in your hand and an early warning system. Interest that accrues to principal tells you nothing until it is too late to act.
  4. Acceleration on the events that matter. A sale of the business, a change of control, a dividend to the buyer's equity, a termination of your employment without cause. Without acceleration, any of those can happen while your note sits outstanding.
  5. Covenants that protect the cash flow, not the collateral. Limits on additional debt senior to you, on distributions, and on management fees paid to the buyer's sponsor. A note behind a balance sheet the buyer can keep adding to is a note that gets diluted after you signed.
  6. Information rights. Monthly or quarterly financials and covenant compliance certificates. You are a lender now; lenders get reporting.

All six belong in the letter of intent. By the time the subordination agreement is circulating, the bank is a party to the negotiation and your leverage has gone. Frequently asked questions

What is seller financing in a business sale? The seller accepts a promissory note from the buyer for part of the purchase price instead of cash at closing, and is repaid with interest over a set term. It bridges a financing gap: the buyer's equity and senior debt do not reach the price, so the seller funds the difference.

How much of a sale price is typically seller financed? In the lower middle market it is usually the minority of the price. The IBBA and M&A Source Q2 2026 survey put average cash at close at 83% to 92% of transaction value, with seller financing under 10% of most deals in that quarter.

What interest rate should a seller note carry? Whatever rate compensates an unsecured lender ranked behind a bank, which is well above what a bank charges. There is also a tax floor: if the stated rate is too low, the IRS treats part of the principal as interest income under the unstated-interest rules, so a below-market coupon costs you capital gain treatment as well as yield.

Is a seller note better than an earnout? Usually, because the amount is fixed and does not depend on the buyer's post-closing decisions. The trade is that a note is a credit risk on a leveraged company, while an earnout is a performance risk. Both are deferred; only one has a maturity date.

Can I avoid seller financing altogether? Sometimes, by changing the buyer rather than the clause. A structure financed against the business at a valuation set the way a sponsor would set it can pay the value at closing instead of asking the seller to fund the gap. The bottom line

A seller note means the deal did not fully fund. The buyer's equity plus the bank's loan did not reach your price, and the gap was closed by asking you to lend it back on terms a bank would decline. That can be a reasonable trade when the discount is small and the covenants are real. It is a bad trade when the note is large, the subordination is open-ended, and the cash at closing alone would not have been an acceptable price.

The underlying problem is the financing, not the paperwork. An Independent Buyout makes your own management team the buyer and finances the purchase against the business itself at a valuation set the way a sponsor would set it, so the value is funded at closing rather than borrowed back from the seller.
