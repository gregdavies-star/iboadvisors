Seller Financing in a Business Sale

By Michael Chasen · Updated October 20, 2026

The short answer

Seller financing is the part of the purchase price the buyer does not pay you at closing and does not borrow from a bank. You lend it to them. The instrument is a seller note: a promise to pay a fixed principal amount, with interest, on a schedule, usually over three to seven years, almost always ranked behind the buyer’s senior lender.

It exists because the price and the available capital rarely meet. In the second quarter of 2026, GF Data’s middle-market reports put the average valuation at 7.0x trailing adjusted EBITDA and the average total debt on a platform transaction at 2.9x, down from 3.4x the quarter before (GF Data, an ACG company, August 27, 2026). Four turns of EBITDA have to come from somewhere other than the bank. Sponsor equity covers most of it. A seller note, an earnout, or rollover equity covers the rest — and each of the three leaves a different amount of your price exposed to a company you no longer control.

A seller note is the least bad of the three. It has a face amount that does not move, a coupon, and a maturity date. An earnout has none of those. But “least bad” is not “cash,” and the terms that decide whether you are actually paid are buried in two documents most sellers read last: the subordination agreement and the indemnity article.

Where the note sits in the capital stack

The company below is invented and the numbers are arithmetic on a hypothetical — not a quote, a projection, or a description of any transaction.

A business does $6 million of adjusted EBITDA and is bought at the 7.0x market average, so the enterprise value is $42 million. The buyer’s lender advances 2.9x, the current average. The seller carries 14% of the price as a note.

Source of funds Amount Share of price
Senior acquisition debt (2.9x EBITDA) $17,400,000 41.4%
Sponsor cash equity $18,600,000 44.3%
Seller note $6,000,000 14.3%
Enterprise value $42,000,000 100%

Cash to the seller at closing, before fees and any debt payoff, is $36 million. The remaining $6 million is an unsecured-in-practice claim against a company now carrying $17.4 million of senior debt it did not have the week before. How private equity actually finances a buyout walks that stack line by line, and what a private equity buyout is and how it works sets out which buyer types ask for a seller note and which do not. In a management buyout, where there is no sponsor equity at all, the seller note is usually several times this size.

The rate is where the money quietly moves

Price the hypothetical note at 7%, which is the sort of coupon a buyer will propose and few sellers argue with. Then look at what institutional capital charges for the identical position in the identical capital stack: GF Data put average subordinated debt pricing at 12.1% in the first half of 2025, the highest since the fourth quarter of 2023 (GulfStar Group, citing GF Data).

On the $6 million note above, the gap between 7% and 12.1% is about $306,000 a year, or roughly $1.5 million over a five-year term. That is not a swindle and it is not apples to apples — a mezzanine lender gets covenants, security, information rights and often warrants that a seller note does not. But it is the right way to see what you are doing: you are supplying junior capital at a price no professional junior lender would accept, and the discount is a real transfer from your side of the table to the buyer’s. If you are going to be the mezzanine lender, negotiate the coupon like one.

There is a floor you cannot go under. If the note’s stated interest is too low, the IRS recharacterizes part of the principal as interest under section 483 or as original issue discount under section 1274 — converting capital gain into ordinary income. The test rate is tied to the applicable federal rate for the note’s term: short-term for three years or less, mid-term for over three and up to nine, long-term beyond that (IRS Publication 537). The mid-term rate for October 2026 is 4.61% compounded annually, under Revenue Ruling 2026-19 (IRS, applicable federal rates). A below-market rate does not save the buyer anything the IRS respects; it just moves your money into a worse tax bucket.

Subordination is the term that decides whether you get paid

The note is a contract between you and the buyer. The subordination agreement is a contract between you and the buyer’s bank, and it is the one that controls. Four provisions in it matter more than the interest rate:

  • Payment blockage. On a senior default — including a covenant default, not just a missed payment — the lender can stop payments on your note. Negotiate the number of blockage periods, their maximum length, and the minimum gap between them. “One period of up to 180 days in any 360 days” is a materially different instrument from an open-ended block.
  • Standstill on remedies. How long you must wait before you can sue or accelerate after the buyer defaults on your note. A standstill with no outside date means you hold a claim you can never enforce.
  • Permitted payments. Whether scheduled interest continues during a blockage, and whether blocked interest accrues and is paid later or is simply lost.
  • Cap on senior debt. Without one, the buyer can refinance into a larger facility and push you further down without asking you.

Then read the indemnity article, because in most deals your note is the buyer’s real escrow. A right of setoff lets the buyer reduce note payments by the amount of any indemnity claim it asserts. An unqualified setoff right turns a payment dispute into a self-help remedy: the buyer stops paying and you are the one who has to sue. The usual fix is to require that claims be finally determined, or at minimum escrowed, before any reduction, and to carve the setoff out of the first scheduled payments.

The tax: what the installment method defers, and what it does not

A seller note generally qualifies for the installment method, which is the one genuine advantage it has over cash. You report gain as payments arrive rather than all at once. Each payment splits three ways — interest, a tax-free return of basis, and gain — with the taxable share set by the gross profit percentage, gross profit divided by contract price (IRS Publication 537).

Run it on the same hypothetical, as a stock sale with an adjusted basis of $2 million:

Installment calculation Amount
Selling price $42,000,000
Adjusted basis $2,000,000
Gross profit $40,000,000
Gross profit percentage 95.24%
Payment received in the year of sale $36,000,000
Gain recognized in the year of sale $34,285,714
Gain deferred into later years $5,714,286

Four things the installment method does not do, each of which has surprised sellers:

  • It does not defer depreciation recapture. In an asset sale, recapture income is reported in full in the year of sale “whether or not an installment payment was received that year.” Only the gain above the recapture amount goes on the installment method. An equipment-heavy business can therefore owe ordinary-income tax in year one on value it will not collect for five.
  • It does not survive a pledge. Borrow against the note and the net debt proceeds are treated as a payment on it, accelerating the gain. The rule applies where the selling price exceeds $150,000, which is every deal at this size. You cannot quietly monetize the note to fix the liquidity it cost you.
  • It does not survive a funded escrow. If the balance of the purchase price is simply deposited into escrow, the buyer’s obligation is paid in full and the sale cannot be reported on the installment method — unless the escrow imposes a substantial restriction on your right to the proceeds.
  • It does not survive a quick related-party resale. If you sell to a related person who disposes of the property within two years, your remaining gain is accelerated.

You can also elect out and pay the whole tax in year one. The election is made by the due date, including extensions, for the return covering the year of sale, by reporting the sale on Form 8949 or Form 4797 rather than Form 6252; revoking it later requires IRS consent. Electing out is the right answer more often than people expect — notably if you think rates are going up, or if you doubt the note will be paid and would rather take the loss against a gain already recognized.

The $5 million line most sellers at this size cross

Above a threshold, the government charges you for the deferral. Section 453A applies where the sales price exceeds $150,000 and the face amount of installment obligations that arose during the year and are still outstanding at year end exceeds $5,000,000. Only the excess is hit: the applicable percentage is the amount over $5 million divided by the total face amount outstanding. The charge equals that percentage, times the deferred tax liability (unrecognized gain at the top applicable rate), times the section 6621(a)(2) underpayment rate (26 U.S. Code § 453A). That rate is 7% for the quarter beginning October 1, 2026 (IRS, IR-2026-98).

On the hypothetical, with a $6 million note outstanding at year end:

Section 453A charge Amount
Face amount outstanding at year end $6,000,000
Applicable percentage ($1,000,000 ÷ $6,000,000) 16.67%
Deferred gain × 20% top long-term capital gains rate $1,142,857
× applicable percentage $190,476
× 7% underpayment rate = annual interest charge $13,333

That is about 1.2% a year on $1.14 million of tax you have not yet had to pay. Worth knowing about, worth telling your CPA about before you sign rather than in April — and not, on its own, a reason to refuse a seller note. State tax and the net investment income tax sit on top of the underlying liability and change the arithmetic; this is the federal mechanic, not advice on your return.

What to negotiate, in order

  1. The size. Everything else is downstream. Price the deal on the cash at closing and ask whether that number alone is acceptable, exactly as you should with an earnout.
  2. The subordination terms. Blockage length and frequency, a standstill outside date, and a cap on senior debt.
  3. Setoff. Final determination or escrow before any reduction, and no setoff against the first year’s payments.
  4. The coupon. Benchmark it against subordinated debt pricing, not against the AFR floor.
  5. Security and reporting. A second-lien stock pledge is worth more than it looks: it is leverage in a renegotiation even when the senior lender has blocked enforcement. Quarterly financials and covenant certificates mean you learn about trouble before a missed payment.
  6. Acceleration triggers. Change of control, sale of a division, a dividend recapitalization. If the sponsor takes money out, your note should be paid first or at the same time.

Frequently asked questions

How much of the purchase price is usually seller financed?

It varies more by buyer type than by industry. In a sponsor deal at this size, a seller note is typically a single-digit to mid-teens percentage of the price, because institutional equity is doing the heavy lifting. In a management buyout, where the team has no fund behind it, the seller note is frequently the largest single piece of the capital stack. The comparison of buyout types sets out which structures lean on it hardest.

Is a seller note better than an earnout?

Almost always, yes. A seller note is a fixed amount owed on a date, with interest; an earnout is a contingent claim measured against a business somebody else is running. The one advantage an earnout has is that it carries no credit risk you can price in advance — which is another way of saying you cannot price it at all.

What happens to the note if the company is sold again?

Whatever the note says. Without a change-of-control acceleration clause, your note can follow the company into the next owner’s capital structure, behind a bigger senior facility. This is a drafting question, not a market convention, and it is routinely left out.

Can I take security for a seller note?

Sometimes, in a subordinated form: a second lien on assets, or a pledge of the buyer’s equity in the acquisition vehicle. The senior lender will restrict when you can enforce it. A personal guarantee from a sponsor is rare; from an individual or management buyer, it is worth asking for.

Does a seller note reduce my sale price?

Economically, yes, by the difference between its face amount and its present value at a rate that reflects the risk. A $6 million note at 7% behind $17.4 million of senior debt is not worth $6 million. Discount it yourself before you compare two offers, and run the underlying business through the business valuation calculator so you know what the headline multiple is being applied to in the first place.

The bottom line

A seller note is you, financing your own exit, on terms a professional lender would reject, behind a bank that can switch your payments off. Sometimes that is the right trade: it bridges a real valuation gap, it defers tax, and it is the only one of the three deferral tools whose amount cannot be argued down later. But it should be priced, secured and documented as the loan it is, not accepted as a rounding item in the last week of a process.

The deeper question is why the gap exists. A seller note appears when the buyer’s capital cannot reach your number. An Independent Buyout changes who the buyer is: your own employees, through a trust, financed against the company’s cash flow at a valuation set by independent appraisal rather than by a fund’s return model. A seller note is usually part of that structure too — but the equity on the other side belongs to the people running the business, not to a sponsor whose exit clock starts the day you sign. The IBO versus private equity numbers set the two side by side, and how PE firms value a company covers the adjustments that decide the price the note is a slice of.
