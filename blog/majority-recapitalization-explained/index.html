Majority Recapitalization Explained: Selling Control and Keeping a Stake

By Michael Chasen · Updated October 8, 2026

A majority recapitalization is a sale of control. An investor - almost always a private equity or growth equity fund - buys more than half your company for cash, you reinvest part of your proceeds into the new ownership entity, and you keep running the business while someone else holds the board. It is marketed as a middle path between selling everything and selling nothing. It is closer to the first than most owners realize, because the thing that changes hands is authority, and authority does not come back.

What a majority recapitalization actually is

In a majority recap, existing shareholders sell more than 50% of the company to an outside investor - most commonly a private equity or growth equity firm - and the primary goal of the transaction is shareholder liquidity rather than growth capital, as Software Equity Group's breakdown of recapitalization types puts it. Control shifts to the new majority holder. The seller retains a minority position, usually by rolling a slice of the sale proceeds into the buyer's acquisition entity rather than by simply keeping old shares.

That last detail matters more than the headline percentage. The law firm Koley Jessen sets out the standard mechanics in its overview of tax-deferred rollover structures: the buyer forms a new holding company, the founders contribute target equity for Newco equity, and the sponsor contributes cash - with the holdco borrowing against the operating business to fund the rest. You are not keeping your old shares. You are buying into a new, levered company alongside the fund.

Majority recap, minority recap, leveraged recap: three different transactions

The word "recapitalization" only means the capital structure changed. It says nothing about who ends up in charge, which is why owners get three unrelated proposals under one label.

Structure What is sold Who controls afterward Primary purpose
Minority recapitalization Under 50% of the equity, to a new investor You Partial liquidity, growth capital, or both
Majority recapitalization More than 50% of the equity, to a new investor The investor Shareholder liquidity
Leveraged or dividend recapitalization Nothing - the company borrows and distributes the cash You (ownership does not change) Liquidity without diluting anyone
Full sale 100% The buyer Exit

A minority recapitalization and a majority recap are not two sizes of the same deal. A minority investor pays a non-control price and lives with whatever protections it negotiated; a majority investor pays a control price and writes the rules. The gap between "49%" and "51%" is not two points of ownership, it is the entire question of who approves the budget.

Recapitalization vs sale: what is genuinely different

Three things, and only three. You stay - a recap is underwritten on the assumption that you keep operating the business, and the retention terms are set at signing and hard to unwind. Part of your consideration is equity, not cash - a smaller number plus a position in a company that now carries acquisition debt, worth whatever it is worth at an exit you do not schedule, which makes everything true of rollover equity and the second bite truer here, because the rolled amount is larger. The governance changes immediately - the shareholders' agreement carries a consent list covering debt, acquisitions, senior hires, budgets, distributions and any sale, and the investor sits on the approving side of all of it. Owners who assume a retained stake buys them a say should read what a minority position actually controls, because after a majority recap that minority position is theirs.

What is not different: price discovery, diligence, the quality-of-earnings exercise, the escrow, the reps and warranties. A recap is a full sale process with a reinvestment stapled to the back of it. Budget the same time and the same fees.

The arithmetic on an invented $5 million EBITDA company

Numbers make this concrete. The company below does not exist and is not based on any transaction; the figures are invented so the structure can be followed end to end.

Assume $5,000,000 of normalized EBITDA and no funded debt. GF Data's contributed deal set, compiled by enterprise value band, puts $25-50 million transactions at 6.8x in 2025 year to date, so price it at 6.8x, or $34,000,000 of enterprise value. With no debt, that is $34,000,000 of equity value - yours today.

You agree to roll 20% of it. The deal then funds like this:

Source Amount Share of the new capital
Senior debt on the operating company, at 3.5x EBITDA $17,500,000 51.5%
Your rollover into the new holding company $6,800,000 20.0%
Sponsor cash equity $9,700,000 28.5%
Total purchase price $34,000,000 100%

You receive $27,200,000 before fees, escrow and tax. The company you still partly own now carries $17,500,000 of debt it did not have on Friday, serviced out of the same cash flow that used to be yours. That is not a quirk of this example; it is how private equity finances a buyout in every structure.

Why the percentage you roll is not the percentage you own

Here is the part that surprises people, and it cuts in the seller's favor. You rolled 20% of enterprise value. Post-close equity is only $16,500,000, because debt funded the rest. Your $6,800,000 is 41.2% of it.

This is a documented mechanic, not a rounding artifact. PCE Companies works through the same effect in an equity rollover: a management group rolling what looks like 12.5% of ownership, $6 million, ends up holding 21.4% of a $28 million post-transaction equity base, because $22.5 million of new debt shrank the equity denominator. Leverage inflates the rollover percentage.

Two cautions keep that from being free money. First, the same PCE analysis notes that debt used to finance the deal has priority over rollover equity, and that buyers often structure their own equity as preferred stock with a minimum distribution paid before common holders - so ask whether your rolled shares are pari passu with the sponsor's or sit underneath them. Second, 41.2% of the common is not 41.2% of the exit. Run the invented company forward five years and the point lands.

Say the sponsor's $9,700,000 came in as preferred carrying an 8% accruing return, so roughly $14,300,000 comes off the top at a year-five exit. Sell then at $45,000,000 with $12,000,000 of debt left and the $33,000,000 of equity leaves an $18,700,000 residual; your 41.2% is about $7,700,000, which on top of the $27,200,000 at close beats selling outright today by roughly $900,000 - before tax, and before anything the $27,200,000 could have earned elsewhere. Sell instead at $60,000,000 with $8,000,000 of debt and your share is about $15,500,000, for a total near $42,700,000.

The rollover is a leveraged bet on growth you no longer control. It pays well when the company compounds and pays almost nothing when it merely holds. Before you accept any of these numbers as yours, generate your own range with the business valuation calculator and a defensible normalized EBITDA figure.

The tax question: why the rollover is usually not taxed at close

Structured properly, the rolled portion is not a taxable event; the cash portion is. Under 26 U.S. Code § 351, no gain or loss is recognized when a person transfers property to a corporation solely in exchange for its stock, provided the transferors are in control of the corporation immediately afterward - "control" being defined by § 368(c) as 80% of combined voting power plus 80% of the shares of every other class. The test is applied to the transferring group as a whole, which is why the sponsor's cash contribution is part of the same transaction. Where the acquisition vehicle is a partnership or LLC instead, § 721(a) does the same work: no gain or loss on a contribution of property in exchange for a partnership interest.

Section 351(b) is the catch. If you also receive money or other property, gain is recognized up to the amount of cash plus the fair market value of that other property. In the example above, the $27,200,000 of cash is boot and is taxable now; the $6,800,000 of rolled equity is not, and carries your old basis forward into the new shares. Deferral is not forgiveness - the tax arrives at the second exit, on an amount nobody can predict today.

None of this works by default. Koley Jessen also notes why sponsors want the rollover in the first place: it aligns interests, bridges valuation gaps, and is cheaper than cash because it functions as seller financing. Have tax counsel confirm the structure before the letter of intent, not during documentation.

Leveraged and dividend recapitalizations: liquidity with no new shareholder

The third structure in the table is the one owners rarely get pitched and the one sponsors use constantly on companies they already own. Nobody buys anything: the company borrows and distributes the proceeds to its existing shareholders. The cap table is unchanged; the balance sheet is not.

The scale is worth knowing, because it tells you what happens to a business after a majority recap. Private-equity-backed companies issued $70.2 billion of leveraged loans earmarked for dividend recapitalizations through the first eleven months of 2025, per PitchBook LCD data reported by ABF Journal - with broadly syndicated sponsor dividend payouts reaching $43.6 billion by early December, 24% above 2021's $35.1 billion. The same data puts average pre-dividend leverage at 4.2x debt to EBITDA and average pro forma leverage at 5.2x, a full turn added to fund a distribution, with 22% of those deals levered at 6x or more afterward.

Read that twice before rolling equity: the sponsor that buys 80% of your company may add a turn of leverage two years later to return capital to its own investors, and your rolled stake sits behind that new debt. For an owner who still holds 100%, the same structure can be the cleanest liquidity available - no investor, no consent list, no exit clock, just a loan and a distribution sized to what the cash flow supports in a bad year rather than a good one.

When a majority recap fits, and when it does not

It fits an owner who wants most of the money out now, is genuinely comfortable reporting to a board, and believes the next five years will beat the last five - the rollover is the cheapest way to keep exposure to that without funding it personally. It fits a company that needs acquisition capital faster than retained earnings can supply it.

It is the wrong structure in three situations. If you want liquidity but not a new boss, a majority recap always delivers both; the real question is how much you can sell and still keep control, and the answer lives in the consent list rather than the percentage. If you are close to done operating, a deal priced on your continued involvement is a bad thing to sign twelve months before you want to stop. And if a bidder is closing a valuation gap by asking you to roll more, it has moved consideration from the certain column to the uncertain one without raising the offer.

Where the Independent Buyout sits

The trade in a majority recap is control for cash. An Independent Buyout is the attempt to get the cash without making the trade: the company is purchased through a trust structure financed against its own cash flow, at a valuation set the way a sponsor would set one, with the existing leadership team continuing to run it and no fund holding the consent list or the exit clock. It is the same leveraged arithmetic applied to a buyer who is not going to flip the company in five years, and depending on the seller's facts a qualifying sale can be structured for deferral under 26 U.S. Code § 1042 - worth confirming with a tax advisor rather than assuming. Frequently asked questions

What percentage is sold in a majority recapitalization? More than 50%, by definition. In practice the figure is set by how much the seller is willing or required to roll rather than by a market convention, and a sponsor will generally push the rollover up because every rolled dollar is a dollar of equity it does not have to fund.

What is the difference between a recapitalization and a sale? In a sale you exchange the whole position for cash and leave. In a recapitalization you exchange most of it for cash, keep a minority interest in a newly levered company, and keep working. The process, the diligence and the fees are the same; the consideration and the aftermath are not.

Do I pay tax on the equity I roll over? Generally not at close, if the transaction is structured under § 351 or § 721 - but the cash you receive is boot and is taxable immediately, and the deferred gain on the rolled shares comes due when they are eventually sold. Confirm the structure with tax counsel before signing the letter of intent.

Is a leveraged recapitalization the same as a majority recap? No. A leveraged or dividend recap adds debt and distributes cash without anyone buying equity; ownership and control are unchanged. A majority recap sells control to a new investor. They are often confused because both are described as "taking money off the table".

Can I do a majority recap and still run the company? Yes, and that is usually the plan - but running it and controlling it are different. Operating authority is delegated to you under a shareholders' agreement that reserves the significant decisions to the investor, and that delegation can be revisited.

The bottom line

A majority recapitalization is the best-priced way to convert most of a private company into cash while staying in the operating seat, and it is sold on the half of that sentence owners like. The half that decides the outcome is the retained stake: what it is worth depends on leverage you did not choose, a preference that pays first, and an exit someone else schedules. Price the deal on the cash at close and treat the rollover as an option, not as consideration.
