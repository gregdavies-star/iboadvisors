Majority Recapitalization: The 70/30 Deal

By Michael Chasen · Updated October 12, 2026

A majority recapitalization is a sale in which an outside investor buys control of your company - commonly 51% to 80% of the equity, which is why it gets called a 70/30 deal - and you reinvest, or "roll," the rest into the buyer's new holding company. You take most of your money as cash at closing, you keep a minority stake in whatever the company becomes, and from the day the deal signs there is a new controlling shareholder.

"Recapitalization" is the word the industry uses for it, and it is doing a lot of work. It describes the tax architecture of the transaction accurately. It describes the change in who runs the company not at all.

Majority recap, minority recap, full sale

Three structures get discussed in the same meeting and only one line really separates them: where the 50% mark falls.

Minority recap Majority recap Full sale
Equity sold 20-49% 51-80% 100%
Who controls the board You The sponsor The buyer
Cash at close Partial Most of your equity value All of it
What you keep A majority of the original company A minority of a newly leveraged holding company Nothing
Who sets the exit clock You, mostly The sponsor's fund Not your problem

If you are still deciding which side of 50% you want to be on, the minority recapitalization case is the mirror image of this one, and what the percentage actually decides works through the governance documents that matter more than the percentage itself.

Why it is called a recapitalization at all

The label comes from how the deal is built, and the reason is tax. A sponsor could simply buy 75% of your shares. That works, but it generally produces no step-up in the company's inside tax basis, which is why private equity firms often avoid it, according to Koley Jessen's review of tax-deferred rollover structures.

The common alternative is a holding company. You and the sponsor both contribute to a new entity - you contribute target equity, the sponsor contributes cash - and the entity ends up owning the operating business. Under 26 U.S. Code § 351, "no gain or loss shall be recognized if property is transferred to a corporation by one or more persons solely in exchange for stock in such corporation and immediately after the exchange such person or persons are in control." Your rolled slice rides on that provision; the cash you take is "boot" and is taxed at closing. Where the target is an LLC, a "drop-down" structure is often the most tax-efficient route available. The structure that is cleanest for the sponsor is not automatically the one that is cleanest for you - a question for your own tax advisor before the letter of intent, not after.

The practical takeaway is narrow but important: the word "recapitalization" tells you something true about the paperwork and nothing at all about control. A 75% recapitalization is a change of control.

What the two bites actually pay

Percentages get quoted constantly in these conversations and dollars almost never do. Here is the arithmetic on an invented company - not a quote, a projection, or a description of any transaction.

The business earns $5 million of normalized EBITDA, prices at 7.0x for $35 million of enterprise value, and carries $5 million of existing debt, so the equity is worth $30 million. You sell 75% for cash and roll 25% - $7.5 million.

Now look at how the buyer funds it. The sponsor needs $22.5 million of cash for you, $5 million to retire the existing debt, and roughly $1.5 million of fees: $29 million of uses. It places new debt on the company itself at 4.0x EBITDA, or $20 million. Your $7.5 million rolls in as equity. The sponsor's own cheque is therefore about $9 million.

Which means the post-closing equity of the holding company is $16.5 million, and your $7.5 million is 45% of it. You sold three-quarters of your company and you own nearly half of the thing that replaced it. This is not a drafting error; it is what leverage does to an equity cheque, and the same effect appears in worked examples elsewhere - PCE Companies shows a 12.5% pre-transaction stake becoming a 21.4% post-transaction interest for exactly this reason.

It also explains why your ownership percentage is not where the negotiation happens. Three things sit between that 45% and your second cheque:

  • The preference stack. Sponsor equity frequently comes in as preferred stock carrying a minimum distribution paid before common holders (PCE Companies). Your 45% is 45% of what remains after the sponsor's capital and its accrued return come out first.
  • The acquisition debt. It has priority over rollover equity in the capital structure. The $20 million gets repaid before any equity holder sees anything.
  • The clock. Sponsor holding periods typically run four to six years, and your stake is illiquid for all of them. The second bite happens on the fund's timetable, not yours.

Run the exit five years out, with the sponsor's $9 million carrying a 1x preference accruing at 8%, which compounds to about $13.2 million. Debt has amortized from $20 million to $10 million.

Exit scenario at year 5 Good case Flat case
EBITDA $7.0M $6.0M
Exit multiple 7.0x 6.0x
Enterprise value $49.0M $36.0M
Less net debt ($10.0M) ($10.0M)
Equity proceeds $39.0M $26.0M
Less sponsor preference ($13.2M) ($13.2M)
Common pool $25.8M $12.8M
Your 45% second bite $11.7M $5.8M

So the deal is $22.5 million now plus somewhere between roughly $6 million and $12 million in five years, against $30 million today for a clean full sale. In the good case you come out roughly $4 million ahead. In the flat case - a company that grew 20% over five years into a softer multiple - you come out slightly behind, having carried five years of leverage risk to get there. Neither number is a prediction. They are the two ends of the range any owner should price before agreeing to roll, and the inputs are your own: run your EBITDA and a defensible multiple through the business valuation calculator, then ask the sponsor to model the same exit on their preference terms rather than on a pro rata split.

How often sellers are actually asked to roll

Often, and more than they used to be. Goodwin's private equity deal database shows "a clear and steady upward trend in the inclusion of rollover equity in acquisition agreements" in mid-market transactions valued at $25 million to $500 million, increasing from 46% in 2020 to 57% in 2023. That data set excludes healthcare services deals, which the firm notes typically have higher rates of rollover still - a pattern consistent with what owners encounter in healthcare services roll-ups.

So a rollover request is not a signal that your deal is unusual. It is the base case for more than half of mid-market deals. What varies enormously is the size, the valuation it is struck at, and the terms attached.

What changes the morning after close

At 51% the sponsor appoints the board, and the board appoints you. Everything downstream follows from that. Board representation for a rolling shareholder generally requires a minimum of 15-20% ownership (PCE Companies), and a seat is not a vote that decides anything on its own. Expect a consent list covering budgets, capital expenditure above a threshold, new debt, hiring and firing at the executive level, acquisitions, and the timing and form of any future sale - the full veto list is longer than most owners expect, and it applies to the 45% holder exactly as it applies to a 10% one.

Two mechanics deserve attention before signing. Add-on acquisitions funded with new equity dilute your stake unless you have preemptive rights or the capital to participate. And drag-along provisions mean that when the sponsor sells, you sell, on their terms. What happens in years one through seven of a sponsor's hold is the honest preview of the job.

When a majority recap is the right answer

  • You want most of your net worth out of one asset and you still want to run the company. That is the problem this structure was built to solve, and it solves it.
  • You genuinely believe the next five years are bigger than the last five, and you want exposure to that with someone else's balance sheet funding it.
  • You need capital you cannot raise alone - an acquisition programme, a facility, a market entry - and the sponsor brings both the money and experience doing it.

When it isn't

  • You want to keep running the company your way. You will not be. Price the deal on the cash at close and treat the rollover as an option, because that is what it is.
  • Your second bite is doing the work of making the first bite acceptable. If the deal only clears your number when the optimistic exit case lands, the deal does not clear your number.
  • You are within a few years of wanting out entirely. A four-to-six-year hold plus a sale process on the other side is a long runway.

There is a third position that rarely gets raised in the same meeting. An Independent Buyout uses a trust-based financing structure to buy some or all of your equity without putting an outside financial sponsor on your board at all, which means no forced rollover, no preference stack ahead of you, and no fund clock. Depending on your facts it may qualify for treatment under Internal Revenue Code Section 1042 - confirm that with your own tax advisor rather than assuming it. It is not right for every owner, but it belongs on the list alongside the recap, and the full ledger of selling to private equity is worth reading before choosing between them.

Frequently asked questions

What is a majority recapitalization? A transaction in which an investor acquires a controlling interest - typically 51% to 80% - in your company while you reinvest the balance of your equity into the buyer's new holding company. You receive most of your equity value in cash at closing and keep a minority position going forward.

How is a majority recap different from just selling the company? In cash terms, less than owners expect: you receive most of your equity value either way. The difference is that you keep a stake with upside and downside attached, and you stay in the business under a new controlling shareholder.

Is rollover equity in a majority recap taxable? The rolled portion is commonly structured to be tax-deferred, most often through a holding company contribution under § 351 or its partnership equivalent. Cash you receive is generally taxable at closing as boot. The structure and your entity type decide the answer, so get it confirmed on your own facts.

How much of my company will a sponsor ask me to roll? There is no single standard; what matters more is the dollar amount, the valuation it is struck at, and whether it sits behind a preference. Note that your post-closing ownership percentage will usually differ from the percentage you rolled, because acquisition debt shrinks the equity cheque that your dollars buy into.

Do I keep board control after a majority recapitalization? No. Above 50% the sponsor controls the board. A rolling shareholder may get a seat, typically at ownership levels of 15-20% or more, but a seat is not control, and the consent list in the shareholders' agreement is where real authority is allocated.

Can I negotiate the terms of the rollover rather than just the size? Yes, and it is where most of the value is. The items that move the second bite are the preference and its accrual rate, anti-dilution and preemptive rights on add-on acquisitions, tag-along rights, and whether you have any put right if the hold period runs long.

The bottom line

A majority recapitalization is a control sale with a reinvestment attached. The cash at close is real and usually generous; the second bite is an option on someone else's decisions, priced behind their preference and their debt, exercisable on their schedule. Value the two separately, insist on seeing the exit modelled on the actual preference terms rather than a pro rata split, and decide whether you would accept the deal if the rollover turned out to be worth nothing. If the answer is yes, it is a good deal. If the answer is no, you are being paid in optimism.
