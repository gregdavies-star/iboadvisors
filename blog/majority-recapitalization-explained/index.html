Majority Recapitalization: What You Keep

By Michael Chasen · Updated October 3, 2026

A majority recapitalization is a sale of control dressed in a gentler word. An investor — almost always a private equity sponsor — buys 51% to 80% of your company, you reinvest the balance of your proceeds as rollover equity in the new holding company, and you keep running the business with a new board above you. You are told you "still own 30% of the company." That sentence is the one to take apart, because the 30% you keep is not 30% of the company you sold.

The arithmetic below uses an invented company. It is not a quote, a projection, or a description of any transaction — just the math a seller should be able to run before the first meeting.

The three things called a recapitalization

Owners hear "recap" and assume it means one structure. It covers three, and they sit at opposite ends of the control spectrum.

Structure Equity sold Who controls the board after New debt on the company
Minority recapitalization 20–49% You, subject to a consent list Sometimes
Majority recapitalization 51–80% The investor Almost always
Leveraged or dividend recapitalization None You Yes — that is the whole transaction

A minority recapitalization brings in a partner who cannot outvote you. A dividend recap brings in no partner at all: the company borrows and distributes the cash to you. A majority recapitalization is the only one of the three where you hand over the deciding vote, which is why the governance terms matter more here than in either of the others — a sponsor with a board majority does not need the consent list a minority investor has to negotiate for, because it already has the votes.

If the thing you actually want is cash without a new boss, you are looking for one of the other two structures, or for something off this list entirely. Selling part of the business and keeping control works through board seats and consent rights rather than percentages, and the full list of alternatives to selling to private equity sets out what else is available at this size.

What a majority recap actually looks like, in dollars

Take a business with $6 million of normalized EBITDA and no funded debt. GF Data put the $25M–$50M enterprise value band at 6.8x through the first nine months of 2025, with senior debt at 3.4x EBITDA and total debt at 4.0x in that same band (Capital Pad, citing GF Data). Call the purchase price 7.0x, or $42 million, and assume lenders advance 3.5x, or $21 million.

The sponsor buys 70% and you roll 30%. Here is how the money moves:

Sources and uses at close Amount
Uses — purchase of 100% of the company's equity $42.0M
Uses — buyer financing and transaction expenses $2.0M
Total uses $44.0M
Sources — senior debt at 3.5x EBITDA $21.0M
Sources — sponsor cash equity $16.1M
Sources — your rollover equity $6.9M
Total sources $44.0M

Your ledger: $42.0 million of gross proceeds, less $6.9 million rolled, leaves $35.1 million of cash at close. Sell-side advisory, legal and quality-of-earnings work at roughly 3% of enterprise value takes about $1.3 million. An indemnity escrow at 10% of the cash consideration holds back $3.5 million for eighteen months — held, not lost, but not spendable either. On a near-zero basis the cash portion carries federal tax at 20% long-term capital gains plus the 3.8% net investment income tax (IRS, Topic no. 409), roughly $8.4 million before any state tax. What you keep of the cash, after fees and federal tax, is about $25.4 million — and $3.5 million of that does not arrive for a year and a half, while the tax on it is due long before the escrow releases.

Rolling 30% is not keeping 30% of your company

This is the sentence that costs sellers the most, and it is pure arithmetic.

Before the deal, the company was worth $42 million with no debt. Thirty percent of that is $12.6 million. After the deal, the holding company carries $21 million of debt, and the entire equity layer is $23.0 million — your $6.9 million plus the sponsor's $16.1 million. Thirty percent of that is $6.9 million, which is exactly what you put in.

No value disappeared; you took the difference in cash. But the character of what you hold has changed completely. You now own a minority slice of a levered equity stub, which moves several times faster than the business underneath it in both directions. That is the same mechanic that governs how private equity finances a buyout — the leverage that lifts the sponsor's return on the way up is sitting on your 30% too.

Say the plan works and EBITDA reaches $8 million in five years, with debt paid down to $12 million. What your rolled $6.9 million is worth depends almost entirely on a number nobody controls: the multiple on the day of the exit.

Exit multiple in year 5 Enterprise value Equity after $12M debt Your 30% On $6.9M rolled
5.0x $40.0M $28.0M $8.4M 1.22x
6.0x $48.0M $36.0M $10.8M 1.57x
7.0x $56.0M $44.0M $13.2M 1.91x
8.0x $64.0M $52.0M $15.6M 2.26x

A two-turn swing in the exit multiple — on identical earnings, five years out, decided by credit markets rather than by anything you do — is the difference between 1.22x and 2.26x on your money. And the downside case is not symmetric. If EBITDA merely holds at $6 million, cash generation is weaker, debt only falls to $16 million, and a 6.0x exit produces $36 million of enterprise value against $20 million of equity. Your 30% is $6.0 million: less than you rolled, five years later, in a business that did not shrink.

That asymmetry is the honest case for sizing the rollover against what you can afford to lose rather than against what the sponsor would like you to commit. Middle-market rollovers commonly land in the 10% to 30% range (Porte Brown), and the top of that range is a choice, not a requirement.

The security you roll into matters more than the percentage

"You keep 30%" says nothing about what 30% of. Sponsor equity frequently comes in as preferred stock with a liquidation preference and an accruing dividend, typically around 8%, while the rollover sits in common underneath it. Run the same good-case exit through that structure.

The sponsor's $16.1 million of preferred, accruing 8% compounding for five years, is $23.7 million by exit. Against $44.0 million of equity at a 7.0x exit, the preference comes out first, leaving $20.3 million of common. Your 30% of the common is $6.1 million — less than the $6.9 million you rolled, in the scenario where everything went right and the straight-common version of the same deal paid you $13.2 million.

The fix is a term, not a negotiation over price: roll into the same security the sponsor buys, on the same terms, in the same proportion. Sponsors do agree to this, and the ones that will not have told you something useful about how they expect the deal to go. Rollover equity and the second bite covers the rest of the terms that decide whether that stake is real, and what actually happens in years one through seven after a sponsor buys covers the monitoring fees and dividend recaps that come out of the same equity layer before you see it.

What defers and what does not

The cash portion is taxable in the year you receive it. The rolled portion can often be deferred, and the mechanism depends on what the holding company is.

  • An LLC or partnership holding company. Under 26 U.S. Code § 721(a), "no gain or loss shall be recognized to a partnership or to any of its partners in the case of a contribution of property to the partnership in exchange for an interest in the partnership." This is the common route in a majority recap, and the reason so many of them are structured through an LLC.
  • A corporate holding company. 26 U.S. Code § 351(a) gives nonrecognition when property is transferred to a corporation solely in exchange for stock and the transferors are "in control (as defined in section 368(c)) of the corporation" immediately after. Section 368(c) defines control as "at least 80 percent of the total combined voting power of all classes of stock entitled to vote and at least 80 percent of the total number of shares of all other classes of stock" — which is precisely what a seller rolling 30% alongside a controlling sponsor does not have on their own — so the corporate route turns on how the whole transferor group is assembled, and it is where rollovers get accidentally taxed.

Two practical traps sit on top of the statutes. Deferred is not forgiven — the tax arrives at the second exit, on a larger number if the plan works. And if any part of your rolled equity is subject to vesting or tied to continued employment, it can be taxed as ordinary compensation rather than capital gain unless an 83(b) election is filed inside 30 days (Porte Brown). None of this is a substitute for a transaction tax advisor; the point is to have one in the room before the structure is fixed, not after.

Recapitalization versus sale: how to tell which you are being offered

The word "recapitalization" is doing persuasive work in most of these conversations. Four questions settle what is actually on the table.

  • Who appoints the board majority after close? If it is not you, this is a control sale regardless of what the term sheet calls it.
  • Does new debt go on the company? A majority recap is funded with leverage against your balance sheet. A minority recap often is not.
  • What security does the rollover sit in, and where is it in the waterfall? Common underneath an accruing preferred is a materially different asset from pari passu units.
  • Who decides when and to whom the company is sold next? Drag-along rights mean the sponsor can sell your remaining stake on its timetable, at a price it negotiates.

If the answers are "the sponsor" four times, you are selling the company and financing part of the buyer's equity check. That can still be the right deal — it is how most lower-middle-market liquidity gets done, and the case for and against a private equity sale is genuinely two-sided. It is just not the deal the word "recapitalization" describes. Before you compare offers, build a defensible normalized EBITDA figure and run your own numbers through the business valuation calculator, so that the first valuation you see is not the buyer's.

Where this sits against the other routes

A majority recap solves one problem well: it converts most of an illiquid holding into cash while keeping an operator in the chair and a slice of the upside on the table. If your real objective is diversification and you are content to work for a board, it does the job.

It solves a different problem badly. If what you want is liquidity at private-equity scale without an outside fund setting the agenda, the structure is working against you: the leverage is the point, the board seat is the price, and the second bite is a function of someone else's exit timing. An Independent Buyout sizes financing the way a sponsor would but places the equity with a trust for the benefit of the people already running the company, so no outside fund joins the cap table and no external clock starts. The side-by-side numbers against a sponsor deal run the comparison, and every exit option compared puts both inside the wider list, including how a management buyout gets financed when the buyer is your own team.

Frequently asked questions

What is a majority recapitalization? A transaction in which an investor buys 51% to 80% of a company, usually funded partly with debt placed on the business, while the seller reinvests the balance of their proceeds as rollover equity in the acquiring holding company. Control transfers; the seller keeps a minority stake and typically keeps operating the business.

How is a majority recap different from a minority recap? By the deciding vote. A minority recap sells 20–49% and leaves you with board control subject to a negotiated consent list. A majority recap sells control outright, which is also why it pays substantially more cash at close.

Is a recapitalization a sale? A majority recapitalization is a sale of control, yes. A minority recap is a partial sale that leaves you in charge. A leveraged or dividend recap is not a sale at all — no equity changes hands, the company simply borrows and distributes.

How much equity do sellers typically roll? Middle-market rollovers commonly fall in the 10% to 30% range, though founder rollovers can run higher. Treat the percentage as negotiable and size it against what you could afford to lose, because the rolled stake sits in a levered capital structure.

Is rollover equity taxable at close? Often not, if it is structured properly — § 721 for an LLC holding company, or § 351 for a corporate one, subject to the 80% control test in § 368(c). The cash you take at closing is taxable, and vesting conditions on the rolled stake can convert capital gain into ordinary income. Get a transaction tax advisor involved before the structure is fixed.

What happens to my stake when the sponsor sells? A drag-along right lets the sponsor sell your remaining stake alongside its own, on its timetable and at its negotiated price. Your second bite is the residual equity value after debt, fees, and any preference sitting above you.

The bottom line

A majority recapitalization pays well and reads gently. The two numbers to hold onto are that your rolled stake is a slice of a levered equity layer rather than a slice of the company you sold, and that the security it sits in can quietly matter more than the percentage printed on the term sheet. Both are knowable before you sign, and neither is usually volunteered.
