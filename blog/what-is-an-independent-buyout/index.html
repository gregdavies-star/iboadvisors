What Is an Independent Buyout (IBO)? The Complete Guide for Business Owners

By Michael Chasen · Updated August 25, 2026

What is an Independent Buyout?

An Independent Buyout (IBO) is a transaction structure in which a company's existing leadership team acquires all or part of the business, financed against the company's own value and future cash flow - rather than the owner selling to an outside private equity firm or a strategic competitor. The result is a liquidity event that can look similar to a private equity sale on paper, without bringing in an outside fund, an outside board seat, or an outside veto.

In practice, that means an owner can do a private equity transaction without the Private Equity: real liquidity, a market-based valuation, and a transaction that can be structured to let the owner sell some or all of their company on terms the existing leadership team helps set - rather than terms dictated by a financial sponsor.

The two options most owners are shown - and the one they aren't

Most owners approaching a sale hear two paths from their advisors:

  • Sell to private equity. A fund buys a controlling stake, finances the deal with debt against the company, and typically requires the seller to roll a portion of their equity forward and accept board oversight - often including a veto over major decisions.
  • Sell to a strategic competitor. Usually the fastest close, and often the option most likely to fold the team, the brand, and the culture into someone else's organization.

An Independent Buyout is a third path that exists in the tax code but rarely gets mentioned in these conversations, largely because most M&A advisors and brokers are compensated by the private equity and strategic-buyer deals they broker - not by transactions where the company's own leadership is the buyer.

How an Independent Buyout actually works

An Independent Buyout has three structural components:

  1. The buyer is internal. Instead of an outside fund, the company's existing leadership team - the people who already run day-to-day operations - becomes the acquiring party.
  2. The purchase is financed against the business itself, using a mechanism that has existed in the federal tax code since 1984 (Internal Revenue Code Section 1042), rather than personal capital from the leadership team.
  3. The transaction can be structured for tax-advantaged treatment for the seller. Depending on how the deal is built and the seller's specific facts, an Independent Buyout can be structured to let an owner sell their company tax free - a materially different outcome than a standard taxable sale to a private equity fund or strategic buyer. Every situation is different, and this should be confirmed with a tax advisor before assuming a specific outcome.

Independent Buyout vs. management buyout: what's the difference?

The two structures are often confused because both involve a company's own leadership becoming the buyer. The difference is in the financing and tax treatment:

  • A traditional management buyout (MBO) is typically financed through a mix of leadership's personal capital, seller financing, and conventional acquisition debt from a bank or lender. It has no built-in tax-deferral mechanism for the seller beyond what applies to any ordinary sale.
  • An Independent Buyout uses a financing structure that has been part of the federal tax code since 1984, which can allow the transaction to be built for tax-advantaged treatment for the seller - something a conventional MBO generally cannot offer in the same way.

Put simply: every Independent Buyout is a form of management-led buyout, but not every management buyout is structured to capture the tax treatment available under an Independent Buyout.

Who actually qualifies for an Independent Buyout

This structure tends to make the most sense for owners who:

  • Run a company with $3 million or more in EBITDA
  • Have an existing leadership team capable of running the business without the owner day to day
  • Want liquidity, or a partial exit, without handing decision-making authority to an outside board
  • Want to explore selling some or all of their company without letting private equity take control of it

If that describes your business, it's worth a real conversation even if you ultimately decide a traditional sale process is the better fit - the point of this guide is simply that most owners are never shown this option exists before they sign a term sheet with someone else.

Frequently asked questions

Is an Independent Buyout the same as selling to private equity? No. The financial outcome can look similar - liquidity, a market-based valuation - but the structure is different. The company's own leadership team becomes the buyer instead of an outside fund, which is what allows the seller to avoid handing control to a private equity sponsor.

Do I have to sell the entire company? No. An Independent Buyout can be structured to let an owner sell some or all of their company, depending on how much liquidity they want now versus how much upside they want to retain going forward.

How is this different from a management buyout? Both involve the existing leadership team as the buyer. An Independent Buyout specifically uses a financing structure available in the tax code since 1984 that a conventional management buyout typically doesn't use, which can allow for tax-advantaged treatment of the sale.

What size company qualifies? IBO Advisors generally works with companies at $3 million or more in EBITDA, though every situation is evaluated individually based on the business's specific facts.

Is the sale really tax-free? An Independent Buyout can be structured to be tax-free for the seller depending on the specifics of the transaction and the seller's individual tax situation. This is determined case by case - confirm your specific structure with a tax advisor before assuming any particular outcome.

The bottom line

If the only two exit conversations you've had involve a private equity term sheet or a strategic buyer's acquisition team, you haven't seen the full menu of options. An Independent Buyout lets a company's own leadership become the buyer, using a financing structure that's existed in the tax code for four decades - with the goal of a private-equity-style outcome without the private equity firm.
