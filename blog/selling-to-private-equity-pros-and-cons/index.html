Selling to Private Equity: Pros and Cons

By Michael Chasen · Updated September 20, 2026

The honest case for selling to private equity is that a fund will usually pay a competitive multiple in cash, let you keep a slice of the upside, and bring capital and acquisition capability you cannot assemble on your own. The honest case against is that the purchase price arrives as debt on your company's balance sheet, the consent list moves the important decisions to a board you do not control, your own job becomes the least secure one in the building, and the exit you were promised in three to five years is now averaging closer to seven. Both halves are true at once, and which half matters more depends on facts about your company and your life that no fund can tell you.

What follows is the ledger, a number attached to each line, with a worked hypothetical at the end. For the mechanics of the process rather than the trade-off, read how to sell a business to private equity first.

The pros, with the numbers behind them

1. The price is competitive, and scale raises it

Financial buyers set the floor in most lower-middle-market processes, and the floor is not low. GF Data's transaction sample for the first nine months of 2025, compiled by CapitalPad, put companies with $3M-$5M of EBITDA at 6.7x, $5M-$8M at 7.4x, and companies above $10M of EBITDA at 8.3x. Sliced by enterprise value instead, the spread is wider still: 6.4x for $10M-$25M deals against 10.3x for $100M-$250M deals.

Read that spread twice, because it is the whole argument for sponsor ownership. A fund that gets you from $4M of EBITDA to $12M is not just growing your earnings; it is re-rating every dollar of them by roughly a turn and a half. That arithmetic is why the second bite exists at all, and it is covered in how private equity values a company.

2. You can take most of the money off the table without leaving

A majority recapitalization typically converts 70-85% of your equity into cash at closing while you keep the rest. For an owner whose entire net worth is one illiquid asset, that is the most valuable thing a sponsor offers - not the headline valuation, but the conversion of concentration risk into a diversified balance sheet while the business keeps running. A minority recapitalization does a smaller version of the same trade and leaves control where it is.

3. Capital for acquisitions you could not finance alone

Add-on acquisitions are now the dominant form of sponsor activity by count: 75.9% of U.S. buyouts in the second quarter of 2025 were add-ons rather than new platforms, per PitchBook data compiled by CapitalPad, which counted 4,509 U.S. bolt-on acquisitions in 2025 and 4,950 in 2024. (That is a share of transactions, not of capital invested.)

If your growth plan requires buying three competitors and you have neither the balance sheet nor the diligence infrastructure to do it, a sponsor genuinely solves that problem. The argument is strongest in fragmented sectors, where consolidation is the value creation - though the economics differ sharply by industry, and a healthcare services platform is not a government contractor whose customer reimburses a defined cost base.

4. In deals like yours, the business usually grows

The most careful study of what buyouts do to companies - Davis, Haltiwanger, Handley, Lipsius, Lerner and Miranda's NBER working paper on the heterogeneous economic effects of private equity buyouts, covering U.S. deals from 1980 to 2013 - found that employment at target firms fell about 12% over two years relative to comparable companies when a public company was taken private, but rose about 15% when a privately held company was bought. Productivity gains were large on average.

Founder-owned companies sit in the second category. The "private equity strips the company for parts" story is drawn largely from the first. If your worry is that your people get cut, the evidence for deals structured like yours points the other way.

5. The process forces a real valuation

A competitive process produces something most owners have never had: a market-tested number with financing behind it, which separates what the business is worth from what the owner hopes it is worth. Our business valuation calculator gets you a first approximation; only a process converts one into an offer.

The cons, with the numbers behind them

1. The purchase price becomes your company's debt

The most common misunderstanding about a leveraged buyout is whose balance sheet carries the loan. It is yours. The fund borrows against the company it is buying, and the company services that debt out of its own cash flow forever after. The mechanics are laid out in how private equity actually finances a buyout; the consequence is that the business is not less profitable after closing, it is less free. Every discretionary decision now clears a covenant test first.

2. You stop being the person who decides

Whether the sponsor takes 51% or 30%, the shareholders' agreement carries a consent list: debt, acquisitions, budget, senior hires, distributions, and any sale of the company. Owners who negotiate hard on price and lightly on governance usually discover the asymmetry in year two. What a minority private equity stake actually controls walks through which items on that list are genuinely negotiable before signing.

3. The CEO chair is the least safe seat

AlixPartners' eleventh annual private equity leadership survey, published in March 2026 and drawing 427 responses from 174 sponsor professionals and 253 portfolio company executives, found that 65% of private equity firms report CEO turnover during the holding period and only 9% say they rarely replace CEOs. Thirty-eight percent of portfolio company executives said they worry about losing their jobs to disruption.

Sellers regularly sign transition agreements assuming "transition" means the length of the hold; in practice it often means eighteen to thirty-six months. If running the business is a condition of the deal being worth doing for you, that has to be written down and fought for, not assumed.

4. The clock is longer than the pitch

The three-to-five-year hold is a sales artifact. Aggregated industry data compiled by CapitalPad from Bain, Preqin, McKinsey, S&P Global and PitchBook puts the average global buyout hold at exit at roughly seven years in 2025, with Preqin's 2024 figure at 6.1 years and McKinsey putting the average age of still-held buyout companies above 6.5 years. Bain's count of unsold sponsor-owned companies reached roughly 32,000, worth about $3.8 trillion, by the middle of 2025 and roughly 33,000 by June 2026.

That backlog is your problem, not just the fund's. Your rollover is illiquid until the sponsor finds a buyer, and in the AlixPartners survey nearly half of respondents said finding buyers is difficult.

5. The second bite is a residual, not a promise

Rollover equity sits behind the debt and usually behind a preferred return in the waterfall. It is the last thing paid, which means it is the first thing that disappears in a mediocre outcome. Rollover equity and the second bite goes through the preference stack; the short version is that the headline "20% of the go-forward company" in a term sheet is not 20% of the exit proceeds.

The trade, on one hypothetical company

Invented numbers, real arithmetic. Call it a $5 million EBITDA business with no debt, sold at 7.4x - the GF Data average for its size band - for a $37.0 million enterprise value, funded with $18.5 million of debt and $18.5 million of equity. The owner rolls 20% of the equity, $3.7 million, and takes $33.3 million in cash at close before fees and tax. At a 10% blended cost, the new debt consumes $1.85 million a year, 37% of EBITDA, before a dollar of capital expenditure.

Who it fits, and who it doesn't

Selling to private equity is the right answer when three things are true: consolidation is genuinely where the value is in your sector, you want out of the operating seat within a few years anyway, and the cash at close makes you financially indifferent to what the rollover does. In that configuration you are handing a growth problem you cannot solve to someone who can, and the control you lose costs you nothing you wanted to keep.

It is the wrong answer when you want liquidity but intend to keep running the company, when the business already leads a sector with nothing worth buying, when the debt load would endanger a cyclical business in its next downturn, or when a management team or family member could own this business and simply lacks the capital. Those situations have their own structures - the full list is in alternatives to selling to private equity, sequenced in every exit option compared.

Frequently asked questions

What are the main pros and cons of selling to private equity? The pros: a competitive cash multiple, partial liquidity with upside retained, acquisition capital, and evidence that privately held targets tend to grow after a buyout. The cons: the price becomes debt on your company, a consent list governs your decisions, roughly two-thirds of sponsors replace the CEO during the hold, the hold averages about seven years, and your rollover is the last claim in the waterfall.

Does private equity pay more than a strategic buyer? Usually not at the headline level - a strategic buyer with synergies can pay above the financial-buyer floor. A sponsor offers partial liquidity, a continuing role and a second bite instead, none of which a full sale to a competitor provides.

How much of my company can I keep? In a typical majority recapitalization, 15-30% - though where that equity sits in the waterfall matters more than the percentage.

Will private equity cut jobs at my company? It depends on deal type. In the NBER data, employment fell about 12% over two years in take-privates of public companies but rose about 15% in buyouts of privately held ones - the founder-owned category.

What is the biggest mistake owners make in a PE process? Negotiating the price and accepting the paper. Governance, the transition agreement and the equity waterfall are worth more than the last quarter-turn of multiple, and all three are far easier to change before a letter of intent than after.

The bottom line

Private equity is not a villain and it is not a partner. It is a fund with a mandate, a cost of capital and a deadline, and it will treat your company exactly as that mandate requires. The pros and cons of selling to private equity come down to one question: are you selling a business, or financing one? If you are selling, the fund's model works for you and the control you give up is control you were giving up anyway. If you are financing - if you want the cash but intend to keep building - the same structure charges a very high price for capital.

That second case is what an Independent Buyout exists for: the owner takes liquidity at a market-tested valuation, the existing leadership team becomes the buyer, the transaction is financed against the business itself, and no outside fund holds the consent list or the exit clock. It is not better than a sponsor sale in every situation - it is the right structure for owners whose answer to "selling or financing?" is the second one. Either way, exit planning done properly starts two to three years before any conversation with a buyer.
