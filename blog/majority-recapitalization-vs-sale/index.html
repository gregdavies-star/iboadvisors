Majority Recapitalization vs. Sale: What the Word "Recap" Is Hiding

By Michael Chasen · Updated October 10, 2026

A majority recapitalization is a sale of control. An investor buys 60% to 80% of your company, you keep the rest, and the word "recapitalization" does the work of making it sound like a financing rather than a change of ownership. It usually is a good deal. It is still a deal in which you stop being the person who decides.

That matters because owners compare a "recap" against a "sale" as though they were different species. Three distinct transactions get filed under the same word, and only one of them leaves you in charge:

  • Minority recapitalization - an investor buys 20% to 49%. You keep majority ownership and board control. The mechanics and the real arithmetic are here.
  • Majority recapitalization - an investor buys 51% to 80%, you roll the balance into the new holding company, and control moves. This page.
  • Leveraged or dividend recapitalization - the company borrows money and pays the cash to you. Nobody new joins the cap table at all.

Why the word "recapitalization" stopped meaning anything

The tax code has a precise definition and the M&A market ignores it. Under 26 U.S. Code § 368(a)(1)(E), a "recapitalization" is a reorganization - a reshuffling of a single company's own capital structure, stock exchanged for stock or debt exchanged for stock inside the existing entity. No new owner is required, and in the classic version no cash changes hands.

A majority recap as the market uses the term is something else: a new holding company buys the stock of your company for cash and debt, and you reinvest a slice of your proceeds into that holding company. That is a stock purchase with a rollover attached. Calling it a recapitalization is a marketing decision, not a legal one - and the first practical consequence is that the documents you sign are purchase agreements with indemnities, escrows and a shareholders' agreement, not a simple amendment to your charter.

The arithmetic on an invented company

Numbers make this concrete. Everything below is an invented company - not a quote, a projection, or a description of any transaction.

Take a business with $6 million of normalized EBITDA and no funded debt. GF Data's figures for the $25M-$50M enterprise value band average 6.8x to 7.2x across 2021 to 2025 (Capital Pad, citing GF Data), so call it 7.0x, or $42 million of enterprise and equity value. Getting that EBITDA number right is most of the negotiation; how a private equity firm actually normalizes it is worth reading before you anchor on any multiple, and you can run your own sector-adjusted range through the business valuation calculator.

Option A - outright sale. $42 million, 100% of the equity, you are finished. Fees, escrow and taxes come off the top; nothing comes back.

Option B - majority recap. The buyer forms a holding company, pays $42 million for the business, and funds it with $21 million of bank and private credit debt plus $21 million of equity - roughly the leverage ratio a sponsor typically puts on a company this size. You roll 20% of your proceeds, $8.4 million, into that equity and take $33.6 million in cash.

Here is the part almost nobody explains in the first meeting. You rolled 20% of the price and you now own 40% of the new company - $8.4 million of a $21 million equity layer - because half the purchase was funded with debt. Rolling into a leveraged structure buys a bigger percentage than the percentage you rolled. Rollover is not a rounding detail either: 57% of US mid-market deals included an equity rollover in 2023, up from 46% in 2020, per a Goodwin Procter study (Preqin's summary).

What the 40% is actually worth

Sponsor equity rarely comes in as plain common stock. Assume the sponsor's $12.6 million is preferred, with a liquidation preference and an 8% accruing dividend - standard, and the single most important line in the term sheet. Five years later the company earns $9 million of EBITDA, sells at the same 7.0x for $63 million, and has paid debt down to $12 million. Equity value: $51 million.

Step Amount
Equity value at exit $51.0M
Less sponsor preference, $12.6M accrued at 8% for 5 years ($18.5M)
Common available to split $32.5M
Your 40% of the common $13.0M

So the recap pays you $33.6 million now and roughly $13.0 million in five years, against $42 million today for the clean sale. Whether that is a win depends on a comparison almost no one runs: the $8.4 million you left in would have been worth about $11.2 million in five years at a 6% return, sitting in a brokerage account and carrying none of the operating risk. The second bite beat that, but not by much, and only because EBITDA grew 50%. Flat EBITDA and the same preference leaves you roughly $7 million - less than the cash would have earned. That asymmetry is the whole subject of rollover equity and the second bite of the apple, and it is why the preference terms matter more than the headline multiple.

The leveraged recap: no buyer, all the debt

The third structure removes the buyer entirely. The company borrows against its own cash flow and pays the proceeds to its shareholders as a dividend - "a financing strategy through which a private company incurs new debt to pay a cash dividend to its shareholders," as Dechert puts it. You keep 100% of the equity. You simply own a more fragile company.

Sponsors have been doing this at scale. Total dividend recap volume rose 128.8% year over year in 2024 and another 22.8% in 2025, with leveraged loan issuance tied to sponsored dividend recaps reaching $74.3 billion in 2025 (Capstone Partners, citing PitchBook); leveraged loans are 91% of that volume. A $6 million EBITDA company is not borrowing in the syndicated loan market, but the structure travels down-market through banks and private credit funds unchanged.

On the invented company, a lender advancing 3.0x EBITDA writes an $18 million term loan; after fees roughly $17 million reaches the owner. Now service it. Seven-year amortization puts principal at about $2.6 million a year, and 9% interest on the opening balance is another $1.6 million - about $4.2 million of debt service in year one against $6 million of EBITDA, before capex and cash taxes. One soft year and the covenant conversation starts.

There is also a legal floor. Dividend recaps are tested against U.S. bankruptcy and fraudulent transfer statutes, which is why lenders and boards commission a solvency opinion: an independent assessment that, after the dividend, the company's assets still exceed its liabilities at fair value, it retains enough capital to operate, and it can pay its debts as they mature (Capstone Partners). If a recapped company fails inside the look-back period, that opinion is what stands between the dividend and a clawback.

What changes the day after a majority recap

The cash arrives and the authority leaves, usually on the same afternoon. A 60% to 80% holder appoints the board majority, and the shareholders' agreement adds a consent list on top of it: budget, capex above a threshold, hiring and firing senior management, new debt, acquisitions, and the timing of the eventual sale. Those terms are not softer because the deal was called a recap - if anything the consent lists in majority recaps are longer, because the sponsor is underwriting a management team it did not hire. The same list is worth studying in what veto rights a private equity investor negotiates even for a minority stake, and what the first 100 days after a buyout actually look like.

The decision that follows from all of this is narrower than "recap or sale." It is: do you want cash and control, cash without control, or control without cash? Only the minority recap and the leveraged recap leave you in the chair, and selling part of your business while keeping control turns on the governance terms rather than the percentage sold.

Frequently asked questions

Is a majority recapitalization the same as selling my company? Economically, close to it. You sell most of the equity, control passes to the buyer, and you reinvest part of the proceeds. The difference from a straight sale is that you keep exposure to the next exit - and keep the risk that comes with it.

What is the difference between a partial and a full recapitalization? "Partial" usually means a minority recap, where you sell a non-controlling stake and keep the board. "Full" usually means a majority recap, where the investor takes control and you roll a minority position into the new holding company. The percentage decides the cash; the shareholders' agreement decides the authority.

Is a leveraged recapitalization a sale? No. No shares change hands. The company borrows and distributes the cash to existing shareholders, so ownership is unchanged and the leverage is entirely yours to carry.

How much of my proceeds will I be asked to roll? In lower-middle-market deals, commonly 10% to 30%. Because the buyer funds the rest with debt, rolling 20% of the price often buys a materially larger share of the new equity - and that share usually sits beneath the sponsor's preferred.

Can I get recap-level liquidity without giving up control? That is the question an Independent Buyout is built around, and it is also what the other alternatives to selling to private equity are competing to answer. The bottom line

A majority recapitalization is the most oversold structure in the lower middle market, because the name describes the financing and hides the governance. It can be the right answer: it converts most of your net worth to cash, keeps you in the upside, and hands the operating burden to someone whose job is to carry it. Just price it as what it is - a sale of control with a minority position attached, behind a preference - and compare it against a clean sale and against a minority recap on the same spreadsheet.
