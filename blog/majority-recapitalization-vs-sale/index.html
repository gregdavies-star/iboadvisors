Majority Recapitalization vs Sale: What You Keep, and What You Are Taxed On

By Michael Chasen · Updated October 11, 2026

A majority recapitalization is a sale of control that stops short of a sale of the company. A buyer - almost always a private equity sponsor - takes 60% to 80% of the equity, you keep the rest, and the company itself usually borrows to help fund the purchase. An outright sale is 100%, cash, finished. The two can carry the identical headline valuation and still produce completely different outcomes, because they differ in three places: what you are taxed on this year, what the balance sheet looks like the day after close, and who decides things in year three.

The four things called a "recapitalization"

The word covers four different transactions, each with a different tax answer.

StructureWhat happensWho ends up in control
Minority recapitalizationAn investor buys a non-controlling stake, usually 20%-49%You do, subject to negotiated consent rights
Majority recapitalizationAn investor buys 51%-80%; you roll the balance into the new entityThe investor
Leveraged recapitalizationThe company borrows and uses the proceeds to buy back stock or pay a distributionUnchanged, unless the buyback shifts percentages
Dividend recapitalizationA company already owned by a sponsor borrows again to pay its shareholders a dividendUnchanged

A minority recapitalization is a liquidity event you survive with your authority intact. A majority recapitalization is a change of control with a deferred second payment attached. Owners regularly come into a process shopping for the first and get quoted the second, because the second is what most sponsors buy.

Difference one: you are taxed on the cash, not the paper

In an outright sale, the whole purchase price is a taxable event in the year of close. In a majority recapitalization, the portion you roll into the buyer's new holding company is normally structured so that no gain is recognized on it at close.

The mechanism is 26 U.S. Code § 351, which provides that "No gain or loss shall be recognized if property is transferred to a corporation" solely in exchange for its stock, where the transferors are in control immediately afterwards - control being defined by § 368(c) as "at least 80 percent of the total combined voting power of all classes of stock entitled to vote." In a typical structure the sponsor and the rolling sellers contribute to the same new holding company and together clear that threshold, so the rolled equity goes in untaxed. Partnership and LLC structures reach the same result through a different door. What does not go in untaxed is the cash: § 351(b) recognizes gain to the extent of money and other property received, which in plain terms means the cash portion of your consideration is taxed now at the usual rates - a top 20% long-term capital gains rate plus the 3.8% net investment income tax, a 23.8% combined federal rate before any state tax.

Deferral is not forgiveness. Your basis carries over into the rolled stake, so the tax you did not pay at close sits in front of the second sale, and the deferral itself depends on the deal being papered as a nonrecognition exchange - a question for tax counsel, not an assumption to draw from a term sheet. Rollover equity and the second bite covers the terms that decide whether that second sale is worth waiting for.

Difference two: the leverage is on your company, not the buyer's

A sponsor funding a majority recapitalization does not write the full check. Part of the price is borrowed by the company being bought, so the company you still own a quarter of wakes up with debt it did not have the day before. How private equity actually finances a buyout walks the capital stack; the point here is narrower - the leverage sets a ceiling on what the structure can pay you.

There is a tax ceiling too. Under section 163(j), the deduction for business interest expense is capped at business interest income plus 30% of adjusted taxable income plus floor plan financing interest. Two details matter for a recap: companies passing the section 448(c) gross receipts test are exempt - the inflation-adjusted threshold is $32 million of average annual gross receipts for 2026 - and for tax years beginning after December 31, 2024, depreciation, amortization and depletion are added back in computing adjusted taxable income (IRS, business interest expense limitation Q&A). An asset-heavy company just above the revenue threshold therefore has a materially higher deductible-interest ceiling in 2026 than it did in 2023, which is one quiet reason recap leverage has been easier to place.

Difference three: control transfers on day one, not at the second close

This is the one owners misjudge. A majority recapitalization is sold on continuity - you stay, your team stays, the sign stays - and all of that can be true while the authority to decide moves on day one. The buyer appoints the board majority, the shareholders' agreement carries a schedule of consent rights over budgets, hiring, debt and any sale, and the right to force an exit is usually theirs too. If keeping the final say is the objective, selling part of a business and keeping control covers the governance mechanics that decide it - which are not the same thing as the percentage.

The arithmetic on an invented $42 million company

Take a company with $6 million of adjusted EBITDA, no net debt, priced at 7.0x - a $42 million enterprise value and, with no debt to repay, $42 million of equity. Assume a near-zero tax basis and the 23.8% combined federal rate above. Two offers arrive at the same valuation: a 100% cash sale, and a majority recapitalization in which a sponsor buys 75% and you roll 25%.

At closeOutright saleMajority recap (75/25)
Equity value$42.00M$42.00M
Cash consideration$42.00M$31.50M
Federal tax at 23.8%($10.00M)($7.50M)
After-tax cash in hand$32.00M$24.00M
Rolled equity (untaxed at close)—$10.50M
Debt on the company after closeNot your problemWhatever funded the purchase

The recap is $8 million behind in cash on day one. The usual answer is that the retained 25% makes up the difference, and it might - but that phrase has a number attached, and most owners never see it calculated.

The break-even on the rolled stake

Selling outright puts $32.00M to work five years earlier. At a modest 6% after-tax return that becomes roughly $42.8 million in five years. The recap route compounds $24.00M on the same terms to about $32.1 million, leaving a gap of roughly $10.7 million that the rolled stake has to cover after tax. With basis carried over and the same 23.8% rate applied on exit, covering $10.7 million after tax takes about $14.0 million gross - against a stake worth $10.5 million today.

So the rolled 25% has to be worth about 1.34x what it is worth now, five years out, simply to match the outright sale - and the stake earning it is the most junior claim in a capital structure that now has bank debt in front of it. A flat exit loses. An exit pushed to year seven because the hold got extended loses by more. A majority recapitalization is a leveraged investment in your own company, made with after-tax dollars you were otherwise free to diversify, and it deserves the hurdle rate any other leveraged investment would get.

Run the same table with your own numbers: the inputs that move it most are the multiple and the split, and a sector-adjusted valuation range plus a defensible normalized EBITDA figure are where any version of this starts.

Where a leveraged or dividend recapitalization is different

The two no-new-owner versions look simpler and carry a tax trap the majority recap does not. When the company borrows and hands the money to shareholders, the question is whether you have received a dividend, taxed at ordinary rates on the whole distribution, or sold stock, taxed on the gain.

26 U.S. Code § 302 decides it. A redemption gets exchange treatment if it is "not essentially equivalent to a dividend," if it completely terminates your interest, or if it is "substantially disproportionate" - which has two hard numbers. Immediately after the redemption you must own "less than 50 percent of the total combined voting power of all classes of stock entitled to vote," and your share of voting stock must be "less than 80 percent of" what it was before.

Worked through: an owner at 100% who redeems down to 45% clears both tests. An owner at 100% who redeems down to 60% fails the 50% test and, absent a facts-and-circumstances argument under the first branch, is looking at distribution treatment under § 302(d). Same cash, materially different tax - and the split is usually chosen for non-tax reasons before anyone checks.

Which one fits

  • Outright sale when the proceeds have to fund the rest of your life, when you want out of the operating seat within a year, or when the business faces a risk you would not voluntarily stay levered into.
  • Majority recapitalization when you genuinely believe the next five years are worth more than the last five, you are willing to be a minority shareholder under someone else's board, and the after-tax cash at close is already enough on its own.
  • Leveraged recapitalization when you want cash without a new owner and the company can carry the debt - with the § 302 question answered in advance.
  • Neither when the real objective is liquidity plus continued control, which is the gap the standard private equity options do not fill.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a partial and a full recapitalization? A partial recapitalization leaves you holding equity after close - a minority or majority recap, depending on who ends up with control. A full recapitalization, in practice an outright sale, takes 100% of your stock and ends your interest in the company.

Is a majority recapitalization a sale? For control purposes, yes: the buyer holds the majority and appoints the board. For tax purposes it is a sale of the cash portion and, where it is structured as a nonrecognition exchange, a deferral on the portion you roll.

What percentage do sponsors usually take in a majority recap? Commonly 60% to 80%, with the seller rolling the balance. The split is negotiable and is the single biggest determinant of both your cash at close and your break-even on the retained stake.

Do you pay tax on rollover equity? Not at close, where the rollover qualifies as a nonrecognition exchange. Your original basis carries into the new stake, so the deferred gain is taxed at the second exit. Whether your specific structure qualifies is a question for tax counsel.

The bottom line

A majority recapitalization and an outright sale are not two prices for the same thing. They are a payment today against a payment today plus a levered, illiquid, minority-position bet on a company you no longer control - and on the numbers above that bet has to appreciate about a third in five years just to draw level. That can be the right trade. It is only the right trade if the arithmetic was done before the term sheet was signed, rather than discovered at the second exit.
