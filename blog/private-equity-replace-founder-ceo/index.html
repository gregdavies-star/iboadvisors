Does Private Equity Replace the Founder CEO?

By Michael Chasen · Updated November 11, 2026

Usually, yes — eventually. The best measurement of it comes from Gompers, Kaplan and Mukharlyamov’s NBER working paper on the market for CEOs in private equity, which tracked 192 US buyouts of companies worth more than $1 billion done between 2010 and 2016. By the end of 2021, 71% of them — 136 companies — had changed their CEO before the company was sold or went bankrupt. Another 28% went through a second CEO change in the same period.

The replacements are not promotions from within. More than 75% of the new CEOs were external hires, and 67% were complete outsiders — no prior board seat at the company and no previous job there as an executive. The same paper contrasts that with public companies, where Cziraki and Jenter found 72% of new S&P 500 CEOs are internal promotions. Private equity does the opposite of what a public board does: it goes outside, and it hires someone it has usually worked with before. The median new CEO was 51.

How much of that applies to a founder-owned company

Read the sample before you read the headline. Those 192 deals are large: just over 30% were take-privates, 16% were divisions carved out of other companies, 48% were bought from another private equity firm, and only 4% were unaffiliated private companies. Almost none of them had a founder sitting in the CEO chair at closing. What the paper measures precisely is the behaviour of sponsors, not the fate of founders.

Two other data sets fill in the gap. AlixPartners’ eleventh annual private equity leadership survey — 174 private equity professionals and 253 portfolio company executives, surveyed between October and December 2025 — reports that portfolio company CEO turnover is rising and spikes around year two of the hold. Spencer Stuart, looking at private-equity-backed software companies, found that more than half had a CEO transition during the hold period, and 25% of those had two or more.

So the honest summary for an owner of a $3M–$30M EBITDA company: the exact 71% is not your number, but the direction is, and year two is when it tends to happen — not year one, when the sponsor still needs you.

Why sponsors change the CEO even when the company is performing

Being replaced is rarely a verdict on how you ran the business. It is a verdict on the fit between the founder and the next five years of the business, and the two are different jobs:

  • The job changes at closing. A founder-run company optimises for the owner’s own risk tolerance. A sponsor-owned company runs a value-creation plan to a deadline, with monthly reporting to an investment committee, a debt service schedule, and an exit to prepare for. Some founders enjoy that. Many discover they have taken a job reporting to a board on someone else’s timetable.
  • Buy-and-build needs a different operator. If the thesis is six add-on acquisitions, the skill being hired for is integration, not the founder’s original market insight.
  • The sponsor has a bench. Firms keep rosters of operating executives who have run a portfolio company before and know what the reporting pack looks like. That is why two-thirds of replacements are outsiders: a known quantity from outside beats an unknown quantity from inside.
  • The clock is the boss. With holds now stretching to six to seven years and lengthening, a sponsor sitting on a flat year in year two has time to act and every incentive to act early.

Who actually makes the decision

The board does, and the board is constituted by the documents you sign at closing. Appointing and removing the CEO sits on the consent list in essentially every sponsor investment — including minority ones. An investor holding 30% of the equity can still hold the right to approve or veto senior hires, which is the whole point of what a minority private equity stake really controls. The percentage you sell tells you very little; the consent list tells you nearly everything.

It follows that the moment to negotiate your own tenure is before signing, while the sponsor still needs your signature. Afterwards, you are an employee with a shareholding, and the governance you agreed to is the constitution you operate under. The broader version of that argument is in what happens after private equity buys your company.

What the employment agreement protects, and what it does not

Founders tend to read the purchase agreement closely and the employment agreement quickly. The employment agreement is where replacement gets priced. Four clauses do the work:

  • “Cause”. A tight definition lists specific acts — fraud, conviction, material breach, each with a cure period. A loose one includes phrases like “failure to perform duties to the satisfaction of the board”, which means the board can manufacture cause at will.
  • “Good reason”. Without it, the sponsor can strip your title, change your reporting line or move your office until you resign — and a resignation is not a termination without cause, so nothing is owed. With it, a material demotion counts as a termination and triggers the same severance.
  • Severance. Usually 12–24 months of base salary, sometimes with a pro-rated bonus. This is the smallest number in the discussion and absorbs most of the negotiating energy.
  • The call right on your shares. This is the one that matters, and it lives in the shareholders’ agreement rather than the employment agreement. It lets the company repurchase a departing executive’s equity — often at fair market value for a “good leaver”, and at the lower of cost and fair market value for a “bad leaver”, with bad leaver defined by the same “cause” clause you skimmed earlier.

If your rolled equity is treated as executive equity rather than as purchase-price consideration, then the call right applies to it, and being replaced stops being a question about your salary and becomes a question about the second half of your sale proceeds.

What replacement costs a rolled-over founder: a worked example

Made-up numbers, round for clarity. A debt-free company earns $8M of EBITDA and is bought at 7.5x, an enterprise value of $60M, funded with $33M of debt and $27M of new equity. The founder rolls $5.4M — 20% of the new equity — and takes $54.6M in cash at close. (If you want to run your own version of the first half of that arithmetic, the business valuation calculator prices the enterprise value from your EBITDA and industry.)

In year three the board replaces the founder. EBITDA is $9M, the implied value is 8x, debt has been paid down to $25M, so the equity is worth about $47M and the founder’s 20% is worth $9.4M. The sponsor sells in year six at $90M against $18M of remaining debt: $72M of equity, and 20% of that is $14.4M. What the founder actually receives depends on one clause:

What the shareholders’ agreement says about the rolled stakeWhat the founder collects
Purchase-price consideration — no call right, rides to exit$14.4M in year 6
Call right at fair market value on termination$9.4M in year 3
Call right at the lower of cost and fair market value (bad leaver)$5.4M in year 3

Same company, same performance, same termination: a $9M spread, decided by a definition. That is why the rollover is worth negotiating as consideration rather than as compensation — the full case for which is in rollover equity and the second bite of the apple.

What to negotiate before you sign

  • Carve the rollover out of the leaver provisions entirely. It is part of the price you were paid for the company, not a grant. If that fails, cap the downside at fair market value regardless of why you left.
  • Make “cause” exhaustive and curable, with a board vote and written notice, and define “good reason” to cover title, duties, reporting line, compensation and location.
  • Decouple the board seat from the job. A founder who is removed as CEO and automatically loses their board seat has no visibility into the exit that pays them.
  • Ask for the transition plan up front. If the sponsor expects to bring in a CEO in year two, it is better to know that at the term sheet than to find it out at the year-two board meeting. A sponsor who will not answer is answering.
  • Negotiate tag-along rights so the rolled stake exits alongside the sponsor rather than at the sponsor’s discretion.

Frequently asked questions

Does selling a minority stake protect me from being replaced?
Not by itself. Removal of the CEO is a consent-list item in most minority deals, so a 30% investor can hold the same veto a 70% investor holds. What protects you is the wording of the consent list and the leaver provisions, not the percentage.

Will my management team be replaced too?
Less often than the CEO. Sponsors generally keep the operating bench and change the top of the house; the seat most at risk in a buyout is the one the founder is sitting in.

How long do founder CEOs usually last?
There is no clean figure for founder-owned deals specifically, but AlixPartners puts the spike in portfolio company CEO turnover around year two, and the NBER sample’s 71% accumulated over holds of roughly five to eleven years. Plan for a two-to-three-year runway unless your agreement says otherwise.

Can I just stay on as a board member or chairman?
Sometimes, and it is worth asking for in writing at the term sheet stage. A chairman title agreed after you have been replaced is a courtesy; one agreed before closing is a contract.

The bottom line

The question founders ask is “will they replace me?” The better question is “what happens to my money when they do?” Replacement is the base case in a sponsor deal, it clusters around year two, and the person hired is usually an outsider the sponsor already knows. None of that is hidden — it is simply never the subject of the meeting where the multiple is agreed.

Price it accordingly: take more of the consideration in cash at close, treat the rolled stake as the contingent instrument it is, and spend your negotiating capital on the leaver provisions rather than on the severance number. Owners who work through the pros and cons of selling to private equity usually find this is the term they object to most and discuss least.
