Will Private Equity Replace You as CEO After the Sale?

By Michael Chasen · Updated November 12, 2026

Usually, eventually, yes — and in large deals it is closer to a rule than a risk. Of the US companies with enterprise values above $1 billion that private equity firms bought between 2010 and 2016, 71% hired a new CEO under private equity ownership, and almost 75% of those new CEOs were external hires, 67% of them complete outsiders to the company. That is Gompers and Kaplan’s work on the market for buyout CEOs, summarised on the Harvard Law School Forum on Corporate Governance.

The industry does not treat this as a failure mode. In AlixPartners’ 11th Annual Private Equity Leadership Survey, published in March 2026 from 427 respondents — 174 private equity leaders and 253 portfolio company executives — 65% of PE firms reported CEO turnover during the holding period, and only 9% said their firm rarely replaces CEOs.

Two things are worth saying immediately, because most articles on this topic skip both. The 71% figure comes from billion-dollar buyouts, not from companies doing $3M–$30M of EBITDA, where the sponsor is usually buying the operator along with the business. And “replaced” is rarely a dramatic event at close; it is a thing that happens in year two or three, to a founder who was told at signing that nothing would change.

Read the last row carefully, because it is the one an optimistic banker will quote at you. The Center’s research says 76% of sellers “continue to have roles in their companies today” — a role, not the chief executive’s chair. Chairman, consultant, board member and advisor are all roles. So is CEO-for-eighteen-months-then-vice-chairman.

When it happens: year two, not day one

The timing is the part founders get wrong. Replacements cluster in the middle of the hold. In the AlixPartners survey reported by Consultancy.uk, 58% of portfolio-company CEO replacements were made in the window running from one year after the deal closes to one year before the exit, and 39% of respondents named that same window the most disruptive time to do it. They do it anyway.

That window is not an accident. It is what remains after two bookends are removed. Nobody replaces the CEO at close, because the sponsor just paid a multiple for a business this person runs and the lenders underwrote the management team. Nobody replaces the CEO in the last year either, because a leadership change mid-process scares buyers. Everything in between is live — and on current private equity holding periods of six to seven years, “in between” is four or five years long.

The same survey found the change is expensive when it is unplanned: internal rate of return got worse in 46% of those cases, improved in only 25%, and was unchanged in 29%, while 83% of respondents said forcing a CEO out lengthens the hold. Everyone involved knows this. It does not stop it happening, which tells you the pressure to act is stronger than the evidence against acting.

Why a founder is the most exposed CEO in the portfolio

Every other executive in a portfolio company was hired to execute a plan. The founder is the only one who was there before the plan existed, and the plan was written by people who were simultaneously bidding for the business.

Four things change on the Monday after close, and each one is a place the founder can be found wanting against a standard that did not exist the week before.

  • The budget becomes a contract. The model that justified the price — built during diligence, when optimism was useful to both sides — becomes the number you are measured against each month.
  • Reporting gets industrialised. Monthly close in ten business days, a board pack, 13-week cash, covenant compliance certificates for the lenders behind the acquisition debt. Owners who ran a good business on a quarterly review cycle are suddenly late every month.
  • The job becomes acquisitive. Buy-and-build means integrating companies, which is a different skill from running one well, and it is often the specific skill the sponsor hires an outsider to supply.
  • Authority stops coming from ownership. Before the deal you could decide something because the shares were yours. Afterwards, what you can decide is whatever the governing documents say you can — a shift covered in more detail in what happens after private equity buys your company.

The anxiety this produces shows up in the data too: in the 2026 AlixPartners survey, 38% of portfolio-company executives said they worry about losing their jobs because of disruption, which the firm notes is far higher than at companies without PE investment.

The four documents that decide it, and none of them is the relationship

Founders negotiate price for months and sign the employment agreement in the last week, often without separate counsel. It is the wrong allocation of attention. Whether you stay, and what leaving costs you, is settled in four places:

  1. The employment agreement. Fixed term or at will; the definition of “cause” and whether it carries notice and a cure period; severance; what “good reason” lets you resign for, if anything. A cause definition that includes “failure to meet performance objectives set by the board” is not a cause definition; it is a termination right with extra words.
  2. The board. How many seats there are, who appoints them, and what vote removes an officer. In a majority sale the sponsor appoints the majority of the board, and that board hires and fires the chief executive. In a minority recapitalisation the arithmetic is different, but the consent list a minority investor negotiates still reaches a long way into who runs the company.
  3. The rollover documents. Your rolled equity almost always carries a call right that lets the company buy it back if you stop being an employee. The price in that clause — fair market value, or the lower of cost and fair market value — is worth more than any other sentence you will negotiate that week.
  4. The incentive plan. Management option or profits-interest units typically vest partly on time and partly on exit. Exit-vesting units are worth nothing to a leaver, by design.

What a termination actually costs: a worked example

Take an invented company with no real-world counterpart. $6,000,000 of adjusted EBITDA, sold at 7.5x for a $45,000,000 enterprise value, debt-free and cash-free. The sponsor funds it with $22,500,000 of debt and $22,500,000 of equity. The owner takes $40,500,000 in cash and rolls $4,500,000, which buys 20% of the new equity, and is granted incentive units equal to 5% of the equity, vesting 20% a year over five years. (Real profits interests usually pay only above a hurdle; this example ignores that to keep the arithmetic legible.)

Three years in, EBITDA is $7,500,000 and debt has amortised to $16,500,000. At the same 7.5x, the equity is worth $39,750,000, so the rolled 20% is worth $7,950,000 on paper and the 60% of the incentive units that have time-vested are worth 3% of the equity, or $1,192,500. Then the board decides it wants a buy-and-build operator. What the founder actually receives depends entirely on which version of the clauses got signed.

Note also that the first row is not the good outcome it looks like — an illiquid 20% of a company you no longer run, with no information rights, is the position most founders least want to be in. What to settle before you sign

  • A fixed term, not at-will employment. Three years with defined severance is a position. “At will” with a handshake is not.
  • A narrow “cause” definition — fraud, felony, material breach — with written notice and a cure period, and no reference to performance targets.
  • Good-leaver treatment at fair market value on the rolled stake in every scenario except the narrow cause definition above, with the valuation method and the appraiser named in the document.
  • Time-vesting on at least half the incentive units, with accelerated vesting on a termination without cause.
  • A board seat for as long as you hold equity, and information rights that survive your employment.
  • The first-year budget attached as an exhibit, agreed by you, so the number you are measured against is one you signed rather than one the deal team modelled.

Frequently asked questions

Will private equity fire me at closing?
Almost never. The lenders underwrote you and the sponsor needs continuity through the transition. The exposure begins once the first full budget cycle has been reported, which is why 58% of replacements sit in the middle of the hold rather than at either end.

Does rolling equity protect my job?
No. Employment and equity are separate documents, and the leaver provisions in the equity documents exist precisely because the two come apart. Rolling a larger percentage buys you economics, not tenure.

Can I be removed if I keep a minority stake?
Yes, if the board that hires the CEO has a majority appointed by someone else. The question to ask is never “how much do I own” but “who appoints the board, and what does the consent list cover”.

What if I want to stop being CEO anyway?
Then say so early and price it, because a planned handover is worth more than an unplanned one to every party. That is a succession planning conversation, and it is a much better one to have before a process than during it.

The bottom line

The honest framing is not “will they replace me” but “on what terms, and in which year”. A sponsor buys a business to sell it at a higher price in six or seven years, and the chief executive is an input to that plan like any other. If a different operator raises the exit value, the board that the sponsor appointed will hire a different operator, and it will be a commercial decision rather than a personal one.

What a founder can do about it is almost entirely done before signing: a fixed term, a narrow cause definition, fair-market-value treatment of the rolled stake for a leaver, and time-vesting on the incentive units. Those four lines decide whether an exit from the corner office in year three is a $9,100,000 outcome or a $4,500,000 one. They are also the lines that get least attention, because they are negotiated in the week everyone is focused on the headline multiple and the pros and cons of selling to private equity have already been settled in principle.

It is also the reason an Independent Buyout is structured the way it is. The company is bought by a trust, the trust holds control, and the existing team carries on running the business — there is no fund behind the purchase, no exit date set by someone else, and no incoming operator hired to prepare the company for the next sale.
