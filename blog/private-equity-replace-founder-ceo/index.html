Does Private Equity Replace the Founder CEO?

By Michael Chasen · Updated November 15, 2026

Usually, yes — eventually. In AlixPartners’ 11th annual Private Equity Leadership Survey, published in March 2026 from 427 respondents (174 private equity leaders and 253 portfolio company executives), “nearly two-thirds of private equity firms replace portfolio company CEOs during the holding period.” Only 9% of the PE leaders surveyed said their firms rarely do it.

Deal records say the same thing. Russell Reynolds mapped the CEO history of 196 European private equity exits for The CEO Decision: 135 of them — 69% — had at least one CEO change during the hold, and one in four of those changed more than once, averaging 2.4 transitions per deal.

So the question an owner should be asking in diligence is not whether the sponsor intends to replace him. It is when, and what that does to the money he has not been paid yet.

Nobody is lying to you at the letter of intent

“We are backing the management team” and “we replace the CEO about two-thirds of the time” are both true, and they are true for the same reason. A sponsor buys a company at $5M of EBITDA intending to own something materially larger at exit. The job of running the larger thing — four acquisitions integrated, a real finance function, monthly reporting a lender signs off on, a management layer that did not exist before — is not the job the owner has been doing. Nothing has to go wrong for the role to change out from under the person holding it.

That is also why the replacement rate is not a verdict on the founder. Russell Reynolds is explicit that CEO transitions are not inherently costly, and its data shows only 30% of new CEOs were internal promotions: the rest came from outside, and more than three-quarters of those came out of the same sector. The sponsor is not looking for someone better than the founder in general. It is looking for someone who has already done the specific thing it plans to do next.

The change lands after the earn-out, not on day one

The folk belief is that private equity fires the founder in the first hundred days. The evidence says the opposite, and the real timing is far more awkward for a seller.

Spencer Stuart examined 155 CEO changes in European private-equity-backed companies and split them down the middle: half were planned at, or shortly after, the deal, and half were unplanned at the time of the transaction. The two halves run on different clocks.

  • Planned changes happen early. Half of them occurred within the first six months and most within eighteen months. Spencer Stuart notes that a planned change often means replacing the incumbent CEO — frequently the business founder — after an earn-out period. The decision was made before the ink dried; the timetable was set by the earn-out.
  • Unplanned changes happen in the middle. Only about 10% occurred in the first year. Nearly 90% occurred two to four years into the deal. Of those, half involved an underperforming CEO, around 10% a disagreement over strategy or a breakdown in the relationship with the sponsor, and around 10% a CEO who left for a job somewhere else.

Russell Reynolds’ exit data points the same way. Among the deals that changed CEO, only 24% appointed the executive who ultimately led the business to exit in the first year; over 60% appointed that person after the second year, and internal promotions were the latest of all at 71% after year two.

Read those two studies together and a pattern falls out that matters more than the headline percentage. Year one is quiet. Years two through four are when the job changes hands. That window is, almost exactly, the window in which a seller is still waiting on an earn-out, still holding rolled equity with no exit in sight, and no longer protected by the sponsor’s need for a smooth transition.

Why the timing is the whole problem

If you were paid 100% cash at close and left, the replacement rate is an interesting statistic about someone else’s company. Most sponsor deals are not structured that way. The parts of the price that arrive later — the earn-out, the rolled stake, the incentive units granted at close — are the parts most exposed to the founder ceasing to be an employee.

Take an invented company with no real-world counterpart. $5,000,000 of adjusted EBITDA, sold at 8.0x for a $40,000,000 enterprise value, debt-free and cash-free. The owner takes $31,000,000 of cash at close, rolls $5,000,000 for 25% of the new equity, and has a $4,000,000 earn-out payable on year-two EBITDA of $6,500,000. They are terminated in month twenty, without cause, four months before the earn-out measurement date. Three documents decide what happens next, and they are usually negotiated by three different people on the seller’s side.

In the middle column the owner is out $4,000,000 of earn-out and the entire second bite — a rolled stake that would have been worth roughly $13,000,000 at a year-five exit on plan is bought back at the $5,000,000 that went in. In the right-hand column, the same termination costs a job and nothing else. The difference between those two outcomes was decided at signing, by the definition of “cause” and the leaver provisions, not by anything that happened in month twenty.

What the title you are offered actually means

Sellers are often given a different role rather than a departure: Chairman, President, Founder, a board seat, a consulting agreement. These are not equivalent, and the distinction is about who the role reports to and who can end it.

The one worth fighting for is the board seat, because it is the only one that does not sit inside the employment relationship. A founder who is removed as CEO but keeps a contractual board seat still sees the financials, still gets notice of a sale process, and still votes those shares. A founder whose board seat was tied to a job title loses all three on the same afternoon.

What to settle before you sign

  • The definition of “cause.” Narrow it to things a court would recognise — fraud, a felony, a material uncured breach. A “failure to meet performance expectations” clause converts every leaver provision in the deal into a discretionary one.
  • Good-leaver treatment of the rolled equity. No call at cost. If the company can repurchase, it should be at fair market value set by an independent appraisal, and ideally not at all before an exit.
  • An earn-out that survives your employment. If the earn-out is a piece of the purchase price — and it is — it should not evaporate because the buyer exercised a right it already holds.
  • An employment term at least as long as the earn-out. A three-year earn-out and a one-year employment agreement is a structure, not an oversight.
  • A board seat written into the shareholders’ agreement, held for as long as you hold equity, not for as long as you hold the job.
  • Succession stated out loud. Ask directly whether the plan involves a new CEO, and when. Spencer Stuart’s data says half of all changes were planned at the deal; the sponsor usually knows the answer before you ask.

Two caveats on the research. The Russell Reynolds and Spencer Stuart samples are European, and the Spencer Stuart changes date from 2004–2011; the AlixPartners figure is a survey of what sponsors say they do rather than a count of what happened. They are different methods, different continents and different decades, and they agree, which is the reason to take the direction seriously even if no single number is the number for your deal.

Frequently asked questions

Does private equity always replace the founder CEO?
No. Nearly a third of deals run to exit with the CEO who was there at acquisition, and in Russell Reynolds’ sample those were the shortest holds — 5.5 years on average, against 8.5 years for deals that changed CEO twice. Continuity and a quick exit tend to travel together.

How long do founder CEOs usually last?
Long enough to be past the first year and inside the earn-out. Planned changes cluster in the first six to eighteen months; unplanned ones cluster between years two and four. There is no reliable data on founder CEOs specifically — the published studies measure CEOs, not founders — so treat the two-to-four-year window as the planning assumption rather than a forecast.

Can I negotiate a contractual right to stay CEO?
Not in any form a sponsor will sign. What you can negotiate is the price of removing you: severance, a “cause” definition with teeth, and leaver provisions that leave the earn-out and the rolled equity intact. Make being replaced expensive rather than impossible.

Do I lose my rolled equity if I am fired?
It depends entirely on the leaver provisions in the shareholders’ agreement. A bad-leaver call at original cost takes the whole second bite. Good-leaver treatment at fair value, or no call at all, leaves it with you. This single clause is often worth more than a quarter-turn on the headline multiple, and it gets a fraction of the attention — run the headline number through the business valuation calculator and then ask what share of it is actually contingent on your continued employment.

Is it different if I sell a minority stake?
Somewhat, but less than owners expect. A minority investor who holds board seats and a consent list can still reach the CEO decision; see what a minority PE investor’s veto rights actually cover.

The bottom line

The CEO question is not a people question, it is a consideration question. Two-thirds of sponsors expect to change the CEO, the change arrives in years two to four, and that is the same stretch of calendar in which an earn-out matures and a rolled stake sits with no exit in sight. An owner who treats “we want you to stay” as a commitment rather than an intention is leaving the contingent half of the price in someone else’s discretion.

None of this is an argument against selling to a sponsor — the pros and cons of selling to private equity are real on both sides, and a founder who genuinely wants out should care very little who runs the company afterwards. It matters if you intended to keep running it. In that case the structure deserves as much scrutiny as the price, including how long the sponsor is likely to hold and what happens to your stake over that period.
