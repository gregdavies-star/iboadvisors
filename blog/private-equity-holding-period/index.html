Private Equity Holding Period: 6 Years, Not 3 to 5

By Michael Chasen · Updated November 9, 2026

A private equity firm will tell you it holds a company for three to five years. The data says otherwise. Across 13,757 completed exits from 2021 through mid-2026, the median holding period is 6.0 years, up 1.1 years since 2021 (Private Equity Info). Bain’s 2026 global report puts the average hold at exit at roughly seven years, against five to six for most of the prior decade.

That gap matters to exactly one person at the closing table: the owner who rolls equity. Your cash at close is fixed on the day you sign. The rest of your consideration — the rolled stake, the second bite, whatever the earnout pays — is priced in time, and the clock is not yours.

Why the hold keeps getting longer

None of this is a sponsor being sentimental about your company. It is arithmetic at the fund level. Bain counted roughly 32,000 unsold portfolio companies worth about $3.8 trillion still sitting in funds globally, with distributions to investors below 15% of net asset value for four consecutive years. Exit value did recover — up 47% to $717 billion in 2025 — but buyout deal value rose 44% to $904 billion in the same year, so the inventory kept building even in a good exit market.

When a firm cannot sell what it already owns at the price it underwrote, it waits. Waiting is rational for the sponsor: selling into a soft market crystallises a mediocre multiple, while holding costs it very little, because the management fee keeps running and the portfolio company keeps paying its monitoring fee every quarter. The cost of waiting falls mostly on the people whose money is locked in the asset. One of those people is you.

The fund clock, and why your company is not on it

A buyout fund is a closed-end vehicle with a fixed life. Goodwin’s survey of fund terms puts the typical lifespan at 8 to 12 years, with almost all funds permitting extensions beyond the stated term: most allow two one-year extensions, and in 63% of funds surveyed the general partner can take the first extension at its sole discretion. The second extension typically needs the advisory committee (42%), the third needs investor consent (41%).

Two practical consequences for a seller:

  • Where you sit in the fund’s life decides your timeline more than your performance does. A company bought in year two of a ten-year fund has runway. A company bought in year six, out of the tail of the same fund, is being acquired by a buyer that needs liquidity sooner — or will need an extension to keep you.
  • The hold assumption in the model is not a commitment. Nothing in the purchase agreement obliges a sponsor to sell in year five. The timing right sits with whoever appoints the board majority and holds the drag-along, which after a control sale is the sponsor, not you. That is the governance package, not the price — the same package that carries the consent list and veto rights.

What two extra years do to rollover equity: a worked example

Here is the arithmetic on an invented company — not a quote, a projection, or a description of any transaction.

Take a business with $6 million of normalised EBITDA sold at 7.0x, so $42 million. Following the capital structure in how private equity finances a buyout, the sponsor writes a $15 million equity cheque, the company borrows $22.5 million, and you roll $4.5 million — about 10% of the price — into the new holding company.

The sponsor’s model shows a sale in year five at 2.5x its money. On your rolled $4.5 million that is $11.25 million, an annualised return of 20.1%. It is the number that makes the rollover feel like the smart half of the deal.

Now move the exit to year seven, the industry median, with the same $11.25 million of proceeds.

Exit yearRolled equityProceedsAnnualised return
Year 5 (the model)$4,500,000$11,250,00020.1%
Year 7 (the median)$4,500,000$11,250,00014.0%

Same dollars, six percentage points of annual return gone. Put the other way round: to still earn 20.1% a year over seven years, your second bite has to pay about $16.2 million rather than $11.25 million — the business has to be worth roughly 44% more than the model assumed, not the same. Two years of patience is not neutral; it is a repricing of the only part of the consideration you did not take in cash.

And the delay is not free in the meantime. Those are two further years of interest and amortisation on the acquisition debt, two more years of an escalating monitoring fee, and two more years in which a preferred return accruing to the sponsor’s capital sits ahead of yours in the waterfall. Rollover equity and the second bite covers how that waterfall is built; the point here is that every clause in it is time-weighted and the clock just ran two years longer.

Continuation vehicles: the hold that does not end

There is now a standard answer when a fund runs out of time and the sponsor does not want to sell: move the company into a continuation vehicle, a new fund raised to buy the asset from the old one. GP-led secondaries hit a record $47 billion in the first half of 2025 on Jefferies’ count, and in Dechert’s survey of sponsors, 41% of those planning more GP-led deals named “greater flexibility on how long portfolio companies are held” as a driver. Bain’s figures keep it in proportion — continuation vehicles and secondaries together are still under 10% of total exit value — but the direction is unmistakable, and it is concentrated in exactly the assets that have been held longest.

For a rolled-over owner, a continuation vehicle is a question you probably have no vote on: do you take the cash at the transfer price, or roll again into a new vehicle with new terms, a new fee load and a new clock? Ask, before you sign the first deal, what your rights are if the asset moves to an affiliate of the buyer. The answer lives in the shareholders’ agreement, and it is negotiable while the price is still being discussed.

Five questions to ask before you roll

  • Which fund is buying me, and what vintage is it? Ask for the fund’s first closing date and the end of its investment period. A 2019-vintage fund buying you in 2026 has a different timeline from a fresh one.
  • What hold does your model assume, and what did your last three exits actually take? A sponsor that will not compare its assumption to its own realised record is telling you something.
  • What happens at the end of the fund’s term? Extension, continuation vehicle, or forced sale — and which of those can happen without my consent?
  • Do I have a put, a tag-along, or any liquidity right on a timetable? Most rollover paper has none. If there is none, say so out loud so everyone has heard it.
  • If the hold runs to year eight, what does my rollover return look like in your own model? Make them run the downside case on the page, in dollars.

How the timeline works in an Independent Buyout

An Independent Buyout removes the fund clock from the equation, because there is no fund. The company is bought by a trust, the trust holds control, and the existing management team carries on running the business. There is no investment period ending, no limited partners waiting on distributions, no vintage year pressing for a sale, and no continuation vehicle, because nothing has to be recycled into a new structure to buy more time. The debt amortises on its own schedule and the business is run for the long term rather than for a dated exit.

That does not make an IBO the right answer for everyone — the pros and cons of selling to private equity are real on both sides, and a sponsor sale can pay more in cash at close. It does mean the comparison should be run over the same time horizon. Start with a defensible enterprise value from the business valuation calculator, then ask what each structure pays you, and when.

Frequently asked questions

How long does private equity typically hold a company?
The median across US platform exits from 2021 through mid-2026 is 6.0 years, and 5.9 years for companies exited in 2025. Bain puts the average at exit nearer seven years. The commonly quoted “three to five years” describes the fund model, not the realised data.

Why do private equity firms say three to five years?
Because that is what the underwriting assumes, and the internal rate of return the fund markets to its own investors depends on it. The assumption is sincere; it is simply not what has been happening. Returns tend to flatten the longer an asset stays in a portfolio, which is why sponsors want short holds and keep not getting them.

Can a sponsor hold my company longer than the fund’s life?
Effectively yes. Most fund documents allow two one-year extensions, and when those run out the asset can be sold into a continuation vehicle managed by the same firm. Neither route requires your agreement as a minority rollover holder.

Does a longer hold mean a better price for me in the end?
Not reliably. A longer hold can mean a bigger company, but the same proceeds arriving two years later is a materially lower annualised return, and in the meantime the debt, fees and any preferred return keep accruing ahead of your stake.

Is the holding period negotiable?
The hold itself, rarely. What is negotiable is what happens to you inside it: tag-along and drag-along terms, a put right or redemption at a defined date, information rights, and whether your consent is needed before the asset moves to another vehicle the sponsor controls. Those are cheap to ask for before the letter of intent is signed and nearly impossible to add afterwards. What happens after private equity buys your company walks through the rest of the post-close reality.

The bottom line

The holding period is the least negotiated number in a private equity deal and one of the most consequential. It does not appear in the letter of intent, it is not warranted anywhere in the purchase agreement, and it has moved by more than a year in half a decade — in the direction that costs a rolled-over seller money.

So price your rollover on the median, not on the pitch. If the deal only works for you at a five-year exit, it is a deal that depends on something the sponsor has not promised and the market has not delivered since 2021.
