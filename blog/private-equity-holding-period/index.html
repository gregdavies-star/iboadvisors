Private Equity Holding Period: 6 Years and Climbing

By Michael Chasen · Updated November 7, 2026

A private equity firm that buys your company intends to sell it again. The question every owner should ask before signing is how long that takes, because the answer governs when a rolled stake turns into cash, how long the business carries acquisition debt, and how many years you spend as a minority shareholder in a company you used to own outright.

The current answer is about six years, and it has been getting longer. Private Equity Info’s September 2026 study of 13,757 completed exits between 2021 and mid-2026 puts the median at 6.0 years, up 1.1 years since 2021 — “the typical investment now takes roughly a year longer to exit than it did five years ago”. Bain’s 2026 Global Private Equity Report, which measures the average rather than the median, says “for buyout funds, holding periods at exit are hovering around seven years, up from an average of five to six years from 2010 to 2021”. Both numbers are right; they measure different things. The five-year hold a banker describes in a pitch is neither of them.

If you run a healthcare services business and are being told the sponsor flips platforms in four years, the exits actually completed in your sector say otherwise.

The clock belongs to the fund, not to your company

The hold has a length because the fund that owns you has an end date. A closed-end private equity fund is a limited partnership with a stated term: Goodwin’s 2024 survey of fund documents says “the lifespan of a closed-end fund typically ranges from 8 to 12 years, depending on the asset class”, with two one-year extensions standard and some documents allowing three. The first extension is usually the sponsor’s to take — 63% of the funds surveyed let the general partner approve it at sole discretion. Oversight arrives later and only partially: 42% require the limited partner advisory committee to approve a second extension, and 41% require consent from the investors themselves for a third.

Three consequences follow for a seller, and none of them appear in a letter of intent:

  • Your deal inherits the fund’s age. Capital invested in year one of a fund has a decade of runway. Capital invested in year five of the same fund does not. Ask which fund is buying you and what vintage it is; it is a fair question and the answer is usually known to every limited partner already.
  • The exit date is set by portfolio logic, not by your business. A fund under pressure to show distributions sells what is sellable, which may mean selling you early at a mediocre price, or holding you late because something else sold first.
  • Extensions are routine. “Almost all funds permit extensions beyond the stipulated term,” Goodwin notes. A fund running past its term is not a crisis; it is a Tuesday. It is also two more years in which your rolled equity is worth whatever someone eventually pays for it.

Why holds are stretching

The backlog is the reason. Bain puts the stock of unrealized private equity assets at “roughly 32,000 companies representing a stunning $3.8 trillion in value”, and reports that “almost 40% of all companies are now held for more than five years, up from 29% in 2019”. Exits are happening — global buyout-backed exit value rose 47% to $717 billion in 2025 — but the count of exit transactions slipped 2% to 1,570, so the value came from bigger deals rather than from more of them. Meanwhile distributions as a percentage of net asset value “have now held below 15% for four years running”, and the metric “remained essentially flat at 14%, a level not seen since 2008–09”.

That is a queue, and your company would be joining it. The queue does not shorten because your business performs well. It shortens when financing costs fall and buyers and sellers agree on price.

What three extra years do to rolled equity: a worked example

The company below is invented, and so are its growth rate and exit multiple. The point is the shape of the arithmetic, not a forecast.

A business earns $6 million of EBITDA and sells at 7.0x, a $42 million enterprise value, financed the way most sponsor deals are financed: $22.5 million of debt on the company, $15 million of sponsor equity, and $4.5 million of seller rollover equity. The rollover is 23.1% of the $19.5 million equity. Assume the senior facility amortizes $1.8 million a year and the exit happens at the same 7.0x.

Read the two pairs separately, because they fail differently.

In the first pair the business does everything it was supposed to do and the exit simply slips. The owner ends up with $3.2 million more cash — and a lower annual return, because the same compounding is spread over three more years. That is the benign version, and it is still a worse deal than the one that was described.

The second pair is the one worth dwelling on, because holds usually stretch for a reason, and the reason is usually that the asset is not ready to sell. With flat EBITDA, the extra three years add $1,246,154 to the rolled stake, and nearly all of it is debt repayment rather than value creation — the enterprise value never moves. The owner’s eight-year compound return on the rolled $4.5 million is 7.2%. Cash taken at closing and left in a liquid portfolio earning 7% would have reached $7,731,838 over the same eight years, against $7,823,077 for the rollover: effectively the same money, for eight years of illiquidity in a single undiversified position you do not control. That comparison, not the headline multiple, is the real test of a rollover.

Run your own version before anyone drafts a term sheet. The business valuation calculator gives you the entry enterprise value; the rest is arithmetic, and the only variable that matters is how many years you are prepared to wait.

When the fund runs out of road

If a fund reaches the end of its extensions with your company still in it, there are three outcomes, and none of them is the clean sale that was described at closing.

  • A secondary buyout. The company is sold to another private equity firm. The debt is refinanced, a new sponsor’s equity comes in, and the holding period starts over. For a rolled stake this is often the moment the money finally arrives — or the moment you are asked to roll again.
  • A continuation vehicle. The sponsor sells the asset to a new fund it also manages, bringing in secondary buyers to cash out the original investors. This is now a mainstream outcome rather than a curiosity: Jefferies reports that GP-led secondary volume reached $115 billion in 2025, 48% of a $240 billion secondary market, with the average continuation vehicle at about $900 million and nearly 80% of the hundred largest sponsors having done one.
  • A longer hold. The fund extends, the company carries its leverage for another few years, and the monitoring fee keeps running alongside it.

Continuation vehicles deserve a word of caution for a minority holder. The buyer and the seller are the same firm, which means the price is set in a transaction where your counterparty sits on both sides of the table. Whether you can take cash at that point, and at what valuation, is a function of the shareholders’ agreement you signed years earlier — which is why tag-along rights and the drag threshold are worth more attention than they usually get.

What to settle before you sign

  • Ask the vintage. Which fund is buying, what year did it start investing, and when does its term end? A late-vintage fund is a short clock.
  • Ask for the stated target hold, in writing, next to the extension terms. The target is not binding, but a sponsor unwilling to state one in a letter of intent has told you something.
  • Price the rollover against time, not against the multiple. The number that matters is the annual return at year eight, not the multiple at year five.
  • Negotiate a put, a tag-along, or a redemption right. These are the only mechanisms that give a minority holder a route to cash on a timetable that is not entirely the sponsor’s.
  • Read the continuation-vehicle language. Ask explicitly what happens to your stake if the sponsor sells the company to another fund it manages.
  • Size the cash at closing accordingly. If an eight-year wait would be intolerable, the fix is at the front of the deal: take more cash and roll less.

Frequently asked questions

How long does private equity typically hold a company?
About six years on the current data — a 6.0-year median across 13,757 exits completed between 2021 and mid-2026, and an average at exit of around seven years on Bain’s measure. Five years was closer to the truth a decade ago.

Is the holding period written into the deal documents?
Almost never as an obligation. What is written down is the fund’s term and extension mechanics, and whatever exit rights the shareholders’ agreement gives a minority holder — a put, a drag, a tag, a stated target hold. Those are the enforceable parts.

Does a longer hold mean I make more money on my rollover?
More dollars, usually; a lower annual return, almost always. And if the hold stretched because the business stalled, the extra dollars are mostly debt paydown, which you would have got from simply holding the company yourself.

Can I sell my rolled stake early?
Only if you negotiated the right to. Rolled equity in a private holding company has no market, and transfer restrictions in the shareholders’ agreement are usually drafted to prevent exactly that.

Do minority recapitalizations have shorter holds?
Not reliably. A minority recapitalization involves a fund with the same term and the same investors waiting for distributions. The size of the stake changes the governance, not the clock.

The bottom line

The holding period is the single assumption that a seller is most often given and least often shown. It sets when a rolled stake converts to cash, how long the company carries its acquisition debt, and how many years pass before anyone asks you what you think again. The honest current number is six years and rising, with four in ten sponsor-owned companies already past five, and a $3.8 trillion queue in front of the exit. A sponsor who tells you four or five is describing an intention, not a distribution.

That does not make a sponsor deal wrong. It makes the length of the hold a term to negotiate rather than a detail to accept — and it makes the cash-versus-rollover split the most consequential number in the letter of intent. Owners working through that trade-off usually start with the pros and cons of selling to private equity and what actually happens after private equity buys your company.
