Private Equity Holding Period: Median 6 Years and Rising

By Michael Chasen · Updated November 6, 2026

The median private equity holding period is 6.0 years. That figure comes from Private Equity Info’s September 2026 study of 13,757 completed exits between 2021 and mid-2026, and it is up 1.1 years since 2021. Bain measures the same drift a different way — the average hold of a buyout asset at the moment it is sold — and reports in its 2026 Global Private Equity Report that holding periods at exit are “hovering around seven years, up from an average of five to six years from 2010 to 2021.”

If you are selling to a sponsor and rolling part of your proceeds into the new company, that is not trivia about an asset class. It is the maturity date on the second half of your consideration. The rolled stake pays when the sponsor sells, and the sponsor sells when the fund needs it to.

Why the two numbers differ, and why both understate the wait

A median is the middle of a list; an average is pulled upward by a handful of very long holds. Six and seven years are the same story told with different statistics.

Both share a bigger limitation: they are computed from companies that have already been sold. The ones still sitting in portfolios are not in the sample, and there are a great many of those. PitchBook counted 33,575 private-equity-owned companies awaiting exit as of 30 June 2026, against 15,923 a decade earlier, and separately put the US count at 13,509 at the end of the second quarter, estimating that clearing that US inventory at the current pace of exits would take roughly eleven years. Bain puts the global stock at about 32,000 companies holding $3.8 trillion of unrealised value, and notes that almost 40% of all portfolio companies are now held for more than five years, against 29% in 2019.

So the honest answer to “how long will they own my company” is: six years is the middle of what has been sold, seven is the average at exit, and the queue behind those exits is longer than it has ever been.

The sectors that used to turn over fastest have moved the most. Technology, business services and financial services all sat around 4.5 years in 2021 and now sit between 5.8 and 6.0. Healthcare services, where roll-ups were being flipped in well under five years, reached 6.6 years in 2025. Consumer and retail is the slowest line in the table at 6.9 years — the bucket most multi-unit restaurant platforms are counted in. If your industry is not on this list, assume the trend rather than the exception: the pressure behind it is market-wide, not sector-specific.

Why the hold keeps stretching

It is not that sponsors have changed their minds about selling. It is that selling has become the hard part.

  • The number of exits is flat while the number of owned companies is not. Bain counts 1,570 buyout-backed exits globally in 2025, a 2% fall year over year and roughly the pre-pandemic level, even though exit value jumped 47% to $717 billion. Large assets cleared; the count did not move.
  • Limited partners are not getting their money back. Bain’s 2026 midyear report describes distributions running at record lows as a share of net asset value for four straight years, with “little signs of progress towards easing the industry’s exit logjam.”
  • Nobody wants to print the loss. The same report carries an MSCI analysis finding that roughly 75% of buyout assets still exit above their next-to-last quarterly mark. Assets that would sell below their carrying value tend not to be sold at all, which is precisely the kind of company that ends up held for nine years.
  • Entry price has to be grown out of. A company bought at a 2021 multiple needs either EBITDA growth or a multiple recovery before the equity clears the fund’s preferred return. Waiting is cheaper than selling into a worse market — for the sponsor. The company carries the acquisition debt for every extra year of the wait.

Underneath all of it sits the fund itself. Closed-end funds are raised with a stated term — Goodwin, drawing on its terms database for private investment funds, puts the lifespan at “8 to 12 years, depending on the asset class”, with documents that “typically provide for two one-year extensions” and some that allow three. Extensions are close to universal: the same note says that to deal with illiquid assets, unresolved claims and market downturns, “almost all funds permit extensions beyond the stipulated term”.

Who gets to take them is the part worth knowing. On Goodwin’s figures, “63% of funds surveyed allow GPs to authorize the first extension at its sole discretion, typically for one year”. Investor oversight exists, but it arrives later in the sequence: approval from the limited partner advisory committee is the common requirement for a second extension, and consent from the fund’s own investors for a third. So the first year past the term is, more often than not, the sponsor’s to take alone — and your company was probably not bought in year one of the fund. The hold quoted at the letter of intent is the sponsor’s plan; the fund’s remaining life is the constraint that plan has to fit inside, and nobody shows you that document.

What a longer hold does to the equity you roll

Take an invented company with no real-world counterpart: $5,000,000 of adjusted EBITDA, sold at 8.0x for a $40,000,000 enterprise value, debt-free and cash-free. The sponsor funds it with $20,000,000 of debt and $20,000,000 of equity. The owner takes $35,000,000 in cash and rolls $5,000,000, which buys 25% of the new equity. The plan is to grow EBITDA to $8,000,000, exit at the same 8.0x, and amortise $2,000,000 of debt a year.

Read the first two rows together. Four extra years of waiting raise the cheque by $2,000,000 — and cut the annual return almost in half, because the extra years bought mostly debt paydown rather than growth. Put the other way: $13,000,000 received in year four only has to compound at 3.6% a year to beat $15,000,000 received in year eight. A Treasury bill clears that. The longer hold is not a bonus; it is a loan you made to the sponsor at a rate you did not agree to.

The third row is the one to think hardest about. Nothing catastrophic happens in it: EBITDA simply does not grow and the sector reprices by one turn, over a period long enough for both to be unremarkable. The same rolled $5,000,000 returns 5.6% a year. Length did not cause that, but it is what gives slippage the time to happen, and a rolled stake has no maturity date to protect it. What sponsors do when the hold runs long

Four things, mostly, and each one changes what your stake is worth.

They sell the company to themselves. A continuation vehicle moves the asset out of the ageing fund and into a new one run by the same firm, funded by secondary buyers. Lazard’s interim 2026 secondary market report puts first-half volume at about $124 billion, a record, with GP-led deals — mostly continuation funds — at roughly $61 billion, also a record, and issuance spreading from technology into healthcare, industrials and services. For a rolled stake this is the single most important clause nobody negotiates: in an affiliate transaction, are you paid out in cash at the same price the secondary buyers paid, or is your equity simply rolled again into the new vehicle for another five years? Ask before you sign, not when the notice arrives.

They sell to another sponsor. A secondary buyout refinances the debt, resets the clock for the new owner, and is frequently the event that finally converts a founder’s rolled stake to cash.

They take cash out of the company instead. A dividend recapitalisation borrows against the business and distributes the proceeds pro rata, which does return some money to you, but it loads debt onto the balance sheet your remaining equity sits behind, and it reduces the pressure to sell at all.

They keep buying. Add-on acquisitions need fresh equity. If you do not fund your share, your percentage falls; if you do, you are putting new money into a hold whose end date keeps moving. And the governance and operating changes covered in what happens after private equity buys your company apply for all seven years, not the first two.

What to settle before you sign

  • A stated target hold, with a consequence. A target in a slide deck is marketing. A put right exercisable at a defined date, priced off a defined formula, is a term.
  • Continuation-vehicle treatment. Your right to take cash at the transaction price in any sale to an affiliate of the sponsor, on the same terms as third-party sellers.
  • Where you sit in the stack. If the sponsor’s money is preferred and accretes at, say, 8% compounding, time is not neutral: $15,000,000 of preferred becomes $20,400,000 by year four and $25,700,000 by year seven. That $5,300,000 difference comes out of the common before you see anything.
  • Information rights for the life of the stake. Quarterly financials and notice of a sale process, in writing, for as long as you hold equity.
  • The fees that run with the hold. Every extra year is another year of monitoring fees charged to the company, and a share of them lands on your rolled percentage.
  • What the exit definition actually covers. Drag-along rights, tag-along rights and the consent list a minority investor negotiates decide who can start the clock and who merely watches it.

Frequently asked questions

How long does private equity hold a company?
Six years at the median across completed exits since 2021, around seven on Bain’s average-at-exit measure, and lengthening on both. Plan on the high end: the companies still unsold are not in either statistic.

Can I make the sponsor sell?
No. What you can negotiate is a put right at a defined date and price, which is a different thing: it gives you an exit from the stake rather than an exit for the company.

Does a longer hold mean a bigger second bite?
Often a bigger number and a worse return, as the table above shows. It is only unambiguously better if EBITDA keeps compounding through the extra years.

Is the hold shorter in a minority deal?
Not reliably. A minority recapitalisation is funded out of the same fund with the same life, so the same clock applies — which is why minority investors negotiate puts and target hold periods for themselves.

The bottom line

A holding period is the one deal term that is never written into the purchase agreement and always decides what the deal pays. Six to seven years is the current answer, it has moved by more than a year since 2021, and the 33,575 companies waiting in line say it has not finished moving. Every projection a sponsor shows you for your rolled equity is an annualised return, and an annualised return is a number divided by a hold length that the sponsor controls and you do not.

That is an argument for taking more of the consideration in cash at close, pricing the rolled portion as the undated instrument it is, and negotiating the exit mechanics with the attention most owners spend on the headline multiple.

It is also the reason an Independent Buyout is built the way it is. There is no fund behind it, so there is no fund life, no limited partners waiting on distributions, and no future sale that someone else schedules: the company is bought by a trust, the trust holds control, and the existing team carries on running the business. Owners usually arrive at it after reading the pros and cons of selling to private equity and realising the clock is the part they object to.
