Private Equity Holding Period: 6 Years and Rising

By Michael Chasen · Updated November 8, 2026

A private equity firm now holds the average company it buys for about six years. Private Equity Info, which tracks completed sponsor exits, puts the typical hold at 6.0 years across 13,757 exits between 2021 and mid-2026, and reports that the median has risen 1.1 years over that stretch. That is the number to plan against — not the three-to-five years a sponsor will quote you in a first meeting.

The gap between those two numbers matters to one person above all: the owner who rolls part of the price into the new company instead of taking all of it in cash. That slice is illiquid for the whole hold, and the length of the hold is decided by the sponsor, not by you.

How long private equity actually holds a company

The same firm’s longer series, built on US platform exits, shows where the shift happened. From 2015 through 2022 the median held “remarkably steady, ranging narrowly between 4.7 and 5.0 years”. Then it moved, three years in a row.

The cause is not mysterious. Sponsors who bought at 2021 prices cannot sell at 2021 prices, so they wait: “sponsors who bought during 2021 peak multiples are now the primary contributors to the elongation”. An owner selling today is selling into a market where the buyer’s last fund is already behind schedule.

Where the clock comes from, and why it is not about your company

A holding period is not a judgment about your business. It is a residual of the fund that bought it.

Buyout capital is raised into closed-end funds with a fixed life. Goodwin’s survey of fund terms finds that “most private equity and venture capital funds — 56% and 72%, respectively — have a 10-year term”, and that all funds “typically include extension provisions” to wind down in an orderly way. Those extensions are short and they are not automatic: fund documents usually allow two one-year extensions, with 63% of surveyed funds letting the manager authorise the first at its sole discretion, 42% requiring advisory-committee approval for the second, and 41% requiring investor consent for a third. Beyond that, extending means amending the fund documents with the consent of 50% to 75% of investors by commitment.

Now put your company inside that. The fund’s clock started at its first closing, not at yours. A fund typically invests over roughly its first five years, so a company bought early in the investment period has most of a decade of runway, while one bought late in it may have four or five years before the fund is trying to wind up. Two owners selling to the same firm in the same year can land in funds at opposite ends of their lives.

Which is why the useful question in a first meeting is not “what is your hold period?” It is: which fund is buying us, what vintage is it, how much of its capital is already deployed, and when does its stated term end? Those are facts, and they predict the timing of your second payday far better than a sponsor’s stated philosophy does.

What an extra three years does to the money

The company below is invented. The arithmetic is the arithmetic.

A business earns $6 million of EBITDA and sells at 7.0x, a $42 million enterprise value. The stack is the conventional lower-middle-market one covered in how private equity finances a buyout: $18 million of senior debt, $4.5 million of mezzanine, $15 million of sponsor equity and $4.5 million the owner rolls into the new holding company. The owner therefore owns 23.1% of the equity above $22.5 million of debt.

By exit the business is doing $9 million of EBITDA, sells at 7.5x for $67.5 million, and debt has been paid down to $12 million. Equity value is $55.5 million — a 2.85x return on the $19.5 million that went in. The rolled stake is worth $12.8 million. That figure does not change with the length of the hold. What changes is the annual rate of return, and that is the sponsor’s scorecard.

Read the last column as a statement about the sponsor’s behaviour, not about your company. Carried interest is normally earned only above a preferred return to the fund’s investors, so the gap between the top row and the bottom row is the gap between a deal that pays the deal team well and one that may not pay them at all. A sponsor approaching year six is a motivated seller, and motivated sellers take the bid in front of them.

For the rolling owner the table says something less pleasant. The multiple is the same, the dollars are the same, and the money is simply absent for three more years — unpaid, undiversified, and not yours to liquidate. It is also three more years of the fees the company pays the sponsor under a monitoring agreement, which do not stop because the exit slipped. Before weighing any of this against a price, get your entry value roughly right with the business valuation calculator.

What happens when the fund runs out of time

The backlog is real and it is large. Preqin put the global buyout “exit overhang” — portfolio companies held five years or more — at $989bn as of early 2026. Sponsors sitting on companies they cannot sell at the price they need have four moves, and three of them extend your wait.

  • Sell to another sponsor. The company changes hands, a new fund’s clock starts, and new debt is usually layered on at the same time. Your rolled stake is normally cashed out here, but on the new buyer’s price, not the one you were shown at your own closing.
  • Recapitalise instead of selling. A dividend recapitalization borrows fresh debt to pay the shareholders a distribution, which returns cash to the fund without an exit. You may receive a cheque in proportion to your stake; the company receives more leverage.
  • Move the company into a continuation fund. The sponsor sells the asset to a vehicle it also manages, funded by secondary buyers. This has gone from workaround to institution: Preqin counts continuation funds closing globally rising from 13 in 2018 to 123 in 2025, and capital raised from $9.0bn to $75.0bn — “12.4% of direct private equity fundraising in 2025, up from 1.2% in 2018”. For a rollover holder the question is whether the deal offers a cash election at the price the incoming investors are paying, or simply rolls you again.
  • Hold on and run the extensions. The cheapest option for the sponsor and the slowest for you.

None of those four is a decision you make. Which brings us to the documents.

What in the paperwork decides when you get paid

Nothing in a purchase agreement obliges a sponsor to sell by a given date, or to buy your rolled stake back. Timing lives in the shareholders’ agreement, and for a minority holder it reads in one direction:

  • Drag-along. The majority holder can require you to sell into its chosen transaction, on its terms and its timetable.
  • No put right, unless you negotiate one. There is usually no mechanism by which you can oblige anyone to buy your shares, at any price, ever.
  • Transfer restrictions. Rights of first refusal and consent requirements make a private sale of your stake difficult in practice even where it is permitted on paper.
  • Reserved matters. A sale, a recapitalisation and a continuation-fund transaction all sit on the investor’s consent list rather than yours — the same list that governs board composition and veto rights.

Three terms are worth negotiating capital on if you are rolling meaningful money: a liquidity window or put right at a defined valuation formula after a stated number of years; tag-along rights with no carve-outs, so you exit alongside the sponsor in any sale, including a continuation-fund transfer; and a cash election wherever the sponsor rolls the asset into a vehicle it also manages. All three are easier to ask for while the price is still being discussed.

Frequently asked questions

Is the holding period the same as the fund’s life?
No. The fund’s term is fixed at its first closing; your holding period starts whenever your deal closes. A company acquired in year four of a ten-year fund has a structurally shorter runway than one acquired in year one of the same fund.

Do longer holds mean a better price for me?
Not by themselves. The exit price depends on earnings and the multiple at exit. A long hold usually signals that the sponsor could not get the price it wanted earlier, which is the opposite of a good sign.

Can I sell my rolled shares before the sponsor exits?
Rarely. Transfer restrictions, rights of first refusal and the absence of a buyer for a minority stake in a private, sponsor-backed company make it impractical. Assume the money is locked until the sponsor moves.

Does a shorter hold exist anywhere?
Technology and financial services exits run fastest in the data above, at a 5.8-year median in 2026 to date — still above every annual median recorded between 2015 and 2022.

What should I ask the sponsor before signing?
Which fund is buying, its vintage year, the share of committed capital already deployed, the end of its stated term, and how many of its companies are past five years. Every one is a fact the firm knows.

The bottom line

Six years is the planning assumption. Four to five is the sales pitch. The difference between them is three years in which your rolled equity earns nothing you can spend, and the decision to end that wait belongs to a fund manager whose own deadline is set by a document you will never read.

That is a reason to think hard about how much you roll, not a reason to avoid a sponsor deal — the trade-offs are set out in the pros and cons of selling to private equity, and the operational half of the hold in what happens after private equity buys your company. If you need most of your money now rather than at the end of somebody’s fund life, the cash component at closing is the term that matters most.

There is also a structure with no fund behind it and therefore no clock. In an Independent Buyout the company is bought by a trust rather than a financial sponsor: there is no ten-year term, no investment period, no limited partners waiting on distributions and no forced sale at the end of one — the trust holds control and the existing team carries on running the business. Owners usually arrive at it after comparing it with the routes in every exit option available to a private company.
