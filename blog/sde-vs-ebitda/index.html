SDE vs EBITDA: Which One Buyers Actually Use

By Michael Chasen · Updated October 30, 2026

Seller’s discretionary earnings adds the owner’s entire pay package back to profit. Adjusted EBITDA adds back only the part of that pay above what a hired manager would cost. The gap between the two numbers is exactly one salary. The gap between the two valuations they produce is usually seven figures, because the multiple changes along with the metric — and in the opposite direction.

Which one applies to you is not a matter of preference, and it is not something an advisor gets to choose to flatter the number. It is decided by who can realistically buy the business, and that is mostly decided by size.

The difference in one line

Both measures start in the same place: operating profit with depreciation and amortization added back. Then they part company over one question — does the buyer intend to do the owner’s job?

  • Seller’s discretionary earnings (SDE) = EBITDA + the owner’s total compensation + personal and discretionary costs run through the company. It answers: what will this business put in the pocket of one working owner?
  • Adjusted EBITDA = EBITDA + personal and discretionary costs + only the amount of owner compensation above a market-rate replacement. It answers: what will this business earn for an owner who does not work here?

SDE is therefore always the larger of the two, and always larger by the same thing: the cost of the manager who would do your job. An individual buyer who is about to move into your office is right to ignore that cost, because they are the one absorbing it. A private equity fund, a family office or a strategic buyer cannot ignore it, because they have to hire for it in the first ninety days.

A worked example: one company, two numbers

Take an invented commercial landscaping company — the figures below are illustrative, not drawn from any transaction. Revenue of $6,400,000, reported operating profit of $740,000, depreciation and amortization of $260,000. The owner pays herself $450,000 and runs $85,000 of genuinely personal costs through the business. A general manager hired to run it at the same standard would cost $180,000 all-in.

Line SDE Adjusted EBITDA
Reported EBITDA (operating profit + D&A) $1,000,000 $1,000,000
Personal and discretionary costs + $85,000 + $85,000
Owner compensation + $450,000 (all of it) + $270,000 (the excess over $180,000)
Earnings figure $1,535,000 $1,355,000

The two numbers are $180,000 apart, and that $180,000 is not a judgment call or an accounting style. It is the general manager’s salary, a real cost somebody pays every year in every scenario except the one where the buyer does the job themselves.

Why the bigger number is worth less

Now attach the multiples each market actually uses. A Main Street broker quoting 3x SDE prices this company at $4,605,000. An M&A advisor applying 5.8x adjusted EBITDA — the midpoint we publish for home and facility services in EBITDA multiples by industry — prices the same company at $7,859,000. Same business, same year, same books, $3,254,000 apart.

That is the part owners get wrong. The metric that produces the bigger earnings number is attached to the smaller multiple, because it describes a smaller, more financing-constrained buyer pool. Chasing the headline number is how sellers end up in the wrong process.

The reverse error is more expensive and more common in the other direction: applying a lower-middle-market multiple to an SDE figure. Do that here and you get $8,903,000 — $1,044,000 of enterprise value created by capitalizing a salary the buyer has to pay every year for as long as they own the company. No institutional buyer pays it, and every one of them spots it on the first read of the memorandum. How PE firms value a company walks through the rest of that first read.

Where the line between the two markets actually falls

The IBBA and M&A Source Market Pulse survey, which is the closest thing this market has to a census, splits its own reporting at a hard boundary: Main Street is companies worth $0–$2M and the lower middle market is $2M–$50M. That split is the practical answer to which metric you are in.

Three things sit behind it:

  • The buyer is an individual, and individuals borrow from the SBA. The maximum 7(a) loan amount is $5 million, and the current lender rules — SOP 50 10, Version 8.1, effective October 1, 2026 — govern what that buyer can do. Above roughly $5M of purchase price, the individual buyer financed by a single guaranteed loan runs out of road, and the pool changes.
  • The SDE market is genuinely small. In BizBuySell’s third-quarter 2026 report, 2,132 businesses changed hands at a median sale price of $350,000. That is the center of gravity of the market where SDE is the standard language.
  • Multiples above the line are quoted on EBITDA, and they are higher. Market Pulse advisors put the $5M–$50M band at 5.8x in the second quarter of 2026, up from 5.5x and the highest since the first quarter of 2022. GF Data’s contributed set, covering deals from $10M of enterprise value upward, averaged 7.0x EBITDA in 2Q26, down from 7.3x in 1Q26.

If your company does $3M+ of EBITDA, you are not near this line — you are several times past it, and nobody who matters to your outcome will price you on SDE. The place the confusion does real damage is the $2M–$5M band of enterprise value, where both kinds of advisor will take the engagement and the two of them will quote you numbers that differ by half. How to choose an M&A advisor is largely a question about which of those two businesses the firm in front of you is actually in.

The replacement salary is the whole argument

Once you are in the EBITDA world, the owner-compensation line is the single adjustment most worth preparing, because it is the one with no documentary trail behind it by default. You are asserting what a stranger would have to be paid to do what you do, and a diligence team will test it.

What survives that test is a number with provenance. Write the job description you are actually replacing — hours, travel, the three customer relationships that are personal, the quoting or estimating you do yourself. Price it against published compensation data or a third-party study rather than against instinct. If the role genuinely splits into two hires, say so and price both; a $180,000 general manager and a $95,000 estimator is a defensible answer, and it is a better answer than one implausibly cheap number.

The federal tax code has used the same standard for a century: a business may deduct “a reasonable allowance for salaries or other compensation for personal services actually rendered” under 26 U.S.C. § 162(a)(1). Reasonable compensation is an argument you may already have had with your own accountant about S-corporation distributions, and the file from that argument is useful here.

Two traps are worth naming. First, the owner who has been paying themselves far below market to make the P&L look strong: that is not free EBITDA, and a buyer will reduce earnings by the shortfall rather than let it through. Second, family members on the payroll and related-party rent. Both get normalized to market in either direction, and a building leased from the owner’s LLC at half the market rate is an earnings overstatement a lender will find before the buyer does.

“EBITDA” is not a defined term either

Neither metric is a GAAP measure, and that is not a technicality. The SEC’s non-GAAP interpretations say that measures calculated differently from EBIT and EBITDA “should not be characterized as ‘EBIT’ or ‘EBITDA,’ and their titles should be distinguished”, such as “Adjusted EBITDA.” The same guidance treats a measure that “excludes normal, recurring, cash operating expenses” as potentially misleading.

So the fight is never really SDE versus EBITDA as categories. It is line by line, and the lines are the same ones either way — which costs are the owner’s and which are the company’s. Adjusted EBITDA add-backs covers which of those survive diligence and what documentation each one needs.

How to tell which number you are being quoted

Four questions settle it in a single phone call:

  • Does your earnings figure include my salary or exclude it? If it includes the whole salary, you are holding an SDE number no matter what the document calls it.
  • What replacement compensation did you assume, and where did it come from? “We used the owner’s W-2” is not an answer. Neither is silence.
  • Which comparable set does the multiple come from? A multiple drawn from sub-$2M Main Street transactions does not travel up, and one drawn from $10M+ sponsor deals does not travel down.
  • Who finances a buyer at this price? If the answer is an SBA loan and the price is above $5M, the arithmetic does not close.

For a starting range you can test the quote against, the business valuation calculator runs the same adjusted-EBITDA logic across nineteen industry bands. The leverage side — how much debt the EBITDA figure actually supports once a buyer underwrites it — is in how private equity actually finances a buyout.

Frequently asked questions

Is SDE the same as cash flow? No, though listing sites use “cash flow” as a synonym for SDE and this causes a lot of confusion. Neither SDE nor EBITDA is cash flow in any real sense: both ignore working capital, capital expenditure, debt service and taxes. A company can grow its EBITDA and consume cash every year doing it.

Which number is higher? SDE, always, by the cost of a replacement manager. If someone shows you an SDE figure that is lower than adjusted EBITDA, one of the two has been built wrong.

Can the same business be priced both ways? In the $2M–$5M band of enterprise value, yes, and both quotes can be defensible. What is not defensible is mixing them — an SDE earnings figure with a lower-middle-market multiple, which is the single most common inflated valuation an owner is shown.

At $3M of EBITDA, should I care about SDE at all? Only to recognize it when you see it. If a valuation you have been handed adds your full compensation back, the firm that prepared it is working in a different market from the buyers you want, and the multiple attached to it will be wrong too.

The bottom line

SDE and adjusted EBITDA are not two opinions about the same company. They are two different questions, asked by two different kinds of buyer, answered with two different multiples. The number that matters to you is the one your actual buyer pool uses, and at $3M+ of EBITDA that is never SDE.

The same distinction decides what an Independent Buyout can pay. The financing is sized off the company’s own adjusted EBITDA after a market-rate management team is costed in — because in an IBO that team stays and keeps running the business, the equity moves to a trust owned by the employees, and there is no sponsor and no exit clock attached to the money.
